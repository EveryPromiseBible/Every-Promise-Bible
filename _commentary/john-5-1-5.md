John sets the scene carefully, and everything in the setting is against the man.

## Where it is

*Over by **the Sheep Gate**, there is a pool… called **Bethesda**, and it has **five
covered walkways**.*

The Sheep Gate is where the animals came in for sacrifice.

**Bethesda** — house of mercy, or house of outpouring.

Archaeologists uncovered a twin pool complex near St Anne's church in Jerusalem in
the nineteenth century, with colonnades on four sides and one across the middle
dividing it. Five porches, exactly as John describes.

For a long time this verse was cited as evidence that John did not know Jerusalem.

## Who was there

*A great crowd of people who could not get well — **the blind, the lame, the ones
whose bodies had wasted away** — and they were **waiting for the water to move**.*

Three categories, and a crowd of them lying under the roofs.

## The waiting

*From time to time an angel would come down… and **the first one into the water** was
made well.*

Whatever the tradition was, it had a cruel shape to it. Only the first got anything,
which meant the sickest never could.

The people least able to move were competing to move fastest.

## The man

*One man had been lying there a long while. He had been **an invalid for thirty-eight
years**.*

John gives the number, and it is a number with a history — Israel wandered thirty-eight
years between Kadesh Barnea and crossing the brook Zered.

## The question

The verses following: *"**Do you want to be made well?**"*

He does not answer it. He explains the system instead: *"Sir, I have no man to put me
into the pool when the water is stirred, but while I am coming, another steps down
before me."*

Thirty-eight years of arriving second.

## What Jesus does

*"**Rise, take up your bed and walk.**"*

No water, no stirring, no angel, and no race.

*And immediately the man was made well, took up his bed, and walked.*

## The one He picked

Out of a great crowd of blind, lame and paralysed people, John records one healing
that day.

And Jesus *had withdrawn, a multitude being in that place* — He leaves without a
scene.

## What follows

It was the Sabbath, and the objection is about a man carrying a mat.

Then, later, in the temple: *"See, you have been made well. **Sin no more, lest a
worse thing come upon you.**"*

And the man goes and tells the authorities who it was.

> **The other question in the chapter.** *"Do you want to be made well?"* — asked of a
> man who had been lying there for thirty-eight years. Nothing in the account
> suggests it was rhetorical.
