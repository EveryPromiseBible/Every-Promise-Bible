The peace offering has a shelf life, and it depends on why it was brought.

## The three occasions

Leviticus distinguishes them:

- **thanksgiving** — brought because of something received
- **a vow** — brought to fulfil a promise
- **a freewill gift** — brought for no reason at all

## The two time limits

*The meat of **the thanksgiving** peace offering must be eaten **the same day**;
**none may be left until morning**.*

*If the offering is made because of **a vow or as a freewill gift**, it may be eaten
on the day it is offered **and on the next day**.*

Thanksgiving gets one day. The others get two.

The most immediate of the three has the shortest window.

## The third day

*Whatever is left over on the third day **must be burned**. If any meat is eaten on
the third day, **it will not be accepted; it counts for nothing** to the one who
brought it.*

The Hebrew is **piggul** — foul, refuse, an offensive thing. Strong language for
meat that is two days old.

And the effect is retroactive: the offering counts for nothing. It does not become a
partial credit.

## Why the limit

Nothing in the passage is about hygiene — the vow offering keeps a day longer under
the same sun.

The distinction is in the reason for bringing it, and thanksgiving is the one that
cannot be carried over.

Yesterday's gratitude does not do for today. It has to be brought fresh.

## The bread that goes with it

*Loaves of bread **without yeast** mixed with oil, thin wafers spread with oil, and
loaves of fine flour well-mixed with oil.*

Three kinds, all unleavened, all with oil.

And then, unexpectedly:

*Along with these, present **loaves of bread made with yeast** as your peace offering
of thanks.*

Leaven is excluded from almost every offering in Leviticus — *no grain offering which
you bring to the LORD shall be made with leaven.* It is the standing symbol of what
corrupts.

In the peace offering it is commanded. Bread as people actually bake it, brought to
the same table.

## Who eats what

*One of each kind is presented as a contribution to the LORD; **it belongs to the
priest who sprinkles the blood**.*

The priest who handled the blood gets the bread. See the note on Leviticus 7:28–34.

## What the offering is

**Shelamim** — from **shalom**. The one sacrifice in the system the worshipper sat
down and ate.

It was not brought to establish peace. It was eaten by somebody who already had it.

> **What Paul says about the leaven.** *Purge out the old leaven… **for indeed Christ,
> our Passover, was sacrificed for us.*** He uses it the usual way. And yet Leviticus
> puts a leavened loaf on the thanksgiving table — real bread, from a real kitchen,
> alongside the unleavened.
