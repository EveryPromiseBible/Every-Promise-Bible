Nehemiah comes back from a trip to Persia and finds the temple system quietly
collapsed. What he notices first is where people are.

## The detail that tells the story

*The Levites and singers who did the work had gone back, each to his own field.*

Nobody rebelled. Nobody made a decision. The support stopped arriving, and men
with families went home to grow food, because that is what a person does.

Nehemiah's question is aimed at the officials, not at the Levites: *why is the
house of God neglected?* He puts the failure on the people responsible for the
arrangement, not on the men who left.

## What he actually fixes

The interesting part is the administration. He does not merely restart the
collection — he appoints four named men over the storerooms, *for they were
counted faithful*, whose job is to distribute fairly among their kinsmen.

The previous arrangement had been compromised: a few verses earlier, a priest had
given a storeroom over to an official's private use. The problem was not only
supply. It was that the system had stopped being trustworthy.

Nehemiah's answer to a giving problem is accountable handling of what is given.

> **The prayer at the end.** *Remember me for this, O my God, and do not wipe
> away the good I have done.* Nehemiah prays this four or five times in his book,
> always about specific administrative work — walls, gates, storerooms, rosters.
> It is the closing note of a man who did unglamorous jobs and wanted them
> noticed by the only person whose notice he cared about.
