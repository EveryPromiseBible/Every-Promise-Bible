The sentence that comes out of a public argument in a dining room.

## What has just happened

Peter came to Antioch and ate with Gentile believers. Then men came from James, and *he withdrew
and separated himself, fearing those who were of the circumcision*.

*And the rest of the Jews also played the hypocrite with him, **so that even Barnabas was carried
away**.*

*I said to Peter before them all, "**If you, being a Jew, live in the manner of Gentiles… why do
you compel Gentiles to live as Jews?**"* See the note on Galatians 2:11–14.

This paragraph is what Paul said next, and it is not clear where the speech to Peter ends and the
letter resumes.

## The concession

*We are **Jews by birth, not sinners out of the nations**.*

The Greek is **phusei Ioudaioi** — Jews by nature.

**Hamartoloi** — sinners. The standard Jewish description of Gentiles, quoted rather than
endorsed.

Paul grants the distinction and then dismantles it.

## The turn

*And yet **we know that a person is not made right by the works of the law but through faith in
Jesus Christ**.*

The Greek is **dikaioutai** — is justified, declared righteous. A legal verdict, not a moral
process.

**Ex ergon nomou** — out of works of law.

## What *works of law* means

The whole system of doing what the law prescribes.

The Galatian dispute was specifically about circumcision, food laws and calendar — but Paul's
argument is not limited to those. He says *no flesh*.

## The three statements

Paul says it three times in one verse.

*Not made right by the works of the law.*

*So that we might be made right by faith in Christ.*

*And not by the works of the law.*

Twice negative, once positive, in a single sentence.

## The last clause

*Because **by the works of the law no flesh will ever be made right**.*

The Greek is **ou dikaiothesetai pasa sarx** — not shall be justified all flesh.

It is Psalm 143: *do not enter into judgment with Your servant, for **in Your sight no one living
is righteous***.

## Who *we* is

*So **even we** put our trust in Christ Jesus.*

The Greek is **kai hemeis** — we also.

Jews by birth, with every advantage the law provided. Paul includes himself in the group that had
to do the same thing the Gentiles did.

## Why this is the letter's thesis

Everything after it is argument for it: Abraham believed God, the law came four hundred and
thirty years later, the tutor, the sons, the two covenants, the fruit of the Spirit.

## The verse two lines later

*"I have been crucified with Christ; it is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me… **and I do
not set aside the grace of God; for if righteousness comes through the law, then Christ died in
vain**."* See the note on Galatians 2:20–21.

The Greek is **dorean** — for nothing, gratuitously. The same word as *freely*.

## What Peter says about it later

*"We believe that **through the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ we shall be saved in the same manner
as they**."*

At the Jerusalem council, in Acts 15. The man rebuked in Antioch stands up and argues Paul's case.

## What James writes

*You see then that **a man is justified by works, and not by faith only**.* See the note on
James 2:21.

Two apostles, the same verb, two different questions. Paul is asking how a person is declared
righteous. James is asking what a claim to faith looks like when nothing follows from it.

> **The repetition.** Three statements of the same thing in one verse — twice negatively, once
> positively — closing on a line borrowed from a psalm.
