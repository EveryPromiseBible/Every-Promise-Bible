The narrator waits until the last line to say what the story was about, and then
says it flatly.

## The verdict

*So Esau treated his birthright as though it were nothing.*

Not *Jacob cheated him*. Genesis is perfectly willing to record Jacob's
manipulation elsewhere and does not spare him — but the summary of this scene
lands on Esau, and on his estimate of what he was holding.

He is not condemned for being hungry. He is condemned for the exchange rate.

## What a birthright was

The firstborn's portion: a double share of the inheritance, the headship of the
family, and in this family the covenant line — the promise made to Abraham and
passed to Isaac.

That is what was on one side of the trade. On the other side: lentils.

## The speed of it

*Esau ate and drank, then got up and left.*

Four verbs in a row, with no pause between them. The Hebrew reads as a rapid
sequence — he did not linger, did not reflect, did not appear to think anything
significant had occurred.

The transaction cost him nothing at the moment he made it. That is precisely what
the writer wants you to see.

## "I'm about to die"

*Look, I'm about to die. What good is a birthright to me?*

He was not about to die. He had come in from hunting, tired and hungry, and
talked himself into an emergency — and used the emergency to justify letting go
of the long thing for the immediate one.

> **What the New Testament does with him.** Hebrews 12 calls him godless and ties
> him to the warning about missing God's grace — *who traded away what was his by
> birth for a single meal.* The word there is about a person who treats sacred
> things as common. Esau's failure is presented as a failure of valuation. See
> the note on Hebrews 12:12–17.
