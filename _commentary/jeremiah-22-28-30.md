One of the strangest problems in the Bible is set up here, and it is not solved
until Matthew's first chapter.

## The sentence

*Write this man down as childless, a man who will not prosper in his days. For
none of his offspring will prosper, none will sit on the throne of David or rule
again in Judah.*

Coniah — also called Jeconiah and Jehoiachin — was king of Judah for three months
before Babylon carried him off. The curse is not that he has no children; he had
seven. It is that **none of his descendants will ever sit on David's throne.**

## Why that is a problem

God had also sworn to David that his throne would be established forever, and
that the Messiah would come from his line.

So the promise runs through David, and the royal line runs through Jeconiah, and
the royal line has just been declared permanently disqualified. Both statements
are in the same Bible and neither is withdrawn.

## How Matthew resolves it

Matthew's genealogy runs straight through Jeconiah — he names him — and traces
the line down to *Joseph, the husband of Mary*.

Then the wording changes. Every other link in that chapter says *was the father
of*. This one does not:

> *Jacob was the father of Joseph, the husband of Mary, and it was **from Mary**
> that Jesus was born.*

Joseph gives Jesus the legal claim to David's throne, as his adoptive father —
which He needs. But the blood does not come through Joseph, so the curse on
Jeconiah's descendants does not touch Him.

And Luke's genealogy takes a different route entirely, through David's son Nathan
rather than Solomon, bypassing the cursed line altogether.

> **What that means about the virgin birth.** It is usually argued from prophecy
> and from purity. This is a third reason, and it is legal: a Messiah descended
> physically from Jeconiah could not have taken the throne. The line had to arrive
> by another road, and Matthew's careful change of wording is where he shows you
> it did.
