A law about inheritance, written for a situation the book of Genesis is full of.

## The case

*If a man has **two wives, one loved and one unloved**, and both bear him sons, and **the
firstborn is the son of the unloved wife**.*

The Hebrew is *one beloved and one hated* — **senuah**, which in legal usage means the
less-favoured rather than the despised.

## Whose story this is

Jacob's. Two wives, one loved and one not — *Jacob loved Rachel*, and *when the LORD saw
that Leah was unloved, He opened her womb.*

Leah's firstborn was Reuben. Rachel's was Joseph.

The law is written about the exact case in Genesis 29, and Moses is writing it.

## The prohibition

*He **may not treat the son of the loved wife as the firstborn** in place of the true
firstborn.*

Preference is not allowed to redraw the inheritance. The law removes the father's
discretion.

## The requirement

*He must **recognize the firstborn**… and **give him a double share of everything he
owns**.*

The Hebrew is **pi shenayim** — literally *a mouth of two*, two portions.

The idiom uses **peh**, mouth, for a share. The same word is the edge of a blade, and the
teeth of Isaiah's threshing sledge. See the note on Isaiah 41:14–16.

## The reason given

*That son is **the first sign of his father's strength**; the right of the firstborn
belongs to him.*

The Hebrew is *the beginning of his strength* — **reshith ono**.

It is a statement about what the child represents rather than about his character.

## What happened in Jacob's family

He did exactly what the law forbids. Reuben lost the birthright — 1 Chronicles says so
outright: *Reuben was the firstborn, but because he defiled his father's bed, **his
birthright was given to the sons of Joseph***.

And Joseph received a double portion in the most literal sense: his two sons, Ephraim and
Manasseh, were each counted as a tribe.

Twelve tribes, thirteen names, because one man got two shares.

## Where Elisha uses the phrase

*"Please, **let me inherit a double portion of your spirit**."*

The same idiom. He is not asking to be twice the prophet Elijah was; he is asking to be
treated as the heir. See the note on 2 Kings 2:9–10.

Elijah's answer — *you have asked a hard thing* — makes sense in that light. It was not
his to allot.

## What Elisha shouted

*"**My father! My father!**"* — at the moment the chariot came. See the note on
2 Kings 2:11–12.

The word for the relation the double portion depends on.

## What the New Testament calls Christ

**Prototokos** — firstborn. *The firstborn among many brethren*, *the firstborn from the
dead*, *the firstborn over all creation*.

And of the readers: *the church of the firstborn who are registered in heaven.* See the
note on Hebrews 12:18–24.

> **What the law protects.** Not the favourite. It takes the father's preference out of
> the calculation entirely and fixes the double share to birth order.
