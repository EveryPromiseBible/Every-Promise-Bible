One of the darkest passages in the Old Testament, and the first thing to notice
is that God never speaks in it.

## What has already happened

*The Spirit of the LORD came upon Jephthah.* That is the verse immediately before
this one. He has already been equipped, already commissioned. The outcome is not
in doubt.

And then he makes a vow anyway.

## The vow

*If You will truly give the Ammonites into my hand… then whatever comes out of
the door of my house to meet me when I return in peace — it will be the LORD's,
and I will offer it up as a burnt offering.*

He is negotiating for something he has already been given. That is the shape of
the tragedy: not too little faith in God's power, but a bargain offered to
someone who had already said yes.

## What the law he lived under said

The Torah is unambiguous. Human sacrifice is named repeatedly as the thing the
nations did that God detests, and Leviticus provides a way to redeem a rash vow
by payment. A priest could have released him.

Nothing in the text presents the vow as pious or the outcome as required. Judges
records it; it does not commend it.

## How the book frames it

Judges is a downward spiral, and it ends with the line *everyone did what was
right in his own eyes*. Jephthah's story sits inside that. The book is describing
what happens to a people, not modelling behaviour.

## What he already had

*Then **the Spirit of the LORD came upon Jephthah**, and he passed through Gilead and
Manasseh… and advanced toward the people of Ammon.*

That is the verse immediately before the vow.

The Spirit is already on him. The commission is already given. Nothing in the passage asks him
for terms, and he offers them anyway.

## What the vow is

An attempt to secure something already promised, by putting up something of his own.

*Having begun in the Spirit, are you now being made perfect by the flesh?*

*"Nor shall you swear by your head, because you cannot make one hair white or black. But **let
your Yes be Yes**."*

## Where he still ends up

*And what more shall I say? For the time would fail me to tell of Gideon and Barak and Samson
and **Jephthah**, also of David and Samuel and the prophets.*

Hebrews names him in the roll of faith — a man with a bad vow, a shameful family history, and
a chapter nobody enjoys reading.

He is on the list, and the list is called faith.

## What was never asked for

Nothing in this book is ever bought. Every deliverer in Judges is raised up in the same
sentence pattern: *the LORD raised up a deliverer*, after the people cried out, with no terms
attached.

> **On what became of his daughter.** The Hebrew is genuinely ambiguous, and
> readers have divided for centuries between a literal sacrifice and a life
> dedicated in perpetual virginity — the text dwells oddly on her never marrying.
> The honest position is that we do not know. What the passage does say plainly
> is that a man bound himself needlessly, and that a child paid for it.
