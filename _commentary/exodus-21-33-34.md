Two verses of ordinary liability law, and Jesus argues from them twice.

## The case

*If a man **opens a pit, or digs one and does not cover it**, and **an ox or a donkey falls
in**.*

Cisterns were cut into rock across that country to hold winter rain. An uncovered one is
an invisible hole in the ground.

The law names two acts — opening an existing pit and digging a new one — and treats them
the same.

## Where the fault is

Not in the digging. Pits were necessary; every household needed water.

The fault is in leaving it open.

## The liability

*The **owner of the pit must make it good**.*

The Hebrew is *shall make it good* — **shalam**, from the root of **shalom**. To make
whole, to complete, to restore what is missing.

Restitution in this law is described as putting something back to wholeness.

## The settlement

*He **pays the animal's owner in silver**, and **the dead animal becomes his**.*

Both halves. He pays full value and takes the carcass, so the loss transfers entirely and
the injured party is not left holding a dead ox.

## What the surrounding law is like

The chapter runs on cases like this: a goring ox, an ox that has gored before, an ox
killed by another ox, a pit, a fire in a field, a borrowed animal.

Farm accidents, with rulings.

## Where Jesus uses it

*"**If your donkey, or your ox, fell into a well on the Sabbath**, which one of you would
leave him down there? **You would have him out that very moment**."*

Said to a room full of lawyers who had just watched Him heal a man on the sabbath and
could not answer whether it was allowed. See the note on Luke 14:1–6.

And in Matthew: *"What man is there among you who has one sheep, and **if it falls into a
pit on the Sabbath, will not lay hold of it and lift it out?** Of how much more value then
is a man than a sheep?"*

## The argument's shape

He takes a piece of livestock law they all knew and applies it upward: *of how much more
value then is a man*.

Their own practice on the day in question is the premise. Nobody in the room disputed that
they would pull the animal out.

## The two animals

The ox and the donkey appear together throughout the code — in this law, in the sabbath
command, in the rule about a neighbour's beast fallen under its load.

They are the working animals of a household. See the note on Exodus 23:12.

## What the pit law assumes

That somebody made the hole. The law does not deal with an animal that wandered off and
died; it deals with a hazard left uncovered by a person who knew it was there.

> **What the ruling requires.** Full value in silver, and the carcass taken off the
> owner's hands. The Hebrew for making it good is the word for making whole — and it is
> the law Jesus reaches for when they will not answer him.
