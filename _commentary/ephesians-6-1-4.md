Paul quotes the fifth commandment and adds a note about it — *which is the first
commandment with a promise attached to it* — and the aside is worth as much as
the quotation.

## What he is pointing out

Of the ten, this is the first that comes with an outcome named: *so that it may
go well with you and you may live a long life on the earth.*

Paul is not inventing the promise; he is drawing attention to the fact that it is
there. In a list mostly of prohibitions, one command arrives with a stated
benefit.

## How to read the promise honestly

It is a promise in the shape a promise takes in the Old Testament — a general
truth about how a society and a life tend to run, not a personal guarantee. The
same Bible records godly people who honoured their parents and died young.

Read as it was written, it is saying that a people who hold the generations
together last, and a people who do not, do not. Everyone who has watched a family
either work or come apart knows the observation is sound.

## The line to fathers

*Do not stir your children up to anger.*

Paul turns and addresses the party with all the power, which in a first-century
household was a genuinely radical thing to do. The word describes provoking
someone into exasperation — the slow kind, built up over time.

And the alternative he gives is not passivity: *raise them in the training and
correction of the Lord*. Not less involvement. Involvement of a different sort.

> **On "obey" and "honour."** Paul uses both, and they are not the same. Obeying
> belongs to children in a household. Honouring is what the commandment asks, and
> it has no expiry date — it is what an adult still owes an elderly parent long
> after obedience has stopped being the relevant word.
