David wrote this the day the LORD delivered him from all his enemies and from
Saul. It is the longest psalm of rescue in the book, and this is the middle of
it.

## Reaching down

*He **reached down from on high** and took hold of me. He **drew me up** out of
the deep waters.*

The Hebrew behind *drew me up* is the verb in Moses' name — *because I drew him
out of the water.*

David uses the language of a baby lifted out of a river to describe his own
rescue from Saul.

## "From those too strong for me"

*From my mighty enemy, from those who hated me, **from those too strong for
me**.*

He concedes the point without embarrassment. They were stronger. That is the
premise of the rescue, not an objection to it.

## The day

*They came at me **on my day of ruin**, but the LORD was my support.*

The Hebrew is *the day of my calamity*. He does not say there was no such day.

The whole passage takes for granted that the trouble happened, that he went
under, and that the deep waters were deep. He is not describing an escape from
the valley. He is describing being drawn up out of the middle of it.

## The wide place

*He **brought me into a wide open place**.*

**Merchab** — a broad place, open ground, room. It is the opposite of the Hebrew
word for trouble, which is built on the idea of being pressed and narrow.

Distress is being squeezed; deliverance is space. And note the verb: He brought
him *into* it — out of one place and into another.

## The reason given

*He rescued me **because He delighted in me**.*

That is the reason stated in the text, and it is stated before any of the lines
about clean hands.

The Hebrew is a word of pleasure and desire. He wanted to.

## Clean hands

*The LORD rewarded me for my right living. For clean hands He repaid me… I was
blameless before Him.*

David says this in a psalm written after Saul had hunted him for years and he had
twice refused to put out his hand against the LORD's anointed when he had him.

The delight comes first in the passage, and the record follows it.

> **Where the psalm appears twice.** It is Psalm 18 and it is also 2 Samuel 22 —
> the same song printed once in the songbook and once in the history. Whoever put
> the books together thought the day David was drawn out of deep water was worth
> recording in both.
