Peter points at a withered tree, and Jesus answers with three sentences about
prayer.

## "Have faith in God"

The first words, and they set the direction. The Greek can be rendered *have the
faith of God* — faith that has God as its object and its source.

Everything after this hangs on where the faith is placed, not on how much of it
there is.

## The tense that carries the promise

*Whatever you ask for in prayer, **believe that you have received it**, and it
will be yours.*

The verb is aorist — believe you *received*, past. Not that you will receive.

The sequence is: ask, take it as given, and then it becomes yours in experience.
That is an odd order by every ordinary measure, and it is what the sentence says.

## The mountain

*If anyone says to this mountain, "Be lifted up and thrown into the sea."*

They were walking from Bethany to Jerusalem, over the Mount of Olives, with the
Dead Sea visible in the distance. **This** mountain, and that sea.

And notice the verb: *says to*. Twice. The faith in the illustration is exercised
by speaking to the obstacle, not by talking to God about it.

## "Does not doubt in his heart"

The word rendered *doubt* is **diakrino** — to separate, to divide, to make a
distinction between two things.

So doubting, in this sentence, is not weak feeling. It is being divided in
yourself — one thing held in one hand and its opposite in the other.

And the two things being divided are named in the passage itself. Peter is
astonished at a dried-up fig tree; Jesus answers by talking about a mountain
going into the sea. A small withering and a mountain moved.

A man who separates those — who has faith for the tree and none for the mountain
— will not see either. That is what the verb rules out.

## The same word, at the table

Paul uses **diakrino** twice in 1 Corinthians 11: *not **discerning** the Lord's
body*, and *if we **judged** ourselves.*

Two things are handed over at that table, the body and the blood, and dividing
them is the failure Paul names. It is the same verb, doing the same work: a man
divided between two things God gave together.

See the notes on 1 Corinthians 11:27–30 and 1 Corinthians 11:31–34.

## The condition He attaches

*And whenever you stand praying, if you are holding something against anyone,
forgive it.*

Jesus attaches one thing to the whole passage, and it is not effort or
technique. It is forgiveness — and He makes it the immediate neighbour of the
promise.

Whatever unanswered prayer is about, He points at the person you have not
forgiven before He points anywhere else.

> **Why the fig tree is there.** The withering brackets Mark's account of Jesus
> clearing the temple — the tree is cursed on the way in and found dead on the way
> out, with the temple scene in between. Mark's sandwich construction is
> deliberate: a tree in leaf with no fruit, and a house of prayer with no prayer
> in it.
