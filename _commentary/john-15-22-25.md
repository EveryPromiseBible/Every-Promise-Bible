He explains what His coming did to the standing of the people who rejected Him.

## Where this is said

Still at the table, after the vine and before the promise of the Helper.

He has just told them *if the world hates you, you know that it hated Me before it hated
you* — and *a servant is not greater than his master*.

## The first sentence

*"**If I had not come and spoken to them, they would not be guilty of sin**."*

The Greek is *they would not have sin* — **hamartian ouk eichosan**.

Not that they were innocent before. The whole New Testament argues the opposite. This is
about one particular guilt: the guilt of having heard Him and refused.

## The cover

*"**But now they have no cover for their sin**."*

The Greek is **prophasin** — a pretext, an excuse, a plausible cover story.

They had one, and the coming removed it.

Paul makes the same move in Athens: *truly, these times of ignorance God overlooked, but now
commands all men everywhere to repent*.

## The middle sentence

*"**Whoever hates Me hates My Father as well**."*

Stated flatly, with no argument attached.

It is the negative of what He has already said in the same room: *he who has seen Me has
seen the Father*. See the note on John 14:8–11.

## The second condition

*"**If I had not done among them the works no one else has ever done**, they would not be
guilty of sin."*

Twice, the same structure: had I not spoken, had I not worked.

Words and deeds, and both count as evidence. John's Gospel is built on the works — he calls
them **semeia**, signs, and structures the whole first half around seven of them.

## What they had

*"But now **they have seen those works**, and they have **hated both Me and My Father**."*

Seeing is granted. The passage does not say they missed anything.

Lazarus is the case in point. The chief priests' response to a man being raised was to plot
to kill Lazarus as well.

## The quotation

*"And so the word written in **their law** comes true: **they hated Me for no reason at
all**."*

**Their law** — He says it that way, of the Scriptures He is quoting from, to men who
claimed them.

The line is from Psalm 69, and the Hebrew is **sinah chinnam** — hatred without cause. See
the note on Psalms 69:1–4.

The same psalm supplies the gall and the vinegar at the crucifixion.

## What *chinnam* means

Free of charge. Gratis. It is the word Satan uses of Job — *does Job fear God for nothing?*

Hatred with nothing owing on it, given away.

## What He says immediately after

*"But when the Helper comes, whom I shall send to you from the Father, the Spirit of truth
who proceeds from the Father, **He will testify of Me**. And you also will bear witness,
because you have been with Me from the beginning."*

The paragraph about hatred ends with the arrangement for testimony.

## What follows that

*"They will put you out of the synagogues; yes, the time is coming that whoever kills you
will think that he offers God a service."*

And His reason for telling them: *these things I have told you, that when the time comes,
you may remember that I told you of them*.

## What He says from the cross

*"Father, forgive them, for **they do not know what they do**."*

He argued here that the excuse was gone. Six hours later He pleads it for them anyway.

> **What the coming changed.** Not the fact of guilt — the availability of a pretext. And
> the sentence He picks to describe the response is one about hatred given away free.
