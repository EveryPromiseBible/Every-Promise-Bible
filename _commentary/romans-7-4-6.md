Paul applies the marriage illustration, and the application does not go where the
illustration seemed to be heading.

## Who died

*You were made **dead to the law** through the body of Christ.*

In the picture, the husband died and the wife went free. In the application, the
one who dies is **you**.

Paul is not saying the law was abolished. He says the party under obligation was
removed from its reach by death — a death that happened *through the body of
Christ.*

## "So that you could belong to Another"

Not so that you could belong to nobody. The release is not into independence.

*To Him who was raised from the dead* — and the language is marital, carried
straight over from the three verses before. The point of the funeral is the
wedding.

## What the law had been doing

*The sinful passions **that the law kept stirring up** were at work in every part
of us.*

This is Paul's most uncomfortable sentence about the law and he states it without
softening: the passions were stirred **by** the law.

He explains it later in the chapter — *I would not have known what coveting was
if the law had not said, do not covet* — and defends the law itself as holy. The
fault is not in the statute. But a prohibition, read by the old nature, works
like a lit match.

*And what they produced was death.* That is the fruit of the first marriage. The
second one bears fruit *for God*.

## The two fruits

Paul uses the same verb for both — **karpophoreo**, to bear fruit — and the difference is
in the preposition:

- *fruit **to death*** (verse 5)
- *fruit **to God*** (verse 4)

Neither marriage is barren. The question in the passage is not whether there will be
fruit but who it goes to.

And the fruit-bearing in verse 4 is stated as the purpose of the union, not as a
condition of it: *that you should be married to another… **that** we should bear fruit to
God*.

## "That which once held us fast has been put to death"

The Greek verb behind *held us fast* is to hold down, to confine — the word used
for a prisoner in custody.

Something that had a grip on us was killed, and the release follows from the
killing. Nobody wriggled free.

## Two ways of serving

*So that we serve in the **newness of the Spirit** and not in the **old way of the
written code**.*

Serving is in both halves. Paul never offers a release from service; he
distinguishes what the serving runs on.

The Greek for *written code* is **gramma**, letter — the same contrast he draws in
2 Corinthians 3: *the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life.*

> **What the chapter does next.** It gets worse before it gets better — the whole
> of *what I want to do I do not do*, and *who will deliver me from this body of
> death?* Then the answer, and then chapter 8: *there is therefore now no
> condemnation.* Paul takes the long way round on purpose.
