A verse often used as a threat, in a letter written to stop people being threatened. Read where it
stands and it says something else entirely.

## What it is not saying

Paul has already settled the question of penalty in this letter, in the plainest sentence in the New
Testament on the subject: *Christ has redeemed us from the curse of the law, **having become a curse
for us***.

He is not now reopening an account he declared closed three chapters ago. Whatever *reaping* means
here, it cannot mean the punishment of a believer's sin, because that was reaped by Somebody else on
a tree, and Paul is the one who told us so.

## Not sneered at

*Do not be misled: **God is not mocked**.*

**Mykterizetai** — literally *to turn up the nose*. The picture is a sneer, not a court.

And notice what the immediate context is: verse 6 is about sharing generously with the one who
teaches you the word. Paul's warning is aimed at people who were being tight-fisted and telling
themselves nothing followed from it.

## Two soils, and what each grows

*The one who sows into his own flesh will reap **decay** from the flesh; the one who sows into the
Spirit will reap **everlasting life** from the Spirit.*

**Phthora** — rot, decay, the natural breakdown of organic matter. It is not the language of a
sentence handed down; it is the language of compost. Flesh is not being punished with decay. Decay
is simply what flesh grows, because it is cut off from life and has nothing else in it.

And the other harvest cannot be a wage either, for the same reason in reverse: **zoen aionion**,
eternal life, is what Paul everywhere calls *the gift of God*. Nobody earns a crop of that by
farming well. You do not manufacture the seed, the soil, the rain or the life in the grain.

So the verse is not a threat and not a wage system. It is a description of two fields, and an
invitation to spend your days in the one where things live.

## What the paragraph does next

*And let us not grow weary while doing good, for in due season we shall reap if we do not lose
heart.*

That is where Paul takes it — straight to encouragement, aimed at tired people, promising them a
harvest. If he had meant the previous verse as a threat, this would be the strangest turn in his
writing.

> **Which field you are standing in was settled by grace.** You were not able to change soil; you
> were transplanted — *He has delivered us from the power of darkness and conveyed us into the
> kingdom of the Son of His love*. Sowing to the Spirit is not a new law to keep. It is what a
> person does naturally once he knows which field is his, and the crop at the end of it is life
> that does not stop.
