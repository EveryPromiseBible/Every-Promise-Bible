Two words in this passage are worth stopping on. One is Aramaic, and one Paul
appears to have coined himself.

## The word Paul built

*The Spirit who places you as a son with full standing* — behind that phrase is
one Greek word, **huiothesia**. It is not a normal word. Paul takes the word for
son and the word for placing and puts them together, and it appears almost
nowhere in Greek literature before he uses it.

It describes the Roman legal act of adoption, which was not what we mean by the
word. A Roman adoption transferred a person into a new family completely: old
debts cancelled, old obligations void, full inheritance rights, and it could not
be undone. It was more permanent than being born into a family, because a
natural son could be disinherited and an adopted one could not.

That is the standing Paul says the Spirit gives.

## The word Jesus used

*By Him we cry out, Abba, Father.*

*Abba* is Aramaic, not Greek — and Paul is writing to Rome, where hardly anyone
spoke it. He leaves it untranslated anyway, then puts the Greek beside it. He
does the same in Galatians. It is the word Jesus used in Gethsemane, and it
survived into Greek-speaking churches as a word people kept saying.

It is not baby-talk, as it is sometimes described. It is family speech — the
word a grown son would use of his own father, in the language Jesus prayed in.

## What it is set against

*You did not receive a spirit that makes you a slave again, dragging you back
into fear.*

That is the alternative Paul rules out, and notice what he calls it: fear, and
slavery, and *again* — a return to something. He is describing a relationship
run on anxiety about standing, and he says flatly that this is not what was
given.

The evidence he offers is not a feeling. It is the cry itself: the fact that a
person calls God Father at all is presented as the Spirit's own testimony,
witnessing *together with* our spirit.

## The line about suffering

*If indeed we suffer with Him, so that we may also be glorified with Him.* This
reads to many people like a condition attached at the end that could void the
whole thing.

But the sentence it belongs to is about inheritance — heirs of God, heirs
together with Christ. Paul's point is that being a co-heir means sharing what
Christ has, and what Christ has includes both a road that went through suffering
and a glory on the far side of it. It is a description of the inheritance, not
an entrance fee for it.

> **On "led by the Spirit" in the verse before.** The passage just above says all
> who are led by the Spirit are sons of God — and it is often read as a test of
> whether someone qualifies. Paul's next sentence is this one, and it goes
> straight to adoption, Abba and inheritance. He raises leading in order to say
> what it proves about a person's standing, not to make them anxious about
> whether they are being led well enough.
