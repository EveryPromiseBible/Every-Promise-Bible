The third servant returns the money intact and gives his reason first.

## What he hands back

*"Sir, **here is your mina**. I kept it **wrapped up safe in a cloth**."*

Nothing lost. He is not a thief or a spendthrift. By his own accounting he has been
careful.

A mina was about three months' wages — the same amount given to each of the ten
servants in this parable, unlike the talents in Matthew's version where the sums
differ.

## Why

*"**Because I was afraid of you. You are a hard man.** You collect what you never put
down, and you harvest what you never planted."*

The reason is stated before the excuse. He was afraid, and he was afraid because of
what he thought the man was like.

The Greek for *hard* is **austeros** — severe, exacting. It is the root of *austere*,
and it was used of unripe fruit: sharp, not sweet.

## What the master does with it

*"**Out of your own mouth I will judge you.**"*

He does not accept the description or argue with it. He works inside it.

*"**So you knew** I was a hard man…? Then **why did you not put my money on
deposit**, so that when I came back I could at least have collected it with
interest?"*

If that had really been his conviction, the bank was the obvious minimum. Fear that
does nothing at all is not even consistent with itself.

## What he was actually given

Read the instruction: *"**Put this money to work** until I return."*

The Greek is a trading word — do business with this. It is an invitation to activity,
handed out with the capital.

And the master had not asked for ten minas back. The first servant produced ten, the
second five, and both were commended without comparison.

## What the others got

*"You proved trustworthy with **a very small thing**, so take charge of **ten
towns**."*

Three months' wages, and cities in return. The reward is out of all proportion to
the sum, which tells you the money was never the point. See the note on
Luke 19:16–19.

## The other version

Matthew 25 tells a similar parable with talents and different amounts, and the third
servant says nearly the same sentence: *I knew you to be a hard man… so I was afraid,
and I went and hid your talent in the ground.* See the note on Matthew 25:24–30.

Two parables, two servants, and the same self-justification in both — a description
of the master, and fear following from it.

## Where this parable is told

*Because He was **close to Jerusalem now**, and because they were expecting the
kingdom of God **to appear at any moment**.*

Luke gives the reason for the story. They thought it was about to happen; He tells
them about a nobleman going away for a long time.

> **What the man believed, and what was true.** He said the master reaped where he had
> not sown. In fact the master had sown — he had put a mina in this man's hand
> himself. The one thing his description got wrong is the thing he was holding.
