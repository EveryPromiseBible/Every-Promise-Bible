He does not shout, argue or resist. He asks a legal question of the man nearest him.

## The timing

*As they were **tying him up for it**.*

The straps are already on. Luke puts the sentence in the middle of the preparation.

## Who he speaks to

*Paul said **to the centurion standing there**.*

Not to the commander who gave the order, and not to the soldiers doing the work. To the
officer supervising — the man legally responsible for what is about to happen.

## The question

*"**Is it lawful for you to flog a Roman citizen who has not even been condemned?**"*

Not a claim. A question about the law, put to a man whose career depends on the answer.

Two illegalities are named in one sentence:

- **A Roman citizen** — the Lex Porcia forbade scourging one.
- **Who has not even been condemned** — **akatakriton**, uncondemned. No trial, no
  verdict, no sentence.

## What Paul does not do

He does not appeal to his innocence, his mission, or his God. He cites Roman statute.

He had every opportunity to refuse this on other grounds and used the one that would
work.

## The centurion's reaction

*When the centurion heard that, he **went to the commander and reported it**.*

Immediately, and he leaves the prisoner where he is.

## What he says

*"**Be careful what you are about to do**, because this man is a Roman."*

A junior officer warning his superior. The Greek is *see what you are about to do* —
watch yourself.

The penalties were real. Scourging a citizen could end a commander's career, and worse.

## Why the claim was believed

Falsely claiming citizenship was a capital offence. That made an assertion of it
generally credible — nobody said it lightly.

## The word Paul uses about himself

**Rhomaios** — a Roman. He was a Jew of Tarsus, a Pharisee trained under Gamaliel, and a
citizen of the empire, and he uses each identity where it counts.

He had opened the speech before the mob in Hebrew and told them *I am a Jew, born in
Tarsus of Cilicia*. Twenty minutes later he tells a centurion he is a Roman.

Both true.

## The one time he did not use it

At Philippi he and Silas were *beaten with rods* and thrown into the inner prison, and
he said nothing about citizenship until the next morning — when he used it to make the
magistrates come and escort them out personally.

## What happens next

The commander comes and asks him directly, and finds out he was born to it while the
commander himself had to buy it. See the note on Acts 22:27–29.

## The citizenship he does not mention first

**Paul** waits until he is stretched out for the lash before saying it. **Luke** has him do the same
at Philippi — after the beating, not before.

*For **our citizenship is in heaven**, from which we also eagerly wait for the Saviour, the Lord
**Jesus Christ**.*

**Politeuma** — the register a person's name is on. **Paul** writes it to Philippi, a Roman colony
whose whole civic pride was the same privilege he is invoking here.

*Now, therefore, you are no longer strangers and foreigners, but **fellow citizens with the saints
and members of the household of God**.*

## What the Roman right was

Uncondemned citizens could not be scourged. The **flagellum** used for interrogation killed men, and
the commander has ordered it before establishing who he has.

## And the greater standing

*Therefore **you are no longer a slave but a son**, and if a son, then an heir of God through
**Christ**.*

**Paul** holds a citizenship he was born into and writes his letters about one he was not.

*But as many as received Him, to them **He gave the right to become children of God**.*

**Exousian** — the right, the authority. **John** uses the legal word.

> **What the question achieved.** He was still bound when he asked it, and the flogging
> never happened. Luke's account moves from the straps going on to the men stepping
> away in the space of four verses.
