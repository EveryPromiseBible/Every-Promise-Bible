God does not often swear oaths. When He does, it is worth stopping to see what
He binds Himself to — and here He binds Himself twice, on the same terms, about
two different things.

## The comparison is doing the work

*This is like the days of Noah to Me. I swore then that the waters would never
again cover the earth. So now I swear that I will not be angry with you or
rebuke you again.*

The argument runs through the flood. After it, God gave a sign in the sky and a
promise that took the form of a permanent renunciation: never again by water.
Everyone reading Isaiah knew that promise had held for centuries.

So the second oath is measured against the first. *That* is how settled this is.
Whatever you believe about the reliability of the rainbow, believe this on the
same footing.

## What is being sworn

Not that nothing hard will happen. The oath is specifically about **God's
disposition**: I will not be angry with you or rebuke you.

That is a strange thing for God to promise a people whose failures the same book
catalogues at length. Isaiah is not saying they became blameless. He is saying
something changed on God's side about how He will deal with them — and the
chapter before this one is the reason, which is why 53 and 54 sit next to each
other.

---

## The mountains

*Though the mountains walk away and the hills come loose and fall, My love for
you will never walk away, and My covenant of peace will never come loose.*

The image is deliberately absurd. Mountains are the standing metaphor for
permanence in Hebrew poetry — the oldest, heaviest, least movable things anyone
could point at.

Isaiah says: suppose those went. Suppose the most fixed thing you can see got up
and left. Even then. He picks the strongest available comparison and then says
this outlasts it.

## Who it is spoken to

The chapter opens by addressing a barren woman, a widow, someone abandoned and
ashamed. That is the audience being sworn to. Not a people at their best — a
people at their least deserving of a promise about God's permanent good will.

## Why God swears at all

*Thus, God, determining to show more abundantly to the heirs of promise **the immutability of His
counsel, confirmed it by an oath**, that by two immutable things, in which it is impossible for
God to lie, **we might have strong consolation**.*

**Hebrews** 6, and it explains the whole mechanism of this passage. An oath adds nothing to God's
truthfulness. It is added for the sake of the person listening.

He swears because they need Him to, not because He might otherwise.

## What made the second oath possible

The note above says the chapter before is the reason, and it is.

*But He was wounded for our transgressions… **the chastisement for our peace was upon Him**.*

**Musar shelomenu** — the discipline that produced our peace. Isaiah 53 has the anger fall
somewhere, and Isaiah 54 has God swearing it will not fall again on them.

*And by Him **to reconcile all things to Himself**, by Him, whether things on earth or things in
heaven, **having made peace through the blood of His cross**.*

## The covenant of peace

*My **covenant of peace** will never come loose.*

*Now may **the God of peace** who brought up our Lord **Jesus** from the dead, that great Shepherd
of the sheep, **through the blood of the everlasting covenant**, make you complete in every good
work.*

**Hebrews** 13 names the covenant, its blood, and the fact that the guarantee was already
demonstrated: He brought Him up from the dead.

## And the mountains

*Suppose the most fixed thing you can see got up and left. Even then.*

*"**Heaven and earth will pass away, but My words will by no means pass away.**"*

> **On "covenant of peace."** *Shalom* is wider than the absence of conflict; it
> is wholeness, everything in its right place. The word God attaches to His oath
> is not "truce." It is a settled state of things that He is committing Himself
> to maintain.
