Three verbs, three promises, and an argument that runs from bad fathers upward.

## What prompts it

*Lord, teach us to pray.*

The Lord's Prayer, and then the parable of the friend at midnight — a man banging on a
neighbour's door for bread because a visitor has arrived.

*Because of his persistence he will rise and give him as many as he needs.*

The Greek is **anaideian** — shamelessness. Literally *without shame*.

## The three

*"**Ask**, and it will be given to you. **Seek**, and you will find. **Knock**, and the door
will be opened for you."*

All three are present imperatives — keep asking, keep seeking, keep knocking.

Escalating effort: a word, a search, a hand on a door.

## The three again

*"**Everyone who asks, receives. Everyone who looks, finds. And for everyone who knocks, the
door is opened**."*

Said as command, then restated as fact. And the subject of the second set is *everyone*.

## The three questions

*"Is there a father among you who, when his son asks him for **bread, would hand him a
stone**?"*

*"Or if he asks for a **fish, would hand him a snake**?"*

*"Or if he asks for an **egg, would hand him a scorpion**?"*

Three pairs, and each pair is of things that resemble each other. A flat stone looks like a
loaf. An eel-like fish and a snake. A pale coiled scorpion and an egg.

The horror in the picture is not a father refusing. It is a father handing over something that
looks like the thing and is not.

Matthew has two of the three; Luke adds the scorpion.

## The argument

*"If **you, bad as you are**, know how to give good gifts to your own children."*

The Greek is **poneroi ontes** — being evil.

He says it in passing, to a crowd, as an agreed premise. Nobody objects.

## The conclusion

*"**How much more will the Father in heaven give the Holy Spirit to those who ask Him**."*

The Greek is **poso mallon** — by how much more.

The argument runs from the lesser to the greater: if flawed fathers do not trick their
children, then certainly not Him.

## The word Luke changes

Matthew has *how much more will your Father who is in heaven give **good things** to those who
ask Him*.

Luke has *the Holy Spirit*.

Same sermon material, and Luke — who wrote Acts — names the gift.

## Where the same reasoning goes

*He who did not spare His own Son, but delivered Him up for us all, **how shall He not with Him
also freely give us all things?*** See the note on Romans 8:31–34.

Paul's version of *how much more*, argued from the cross rather than from fatherhood.

## The other place in Luke

*"Do not fear, little flock, for **it is your Father's good pleasure to give you the
kingdom**."*

The Greek is **eudokesen** — it seemed good to Him, He was well pleased.

## What is not in the passage

Any hint that the Father is reluctant, or that the asking wears Him down.

The parable of the midnight friend could be read that way, and this paragraph is Luke's
correction of that reading: the neighbour gets up because he is being pestered; the Father is
described by contrast, not by comparison.

> **What the three pairs have in common.** Each is a counterfeit. The question is not whether
> a father would refuse — it is whether he would hand over something shaped like the thing
> asked for.
