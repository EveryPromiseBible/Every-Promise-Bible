---
key: Jonah 4:10–11
title: "Should I not care about Nineveh?"
updated: 2026-08-09
---
The book ends on a question, and nobody answers it.

## The plant

*"You cared about this plant, **though you did nothing to make it grow**. It came up in a night and
it perished in a night."*

The Hebrew is **asher lo amalta bo velo giddalto** — for which you did not labour and which you did
not grow.

God had *appointed* the plant, and then *appointed* a worm to kill it, and then *appointed* a
scorching east wind. The same verb as the fish. Four appointments, all of them uncomfortable, all
of them arguments.

## What Jonah is angry about

Shade. He has built himself a shelter east of the city and sat down *to see what would become of
the city* — hoping, apparently, that it might still be destroyed.

*"**It is right**," Jonah said. "I am angry enough to die."*

He says it about a plant, having said the same thing about a hundred and twenty thousand people two
paragraphs earlier.

## The argument

The logic is from the lesser to the greater, and it is the form **Jesus** uses constantly.

*"If you then, being evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, **how much more will your
Father who is in heaven** give good things to those who ask Him!"*

*"Are you not of **more value** than they?"*

## The question

*"**Should I not care about Nineveh, that great city, with more than a hundred and twenty thousand
people who cannot tell their right hand from their left, and also many animals?**"*

The Hebrew for *care* is **chus** — to pity, to spare, to look on with compassion. The same verb
used of Jonah and the plant.

## The last three words

*"**And also many animals.**"*

The book's final clause is about livestock. It is not a rhetorical flourish — the king's decree had
put sackcloth on the cattle, and God's closing sentence takes them into account.

*"Are not two sparrows sold for a copper coin? And **not one of them falls to the ground apart from
your Father's will**."*

## The hundred and twenty thousand

*Who cannot tell their right hand from their left.* Usually read as small children, though it may
mean the whole population's moral ignorance.

Either way it is stated as a reason for mercy rather than an aggravation.

*Then Jesus said, "**Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do.**"*

## No answer

The book stops. Jonah does not reply, the narrator does not resolve it, and the reader is left with
God's question hanging.

*"And he was angry and would not go in. Therefore his father came out and pleaded with him… '**It
was right that we should make merry and be glad, for your brother was dead and is alive again.**'"*

**Luke** 15 ends the same way. An elder brother outside a party, a father pleading, and no record of
what the son decided. **Jesus** told the story to Pharisees who were complaining that He ate with
sinners, and He left the ending off deliberately.

Jonah and the prodigal's brother are the same man, and both books hand the last line to the
listener.

> **How it ends.** With a question about whether God should be merciful, put to a prophet who
> objects — and no answer recorded. Jesus told the same story to the same kind of audience and
> stopped in the same place.
