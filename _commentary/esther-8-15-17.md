The man who sat at the gate in sackcloth comes out of the palace in blue and
white, and a city that had been reeling shouts.

## What he is wearing

*Royal robes of **blue and white**, a great **crown of gold**, and a mantle of
fine **linen and purple**.*

Blue and white are the colours of the Persian royal house, and they are also the
colours of the tabernacle hangings. Fine linen and purple are priestly cloth.

Four chapters earlier the same man was in sackcloth and ashes in the middle of
the city, crying out with a loud and bitter cry, and could not pass the gate
because no one in sackcloth was allowed in.

## The city

*And **the city of Susa shouted and was glad**.*

The last time Susa was described, in chapter 3, it *reeled in confusion* while the
king and Haman sat down to drink.

Same city, same writer, same word placement — and the reversal is the point.

## Four words for it

*For the Jews there was **light and gladness, joy and honour**.*

**Light** first. It is not the obvious word in a list about rejoicing, and it is
the word chosen to lead.

They had had no prophet, no temple, no king, and no name of God spoken over them
for a generation. What arrives is light.

## Everywhere the decree reached

*In every province and every city, wherever the king's word and his decree
arrived, there was gladness and joy among the Jews, feasting and a holiday.*

The gladness travels at the speed of the couriers. It is the arrival of the
letter that produces it — not the thirteenth of Adar, which is still eight months
off.

Nothing has happened yet. They are celebrating a word that has been sent.

## The last line

*And **many of the peoples of the land became Jews**, for the fear of the Jews had
fallen upon them.*

Outsiders join. The Hebrew is a rare verb — they *judaised*, made themselves
Jews.

The people who were *not My people*, hidden and scattered, are suddenly the thing
the empire wants to belong to.
