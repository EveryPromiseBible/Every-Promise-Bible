Paul takes the word his critics were using and keeps it.

## The two audiences

*The message of the cross is **foolishness to those who are perishing**, but to us who are
being saved **it is the power of God**.*

The Greek for *foolishness* is **moria** — the root of *moron*.

Both participles are present continuous: those who **are perishing**, those who **are
being saved**. Paul describes two directions of travel rather than two finished states.

## What crucifixion meant to them

Cicero called it *the most cruel and disgusting penalty* and said the very word should be
kept away from Roman citizens. It was reserved for slaves, rebels and the lowest
criminals.

Preaching a crucified Saviour in that world was not a difficult doctrine. It was an
absurdity.

## The quotation

*"**I will destroy the wisdom of the wise**, and the cleverness of the clever I will set
aside."*

Isaiah 29, spoken about counsellors advising Judah to trust Egypt.

## The three questions

*Where is **the wise one**? Where is **the scribe**? Where is **the debater of this present
age**?*

A Greek philosopher, a Jewish scholar, and a professional arguer. Between them they cover
every kind of authority in Corinth.

## What did not work

*Since, **in the wisdom of God**, the world **through its own wisdom did not come to know
God**.*

The Greek is *in the wisdom of God* — the arrangement was deliberate. God set it up so
that this route would not arrive.

## What He chose instead

*It **pleased God to save those who believe through the foolishness of the message we
announce**.*

The Greek for *the message* is **kerygma** — the thing proclaimed, the herald's
announcement.

Paul calls his own preaching foolish, and says God preferred it.

## The two demands

*The Jews **ask for a sign** and the Greeks **go searching for wisdom**.*

Both are reasonable requests. Both had been offered honestly for centuries.

## What is preached instead

*But we proclaim **Christ crucified** — **a stumbling block to the Jews and foolishness to
the Greeks**.*

The Greek for *stumbling block* is **skandalon** — the trigger of a trap, the thing that
trips you.

Paul does not adjust the message for either audience. He names the offence it gives to
each and preaches it unchanged.

## The turn

*Yet **to those who are called**, both Jews and Greeks, **Christ the power of God and the
wisdom of God**.*

The two things they were asking for — power and wisdom — are named, and both are located
in the person they found offensive.

## The last line

*For **the foolishness of God is wiser than men**, and **the weakness of God is stronger
than men**.*

Paul concedes the terms and then measures with them.

He does not argue that the cross is really quite clever. He grants it looks foolish and
weak, and says God at His most foolish outdoes men at their wisest.

## What he says about the church itself

*Not many wise according to the flesh, not many mighty, not many noble, are called. **But
God has chosen the foolish things of the world to put to shame the wise**.*

The congregation is the same argument in miniature.

> **The strategy in the passage.** Paul never softens the offence. He states exactly what
> each audience objects to, agrees that it looks that way, and says God chose it on
> purpose.
