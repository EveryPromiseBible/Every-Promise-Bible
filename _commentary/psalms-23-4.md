One verse, and everything in it turns on a single preposition.

## "Through"

*Even when I walk **through** the valley of the shadow of death.*

Not into it, not down to it, and not around it. Through.

The word assumes an entrance and an exit. Nobody is promised a route that avoids
the valley; the promise is about what the valley is — a passage, not a
destination.

## "I walk"

Not run, not flee. The pace does not change.

## The valley

The Hebrew is **tsalmaveth** — a compound of *shadow* and *death*, and it is used
of deep darkness, the places where light does not reach.

A shadow requires a light source and an object, and it can frighten but it cannot
touch. David is a shepherd writing about the ravines he walked his flock through,
where the walls are high and the sun does not get in.

## Where the psalm changes person

Read the psalm from the beginning and watch the pronouns.

*The LORD is **my** shepherd… **He** makes me lie down… **He** leads me… **He**
restores my soul.*

He is being talked about. Then the valley:

*For **You** are with me.*

The moment the darkness comes, the psalm stops speaking about Him and starts
speaking to Him. That is where He stops being the subject of a sentence and
becomes the person being addressed.

## "I will fear no evil"

The reason given is not that there is no evil in the valley. It is *for You are
with me.*

## The rod and the staff

Two implements, and they do different jobs.

The **rod** is the club — a shepherd's weapon, carried against what comes at the
flock. The **staff** is the crook, for guiding and lifting a sheep out of a place
it has got itself into.

One is used on the enemy, one on the sheep, and both are listed as comfort.

> **What is on the other side of the valley.** *You prepare a table before me in
> the presence of my enemies.* He does not clear the room first. The enemies are
> still there and the meal is served anyway.
