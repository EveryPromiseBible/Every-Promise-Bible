David writes a lament for the man who hunted him for years, and orders it taught to
the nation.

## What he does with it

*He **ordered that the people of Judah be taught it** — the Song of the Bow, written
down in the Book of Jashar.*

Not a private grief. He has it learned by heart and archived.

The Book of Jashar is cited once more in Scripture, for Joshua's long day, and has
not survived. See the note on Joshua 10:12–14.

## The refrain

*"**Oh, how the mighty have fallen!**"*

Three times — at the start, in the middle, and as the last line.

## What he will not do

*"**Don't tell it in Gath.** Don't spread the news in the streets of Ashkelon."*

Gath is Goliath's town, and the place David had lived as a fugitive when Saul drove
him out.

He had every reason to want the news carried there. He asks for silence.

## The mountains

*"**Mountains of Gilboa — may no dew fall on you**, no rain touch your slopes, no
fields yield an offering."*

He curses the ground for taking them.

## The shield

*"For there the shield of the mighty was thrown down, **the shield of Saul, no longer
anointed with oil**."*

Leather shields were rubbed with oil to keep them supple and turn a blade. An unoiled
shield cracks, and stops being a shield.

Whatever David means by it, he chose that image for Saul's end.

## What he says about them both

*"Saul and Jonathan — **beloved and beautiful in life, and in death they were not
divided**. Swifter than eagles, stronger than lions."*

Saul had thrown a spear at his own son over David. David's song puts them together
and calls them undivided.

## What he credits Saul with

*"**Daughters of Israel, weep for Saul**, who dressed you in scarlet and fine things,
who put ornaments of gold on your clothing."*

He finds something to praise, and it is Saul's provision for the women of Israel.

There is not one line of criticism in the whole lament. Not the spears, not the cave,
not the years in the wilderness, not Nob.

## Jonathan

*"I grieve for you, Jonathan, my brother. **You were so dear to me. Your love to me
was wonderful, deeper than the love of women.**"*

The covenant between them is in 1 Samuel 18 — Jonathan gave David his robe, his
armour, his sword, his bow and his belt, which is to say his claim on the throne.

## The last line

*"Oh, how the mighty have fallen, and **the weapons of war are lost**."*

He ends on the weapons rather than the men.

## Where the Amalekite fits

The chapter opens with a man arriving at David's camp with Saul's crown, claiming to
have finished him off, expecting a reward. See the note on 2 Samuel 1:5–10.

David tears his clothes, mourns until evening, has the man executed for laying a hand
on the LORD's anointed — and then writes this.

## Who the song is for

The man who threw a spear at him twice, hunted him through the wilderness with three thousand
men, and killed eighty-five priests for helping him.

*"**Saul and Jonathan were beloved and pleasant in their lives.**"*

There is not one line of grievance in the whole lament. David had two chances to kill him and
took neither, and now that he is dead there is no settling of accounts even in poetry.

## What he says instead

*"**Tell it not in Gath**, proclaim it not in the streets of Ashkelon."*

His first instinct is to protect the reputation of the man who ruined his youth.

*"But I say to you, **love your enemies**, bless those who curse you, do good to those who
hate you."*

*Then Jesus said, "**Father, forgive them**, for they do not know what they do."*

## What he orders

*He commanded them to **teach the children of Judah the Song of the Bow**.*

A national curriculum item. He does not merely feel it — he legislates that the next
generation will learn to sing his persecutor's elegy by heart.

> **What he had said twice while Saul was alive.** *The LORD forbid that I should
> stretch out my hand against the LORD's anointed.* He had him in a cave and in a camp
> and did not touch him, and the song is consistent with the two occasions he had the
> chance.
