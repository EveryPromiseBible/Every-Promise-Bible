A challenge is issued, and the answer takes it: one of the two is invisible, and it is not works.

## The objection

*Someone will say, "**You have faith, and I have works**."*

The Greek is **erei tis** — someone will say.

## Who is speaking

Notoriously disputed. The quotation marks could close after *works*, or later, and the Greek has no
punctuation.

Read as an objector, the line sets faith and works against each other as two separate gifts.

## The reply

*"**Go on, then — show me**."*

The Greek is **deixon moi tēn pistin sou chōris tōn ergōn** — show me your faith apart from your
works.

An impossible demand, stated as a demand.

## Why impossible

Faith is not visible. There is nothing to point at.

The challenge cannot be met, and the sentence is built so that it cannot.

## The counter

*"**Mine you will see by what I do**."*

The Greek is **kagō soi deixō ek tōn ergōn mou tēn pistin** — and I will show you my faith out of my
works.

## The preposition

**Ek** — out of, from.

The works are the evidence, not the substance. He does not offer works instead of faith; he offers
them as the only available display of it.

## Who the audience is

Another person. *Show me.*

The whole exchange is horizontal. Nobody in the sentence is showing anything to God.

## Where Paul's audience is

*If Abraham was justified by works, he has something to boast about, **but not before God**.* See the
note on Romans 4:1–3.

The Greek is **pros Theon**.

Paul specifies the courtroom he means. James specifies his: *show me*.

## The example

*Was not Abraham our father **justified by works** when he offered Isaac his son on the altar?* See
the note on James 2:21.

And the verse Paul quotes for the opposite conclusion — *Abraham believed God, and it was accounted
to him for righteousness* — is quoted by James too, three verses later.

## The timing

Genesis 15: Abraham believes, and it is counted to him.

Genesis 22: he offers Isaac.

Something like thirty years apart. James says the second *fulfilled* the first — **eplērōthē**, was
filled full.

## The other example

*Likewise, was not **Rahab the harlot** also justified by works when she received the messengers?*
See the note on James 2:25.

A patriarch and a prostitute, chosen as the two illustrations.

## The line either side of it

*What does it profit, my brethren, if someone says he has faith but does not have works? **Can faith
save him**?* See the note on James 2:14–17.

And *faith without works is dead*.

The Greek is **nekra** — a corpse. Not *false*. Something that was the right shape and is not
breathing.

## What the letter is not arguing against

Not Paul. The letter is likely earlier than Romans.

James is arguing against a man who says he has faith and whose life shows no sign of it — *depart
in peace, be warmed and filled*, said to somebody with no coat.

> **The impossible demand.** *Show me your faith without your works.* There is nothing to point at,
> and the sentence is built so that there cannot be.
