The man being paid to curse Israel opens his mouth and this comes out.

## Who is speaking

**Balaam**, hired by Balak king of Moab to curse Israel as they camped on the plains
opposite Jericho.

He is not an Israelite, not a prophet of the LORD by office, and is described elsewhere
in Scripture as a man who loved the wages of unrighteousness.

He is standing on a hilltop with seven altars behind him, paid to say the opposite of
this.

## The address

*"**Rise up, Balak**, and hear; listen to me, son of Zippor."*

He makes the king stand for it.

## The claim

*"**God is not a man, that He should lie**; He is not a son of man, **that He should
change His mind**."*

The Hebrew is *nor a son of man that He should repent*.

Samuel says nearly the same words to Saul: *the Strength of Israel will not lie nor
relent, for He is not a man, that He should relent.*

## The two questions

*"Does He speak, and then **not act**? Does He promise, and then **not fulfill**?"*

Both are rhetorical, and both are about the distance between saying and doing.

## The admission

*"I have received **a command to bless**."*

Not permission. A command.

## The sentence

*"**He has blessed, and I cannot reverse it**."*

The Hebrew is *He has blessed, and I cannot turn it back*.

The man with the fee in his hand states plainly that the thing he was hired to do is
outside his power.

## How many times he tries

Four oracles, from three different hilltops, with seven altars and seven bulls and seven
rams built each time.

Balak keeps moving him — *come to another place from which you may see them; you shall
see only the outer part of them* — as though a different angle would help.

Every time, a blessing.

## What Balak says

*"**I called you to curse my enemies, and look, you have kept on blessing them these
three times!**"*

And Balaam's answer: *"If Balak were to give me his house full of silver and gold, **I
could not go beyond the word of the LORD**."*

## What Israel was doing at the time

Nothing. They are in their tents at the bottom of the hill and do not know any of this is
happening.

The whole episode takes place over their heads.

## What the next oracle says

*"**He sees no wrong in Jacob, no trouble laid up against Israel**."* See the note on
Numbers 23:21–24.

## How Balaam ends

Not well. Numbers 31 records his death, and the counsel he gave Balak afterward — to seduce
Israel into idolatry at Peor, since cursing had not worked.

The New Testament names it three times: the way of Balaam, the error of Balaam, the
doctrine of Balaam.

He could not reverse the blessing, so he went after the people instead.

## What cannot be turned back

*"He has blessed, and **I cannot reverse it**."*

*For **the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable**.*

The Greek is **ametameleta** — not to be repented of, not subject to a change of mind. Paul
uses it about Israel, and the argument he is making is the one Balaam made from a hilltop
with a fee in his pocket.

## Why it holds

*"**God is not a man, that He should lie**; He is not a son of man, that He should change
His mind."*

*Thus God, determining to show more abundantly to the heirs of promise **the immutability of
His counsel**, confirmed it by an oath, that **by two immutable things, in which it is
impossible for God to lie**, we might have strong consolation.*

Hebrews takes the same ground and names what it is for: so that people holding a promise
would have something that cannot move.

*For **all the promises of God in Him are Yes**, and in Him Amen.*

> **Where the sentence comes from.** Not from a prophet, a psalm, or a friend of Israel.
> From a hired man on a hill, standing in front of the king who paid him, saying he
> cannot take it back.
