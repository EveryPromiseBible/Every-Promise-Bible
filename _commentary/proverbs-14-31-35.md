Five proverbs, and three of them are about what happens at scale.

## The poor and their Maker

*Oppress the poor and **you insult their Maker**. **Honor Him by showing them kindness**.*

The Hebrew is **choreph oseihu** — reviles his Maker.

The argument is not that the poor deserve better. It is that they were made, and the insult goes
past them to the one who made them.

The proverb appears again in chapter 17: *he who mocks the poor reproaches his Maker*.

## The shelter

*The wicked are **swept off by their own evil**. The upright **find shelter even in death**.*

The Hebrew for the first is **yiddacheh** — thrust down, driven away.

**Uvemoto choseh tzaddiq** — and in his death the righteous has refuge.

A striking line for the Old Testament, where the afterlife is rarely in view. The refuge is not
*from* death; it is *in* it.

## Wisdom in the heart

*Wisdom **rests quietly in the understanding heart**. Even among fools **it makes itself known**.*

The Hebrew is **tanuach** — rests, settles.

Wisdom sits still where there is understanding. Where there is not, it is still detectable — the
second line says it becomes known even among fools, which may mean it stands out by contrast.

## The national proverb

*Righteousness **lifts a nation up**. Sin is **a disgrace to any people**.*

The Hebrew is **tzedaqah teromem goy** — righteousness exalts a nation.

**Rum** — to raise, to lift high. And **cheshed** in the second line, a rare noun usually meaning
reproach or shame.

## The two words for people

**Goy** — nation, in the political sense. And **le'ummim** — peoples.

The proverb is not about Israel. It uses the general words, and applies to any nation.

## What kind of righteousness

The book's usual sense: right dealing between people. Honest scales, fair judgments, the poor not
crushed.

Proverbs has spent chapters on false weights, moving boundary stones, and bribes in court. The
proverb is the summary of all of it.

## The scale

Every other proverb in this cluster is about an individual. This one is about a country, using
the same vocabulary.

## The king

*A king delights in **a servant who acts wisely**, but **his anger falls on the one who brings
shame**.*

The Hebrew is **maskil** — one who acts with insight, prudently.

Proverbs is court literature, addressed to young men being trained for administration, and the
last line brings the sequence back to the palace.

## What Joseph was

*"**Can we find such a one as this, a man in whom is the Spirit of God?**"… "There is no one as
discerning and wise as you."* See the note on Genesis 41:37–45.

A servant who acted with insight, in front of a king, during a famine.

## What Daniel was

*In all matters of wisdom and understanding about which the king examined them, **he found them
ten times better***.

Two men who fit the last proverb exactly, in two foreign courts.

## Where the exaltation comes from

*For **exaltation comes neither from the east nor from the west nor from the south. But God is the
Judge: He puts down one, and exalts another***. See the note on Psalms 75:6–8.

The verb is the same root, **rum**.

> **The two words.** **Goy** and **le'ummim** — the general terms for nations and peoples. The
> proverb is not written about Israel, and its scope is anybody.
