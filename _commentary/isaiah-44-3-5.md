Two pourings, and the second explains the first.

## What comes before

*"Yet hear now, O Jacob My servant, and Israel whom I have chosen. Thus says the LORD who made
you and formed you from the womb, who will help you: **Fear not, O Jacob My servant; and you,
Jeshurun, whom I have chosen**."*

**Jeshurun** — the upright one. An affectionate name for Israel, used four times in the Bible.

## The first pouring

*For I will **pour water on the thirsty land** and **streams on the dry ground**.*

The Hebrew is **tsame** — thirsty. And **yabbashah**, dry ground — the word for the dry land in
Genesis 1 and at the Red Sea.

## The second

*I will **pour out My Spirit on your children** and **My blessing on your descendants**.*

The two lines are parallel, which is how Hebrew poetry defines its terms. Water on dry ground
is the picture; Spirit on children is the thing.

The verb is the same for both — **yatsaq**, to pour out.

## Who receives it

*Your children… your descendants.*

The Hebrew is **zar'akha** — your seed, and **tse'etsa'ekha**, your offspring.

The promise is not to the generation being addressed. It is over their shoulder.

Peter uses the same shape at Pentecost: *the promise is to you and to your children, and to
all who are afar off*.

## The growth

*They will **spring up like grass in a meadow, like willows by flowing streams**.*

The Hebrew for the second is **aravim al-yivlei mayim** — poplars or willows by water
channels.

Both images are of things that grow fast where there is water and not at all where there is
not. Nothing is said about their effort.

The same picture as the tree by the river: *he shall be like a tree planted by the rivers of
water*. See the note on Psalms 1:1–3.

## The three declarations

*One will say, "**I belong to the LORD**."*

*Another will call himself by **the name of Jacob**.*

*Another will **write on his hand, "The LORD's,"** and take the name Israel.*

Three people, three ways of saying it, and each is more public than the last.

## The hand

The Hebrew is *shall write on his hand, "To the LORD"*.

A mark on the skin, visible, permanent, and the kind of thing a slave or a soldier carried to
show whose he was.

Isaiah has already used the image the other way round: *see, **I have inscribed you on the
palms of My hands**; your walls are continually before Me*.

Both hands are written on.

## What the chapter goes on to

The longest satire on idol-making in the Bible.

A man cuts down a cedar. *He burns half of it in the fire; with this half he eats meat… and
the rest of it he makes into a god, his carved image. He falls down before it and worships…
and says, "Deliver me, for you are my god!"*

*And no one considers in his heart… "Shall I fall down before a block of wood?"*

## The line after that

*"I have blotted out, like a thick cloud, your transgressions, and like a cloud, your sins.
**Return to Me, for I have redeemed you**."*

Return, because you have already been redeemed. The order is not the expected one.

## The other promise about the children

*"**As for Me,** says the LORD, **this is My covenant with them**: My Spirit who is upon you,
and My words which I have put in your mouth, shall not depart from your mouth, nor from the
mouth of your descendants."* See the note on Isaiah 59:21.

Three generations named there too.

> **How the metaphor is defined.** Isaiah does not leave it to the reader. Water on dry ground
> in one line, and the Spirit on your children in the next — the same verb both times.
