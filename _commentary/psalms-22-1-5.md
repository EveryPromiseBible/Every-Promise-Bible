The opening line of this psalm is the sentence Jesus cried from the cross, in
Aramaic, at the ninth hour.

## The cry

*My God, my God, **why have You abandoned me?***

He did not compose it there. He quoted it. And in that culture, naming the first
line of a psalm called up the whole psalm.

Read the rest of it and it is a description of a crucifixion written a thousand
years before the Romans invented one: *they pierced my hands and my feet… I can
count all my bones… they divide my garments among them and cast lots for my
clothing… all who see me mock me, they shake their heads.*

## "My God" — twice

Even in the abandonment the possessive holds. He says *my* God, twice, to the One
He is saying has left Him.

## What is different here from every other psalm of distress

*I cry out **by day, but You do not answer**, by night, but I find no rest.*

David's psalms of trouble almost always turn on a rescue that comes. This one
records the silence and does not explain it.

## The line that follows

*Yet **You are holy**, enthroned on the praises of Israel.*

The word *yet* carries it. Nothing is being accused. In the middle of the
forsaking, holiness is affirmed.

## The three verbs about the fathers

*In You our fathers **trusted**. They **trusted**, and You **delivered** them.
They **cried** to You and were **rescued**. They trusted in You and were **not put
to shame**.*

Everyone before Him trusted and was delivered. That is what makes the first line
what it is: the pattern held for all of them, and it did not hold here.

He is the one exception in the history of the psalm. He trusted, and He was not
delivered.

## How the psalm ends

It does not stay there. From the middle it turns: *You have answered me.* And it
closes on a proclamation to a people not yet born — *they shall come and declare
His righteousness to a people who will be born, that **He has done it**.*

Three Hebrew words at the end. The Greek of the New Testament renders the same
thought in one: *tetelestai*. It is finished.

## The three psalms together

- **22** — the cross, forsaken, *He has done it*
- **23** — the shepherd, the valley, the table, goodness and mercy following
- **24** — *lift up your heads, O gates… the King of glory shall come in*

Death, then care, then the King coming in through the gates. They stand in that
order in the book. See the notes on Psalms 23:1–3 and Psalms 24:7–8.

> **What is not in the psalm's opening.** Any answer to the question. He asks
> *why*, and no reply is recorded. The answer is on the other side of the psalm, in
> the last line, and it is not an explanation — it is a completed work.
