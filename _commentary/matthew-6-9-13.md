It is the most said prayer in the world, which is exactly why it is worth
slowing down over. Jesus gives it as a pattern — *pray like this* — immediately
after telling people not to heap up words as though volume helped.

## Our Father in heaven

Two words in tension, and both matter. **Father** — relationship, nearness, a
family word. **In heaven** — and He is not one of us.

Notice it is *our*, not *my*. The whole prayer is plural: our bread, our debts,
lead us, deliver us. Jesus taught it to be prayed by someone who has other
people in mind, even when they are alone.

## May Your name be held holy

Not a request that God become holy. The verb asks that His name be *treated* as
what it is — set apart, not used as small change.

## May Your kingdom come

The first thing asked for is not a personal need. And the line that follows tells
you what a kingdom coming looks like: *may what You want be done, here on the
earth just as it is done in heaven.* Two lines, one request. God's rule arriving
is God's will being done.

---

## Give us today the bread we need for today

The Greek adjective here — **epiousios** — is famous for being almost unknown. It
appears in no other surviving Greek text of the period apart from this prayer.
Every translation is a considered guess: bread for the coming day, bread that is
needed, daily bread.

Whatever it means precisely, the request is small and immediate. Today's bread,
today. Nobody is asked to pray for a storehouse.

## Forgive us our debts, as we ourselves have forgiven

Debts, not sins — Matthew uses the commercial word, something owed. And the
second half has already happened: *as we ourselves have forgiven*, past tense.
It is not a bargain being struck. It is a person praying who has already let
somebody off.

This is the only line Jesus goes back and comments on after the prayer ends, so
He evidently expected it to be the sticking point.

## Do not lead us into the time of testing

The word covers both testing and temptation. Given James says plainly that God
tempts nobody, the request is best read as it stands: *keep us out of the trial*
— a prayer of ordinary self-knowledge, from people who would rather not find out
how they would do.

> **On the last line.** *For the kingdom is Yours, and the power, and the glory* —
> it is absent from the earliest manuscripts of Matthew and was almost certainly
> added early, from church use. It is not a corruption; it is a congregation's
> Amen that ended up in the text, and it echoes David's prayer in 1 Chronicles.
> The app prints it because it is what most readers know.
