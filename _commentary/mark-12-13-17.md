A trap with two jaws, and the answer walks between them.

## Who is sent

*Later they sent some of **the Pharisees** and some of **Herod's people** to Him.*

Two groups who agreed on almost nothing.

The Pharisees resented Roman rule and the tax that funded it. The Herodians were the party of a
client dynasty that existed by Roman permission.

They have been put on the same errand.

## The purpose

*Hoping **to catch Him in something He said**.*

The Greek is **agreusosin** — to catch as in hunting, to snare.

## The flattery

*"Teacher, **we know that You are honest and that You are not swayed by anyone**, because **You take
no notice of who a person is**. You teach the way of God as it truly is."*

The Greek idiom is **ou blepeis eis prosopon** — you do not look at the face of men.

Every word of it is true. That is what makes it a trap: they are closing off the escape of
evasion before they ask.

## The question

*"So tell us: **is it right to pay the tax to Caesar, or not? Should we pay it, or should we
not?**"*

The Greek is **kensos** — the Latin *census*, the poll tax. One denarius a year, levied on every
adult in Judea since AD 6, payable only in Roman coin.

The tax that had provoked the revolt of Judas of Galilee.

## The two jaws

Say yes, and He loses the crowd — He has endorsed the occupation.

Say no, and the Herodians have a sentence to carry to Pilate. Which is exactly what they later
claim He said: *we found this fellow… forbidding to pay taxes to Caesar*.

## What He sees

*Jesus **saw straight through the act**. "**Why are you testing Me?**"*

The Greek is **hupokrisin** — their play-acting. The theatre word.

## The request

*"**Bring Me a denarius and let Me look at it**."*

He does not have one.

They produce one immediately, in the temple, which is its own small answer.

## The coin

A silver denarius of Tiberius, with his head in profile and the inscription **TI CAESAR DIVI AVG
F AVGVSTVS** — Tiberius Caesar, son of the divine Augustus.

A graven image and a claim to divine parentage, in the pocket of men asking a question about
religious purity.

## The question back

*"**Whose face is on this, and whose name?**" "**Caesar's**," they said.*

The Greek is **eikon** — image. The word in the Septuagint at Genesis 1:27: *God created man in
His own **image***.

## The answer

*"**Give Caesar back what belongs to Caesar, and give God what belongs to God**."*

The Greek is **apodote** — give back, render, return what is owed. Not *give*.

The coin carries Caesar's image, so it is his; hand it back.

## The second half

And what carries God's image?

He does not say. He has just used the word **eikon** of a coin and left the audience to finish
the sentence.

## The result

*And they were **left marveling at Him**.*

The Greek is **exethaumazon** — an intensified form. Utterly amazed.

Nobody in the passage says another word.

## What He pays elsewhere

*"Nevertheless, lest we offend them, go to the sea, cast in a hook… and when you have opened its
mouth, you will find a piece of money."* See the note on Matthew 17:24–27.

The temple tax, paid out of a fish, by someone who had just established He was exempt.

## What Paul says about the same coins

*Render therefore to all their due: **taxes to whom taxes are due**.* See the note on
Romans 13:6–7.

The same verb, **apodote**, and Paul writing to Christians in Rome about Rome.

> **The word.** **Eikon** — image. He asks whose image is on the coin, gets the answer, and then
> names a second category without saying what carries God's.
