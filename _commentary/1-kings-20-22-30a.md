A defeated army works out what went wrong, and gets it exactly backwards.

## The theory

*"**Their gods are gods of the hills** — that's why they beat us. But if we fight
them **on the plains**, we'll surely be stronger."*

It is a reasonable conclusion by the standards of the time. Gods were local. Each had
territory, and you fought accordingly.

They had lost in the hills, so they book the rematch on flat ground.

## The reorganisation

*"Replace the kings with regular commanders, raise another army as big as the one you
lost, **horse for horse and chariot for chariot**."*

Nothing wrong with the military reasoning either. They rebuild to exact
specifications and change the command structure.

Every adjustment they make is competent, and the premise underneath all of it is
wrong.

## What Israel looked like

*Camped in front of the vast Aramean force, the Israelites looked like **two little
flocks of goats**.*

The Hebrew is two little flocks of kids — young goats.

It is the writer's image, not a boast from either side, and it is the most vivid line
in the chapter.

## The word

*"**Because the Arameans think the LORD is a god of the hills and not of the
valleys**, I will hand this whole vast army over to you, and **you will know that I
am the LORD**."*

The reason given for the victory is not Israel's righteousness — and Ahab is one of
the worst kings in the book, married to Jezebel.

It is the sentence the Arameans said out loud.

God answers a piece of bad theology by demonstrating the opposite in the place they
chose.

## "You will know that I am the LORD"

Said twice in the chapter — once at the first battle in the hills, once here in the
valley.

The same purpose given for both terrains.

## Seven days

*For **seven days the two camps faced each other**.*

A week of standing in the valley looking at an army that outnumbers you, before
anything happens.

The word had already been given on day one.

## What happened

A hundred thousand foot soldiers in one day, and a wall falling on twenty-seven
thousand more at Aphek.

## Who was king

Ahab. The man who *did more to provoke the LORD God of Israel to anger than all the
kings of Israel before him*, according to the chapter before.

God sends him a prophet with a battle plan, twice, and delivers him both times — and
the stated reason is never Ahab.

> **What Ahab does with the victory.** Makes a treaty with Ben-Hadad and lets him go,
> and a prophet tells him *because you have let slip out of your hand a man whom I
> appointed to utter destruction, your life shall go for his life.* He wins in the
> valley and loses it at the negotiating table.
