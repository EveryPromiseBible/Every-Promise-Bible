Paul finishes the argument of eight chapters by asking questions instead of
making statements, and the silence where the answers should be is the point.

## The questions

- *If God is for us — who is there left to stand against us?*
- *Who is going to lay a charge against the ones God Himself has chosen?*
- *Who is left to condemn?*
- and, a few lines on, *who shall separate us from the love of Christ?*

They are courtroom questions — the language of accusation, charge and
condemnation. Paul invites anyone who wants to prosecute to step forward, and
nobody does.

## The argument from the greater to the lesser

*He did not spare His own Son but handed Him over for us all — how will He not,
along with Him, freely give us everything else?*

This is the logic the whole paragraph rests on. If the hardest thing has already
been given, nothing smaller is in doubt. Paul does not argue that God is generous
in general; he argues from one specific act that has already happened.

The phrase *did not spare* echoes Genesis 22, where Abraham does not withhold his
son. Paul is putting the two scenes beside each other deliberately.

## Who is at the right hand

*It is Christ who died — and more than that, who was raised — who is now at God's
right hand, and who is pleading there for us.*

The only figure the passage places in a position to condemn is the one described
as interceding. That is the answer to the questions: the seat the prosecutor
would have to occupy is already taken by the defence.

> **The word "freely give."** The verb is built on *charis* — grace. Paul does
> not say God will *provide*; he says He will **grace** us with all things,
> alongside the Son. The giving of everything else is described in the same
> vocabulary as the giving of Christ.
