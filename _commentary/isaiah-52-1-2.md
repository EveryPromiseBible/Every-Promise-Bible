Six imperatives in two verses, and every one is addressed to somebody sitting down.

## The doubling

*Wake up, wake up, O Zion.*

The Hebrew is **uri uri** — the verb twice.

Isaiah has just used the same doubling on God, at the end of the previous chapter: *awake,
awake, put on strength, O arm of the LORD*. And before that, *awake, awake, stand up, O
Jerusalem, you who have drunk the cup of His fury*.

The same command is aimed at God, then at the city.

## The clothing

*Clothe yourself in **your strength**. Put on your **garments of splendor**, O Jerusalem,
holy city.*

The Hebrew is *put on your beautiful garments*.

Her own strength, and her own clothes. Nothing is being handed over — she is told to wear
what belongs to her.

## What is over

*Never again will **the unclean and the ungodly enter you**.*

The Hebrew is *the uncircumcised and the unclean*.

The city had been walked into by Babylonian troops. This is a promise about that ending.

## The dust

*Shake off the dust. **Rise up. Sit enthroned**, O Jerusalem.*

Three commands about posture in one line.

The Hebrew of the middle one is *arise, sit* — get up off the ground and sit somewhere else.

Dust is the mourner's position and the prisoner's. Job sat in ashes; the exiles *sat down by
the rivers of Babylon and wept*.

## The chains

*Free yourself from the **chains around your neck**, O **captive daughter of Zion**.*

The Hebrew is *loose yourself from the bonds of your neck*.

She is told to do the loosing. The verb is reflexive, and the chapter offers no explanation
of how.

## What the next verse says

*For thus says the LORD: "**You have sold yourselves for nothing, and you shall be redeemed
without money**."*

The transaction that put her there cost nothing, and the one that gets her out costs her
nothing.

## Where the chapter goes

*How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him who brings good news, who proclaims
peace… who says to Zion, "**Your God reigns!**"*

Paul quotes it in Romans about preaching.

And then the watchmen: *they shall see eye to eye when the LORD brings back Zion.*

## Where it ends

*Behold, My Servant shall deal prudently; He shall be exalted and extolled and be very
high… **His visage was marred more than any man**, and His form more than the sons of men.*

The last three verses of chapter 52 are the opening of the passage everyone calls Isaiah 53.
The chapter division falls in the middle of a poem.

*Wake up, shake off the dust* is four verses before *He is despised and rejected of men*.

## What it is quoted for

Paul, twice, in the same shape:

*Awake, you who sleep, arise from the dead, and Christ will give you light.*

And *the night is far spent, the day is at hand. Therefore let us cast off the works of
darkness, and **let us put on the armour of light***.

Take something off, put something on, and get up — the three moves of Isaiah's two verses.

## Who the commands are for

Not for people who need to be rescued. For people whose rescue has been announced.

The chapter says *you shall be redeemed* in verse 3, and the imperatives are in verses 1
and 2.

> **What is not explained.** How to loose the chains. She is told to do it, told to stand up
> and told what to wear — and the reason given is in the verse after: *you shall be
> redeemed without money.*
