A starving widow with one meal left is asked to give it away before she eats it,
and what happens next is one of the plainest pictures of provision in the Bible.

## What she had

*A handful of flour in a jar and a little oil in a jug.* She is gathering sticks
to bake it, and she says out loud what the plan is after that: *we'll eat it, and
then we'll die.*

She is not being dramatic. That is arithmetic.

## What she is asked for

*Make a little loaf for me **first**, and then make something for yourself and
your son.*

The word is doing all the work. Not instead — first. The prophet is not asking
her to go without; he is asking her to change the order.

And the request comes with *don't be afraid* in front of it, and a promise behind
it.

## The promise

*The jar of flour will not run out, and the jug of oil will not run dry, until
the day the LORD sends rain.*

Not a barn filled overnight. A jar that does not empty. The supply is daily, and
it lasts as long as the drought does — provision measured to the length of the
need.

## What she did

*So she went and did what Elijah said.* No argument recorded. A foreign widow in
Sidon — Jezebel's own country, outside the covenant — takes an Israelite
prophet's word and bakes the last of her flour for him.

*And there was food every day.*

> **Who Jesus mentions.** Preaching in Nazareth, He says there were many widows in
> Israel in Elijah's day, and the prophet was sent to none of them — only to this
> one, in Sidon. It is one of the sentences that made His hometown want to throw
> Him off a cliff. See the note on Luke 4:16–21.
