*Here, the men who collect the tenth are men who die. There, the one who collects
it is the one the testimony says is alive.*

One sentence, and the tense in it is the thing to see.

## Two priesthoods, two conditions

- **here** — the Levites, receiving tithes and dying, generation after
  generation. Aaron's robes taken off one man and put on his son, over and over.
- **there** — Melchizedek, of whom the record says only that he lives. No
  genealogy, no death notice, no successor.

The writer's argument is that Christ is a priest *in the order of Melchizedek*,
and that this order does not run out of men.

## The present tense

The verb for receiving is present. The writer does not say tithes *were*
received; he says they are, and that the one receiving them is testified to be
living.

That is a striking thing to leave standing in a letter written decades after the
cross. Whatever a reader concludes about how much or how often, the picture the
sentence gives is of a living Priest still receiving what is brought to Him.

## Why the argument needed Abraham

Abraham gave the tenth to Melchizedek before Levi was born — and the writer says
Levi, still in his ancestor, paid it through him. The greater blesses the lesser;
the lesser pays the tenth.

So the tenth in scripture is first an act of honour toward a priest-king who
blesses, and only later a Mosaic arrangement for feeding a tribe. The order runs
blessing first, response after, from the very first time it appears.

> **Where the chapter lands.** *He is able to save completely those who come to
> God through Him, because He always lives to intercede for them.* The same word
> — He **lives** — carries both halves: the one who receives, and the one who
> never stops praying for the people who come.
