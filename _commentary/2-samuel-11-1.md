One verse of scene-setting, and everything that follows is in it.

## The season

*When spring came around, **the season when kings ride out to war**.*

The Hebrew is *at the return of the year, at the time when kings go out*.

Campaigns were fought in spring — after the rains, when the roads were passable and
there was fodder for the animals. It was the calendar, and everybody knew it.

## What he did

*David **sent Joab** out with his officers and the whole army of Israel.*

He sends. The army goes, the siege is laid, the war is prosecuted.

The machinery works without him, which is part of the problem.

## The clause

*But **David stayed behind in Jerusalem**.*

Six words in the Hebrew, and the writer puts them at the end of the verse where they
cannot be missed.

Nothing about it is illegal. A king delegating a siege is not a crime, and David had
fought enough wars.

## What the writer is doing

He gives the season first, then the fact that everyone else went, then the exception.

The whole verse exists to place David in the wrong location at the right time of
year, and then the next verse opens: *and it happened one evening that David arose
from his bed and walked on the roof.*

## The chapter it introduces

Bathsheba, Uriah, the letter carried by the man it condemned, and a child who dies.

Nathan's parable comes in the next chapter, and God's own summary of the charge is
about Uriah rather than Bathsheba: *you have killed Uriah the Hittite with the sword…
now therefore the sword shall never depart from your house.*

## The other man in the story

Uriah, offered leave and a night at home, sleeps at the palace door instead:

*"The ark and Israel and Judah are dwelling in tents… **shall I then go to my house to
eat and drink and lie with my wife?** As you live, and as your soul lives, I will not
do this thing."*

The foreign soldier will not go home while the army is in the field. The king of
Israel had not gone out with it.

## What the sons of Issachar had

*Men who **understood the times and knew what Israel should do**.*

Two hundred of them came to Hebron to make David king. See the note on
1 Chronicles 12:23–37.

## What is not in the verse

Any judgement. The writer records where the army was and where the king was, and
leaves it.

## The three words

*But **David stayed behind** in Jerusalem.*

Three words in the Hebrew. Everything in the rest of his reign runs down through them.

He is where he should not be, doing nothing, at the season when kings go out — and the
narrator states it flatly and moves on to the roof.

*Therefore let him who thinks he stands **take heed lest he fall**.*

## What the verse does not say

Any suggestion he was tempted before he was idle.

## What comes afterwards

Nathan, a story about a lamb, *you are the man*, and a psalm: *create in me a clean heart, O
God, and renew a steadfast spirit within me… **restore to me the joy of Your salvation**.* See
the note on Psalms 51:3–4.

He does not ask to be restored to the throne. He asks for the joy back.

## Where the line still runs

*Jesse begot **David the king**. David the king begot Solomon **by her who had been the wife
of Uriah**.*

Matthew will not let the reader past it. He does not write *by Bathsheba* — he writes the
sentence that names the crime, in the genealogy of the Messiah. See the note on
Matthew 1:1–3.

The line runs straight through the worst thing David ever did, and Matthew prints it.

> **How much rests on one clause.** The rest of David's reign runs downhill from this
> chapter — Amnon, Tamar, Absalom, the flight from Jerusalem — and the narrative traces
> all of it back to a spring he did not go out.
