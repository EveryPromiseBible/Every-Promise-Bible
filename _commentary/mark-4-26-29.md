The only parable that appears in Mark and nowhere else.

## The opening

*"The kingdom of God is like this. **A man scatters seed on the ground**."*

That is the whole of his contribution to the story. He throws it and the parable moves on
without him.

## What he does next

*"He **goes to bed at night and gets up in the morning**."*

Sleep and rise, sleep and rise. The Greek verbs are present tense and repetitive.

Nothing is said about weeding, watering or worrying. The farmer's activity for the duration
of the parable is going to bed.

## What happens meanwhile

*"And **all the while the seed sprouts and grows**."*

The Greek is **blastai kai mekunetai** — sprouts and lengthens.

## The admission

*"And **he has no idea how**."*

The Greek is **hos ouk oiden autos** — he himself does not know.

This is the sentence the parable exists for. Mark puts it in the middle.

A first-century farmer knew everything worth knowing about when to sow and when to reap, and
nothing at all about what happens underground.

Neither does anyone else. Ecclesiastes says it: *as you do not know what is the way of the
wind, or how the bones grow in the womb of her who is with child, so you do not know the
works of God who makes all things.*

## The soil

*"The soil brings it along **all by itself**."*

The Greek is **automate** — of itself, spontaneously. The root of *automatic*.

The word occurs twice in the New Testament. The other is Acts 12, where the iron gate of the
prison *opened to them of its own accord* while Peter thought he was seeing a vision.

Both times it describes something happening without human agency while a person stands
there.

## The stages

*"First the **stalk**, then the **head**, then the **full ripe grain** in the head."*

Three, in order, and none of them can be hurried or skipped.

The farmer cannot make the head come before the stalk. He can only see which stage it is at.

## The harvest

*"And when the grain is ready, he **takes the sickle to it, because the harvest has come**."*

The Greek is **apostellei to drepanon** — sends forth the sickle.

The phrase is from Joel: *put in the sickle, for the harvest is ripe*. Revelation quotes the
same line at the end of the age.

The farmer's two actions in the parable are throwing the seed and swinging the sickle. He
does the beginning and the end and sleeps through everything in between.

## Where it sits in Mark

Between the parable of the sower with its four soils, and the mustard seed with its
disproportionate result.

Three seed parables in a row, and this is the one about the process nobody sees.

## What the sower parable added

*The sower sows the word.* See the note on Matthew 13:18–23.

If the seed is the word, then this parable is about what the word does after it is heard,
during the time nobody is watching it.

## The other agricultural sentence

*I planted, Apollos watered, but **God gave the increase**. So then neither he who plants is
anything, nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase.*

Paul's version, and he reaches the same conclusion by a different route.

## What is not in the parable

Soil types, birds, thorns, rocky ground, an enemy sowing tares.

Mark has just told the one with four soils and will shortly tell the mustard seed. This one
has no obstacle in it at all.

> **The line the parable is built around.** *He has no idea how* — said of a farmer who is
> asleep while the thing he threw is doing the only work in the story.
