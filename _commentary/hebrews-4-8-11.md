The writer's whole case rests on one word in a psalm and on a name that means two
things.

## The argument

*If **Joshua had given them rest**, God would **not have spoken later on about another
day**.*

Joshua brought them into Canaan. The land was taken, the tribes settled, and the book
ends *the LORD gave them rest all around*.

Four hundred years later David writes *Today, if you will hear His voice, do not harden
your hearts… they shall not enter My rest.* See the note on Psalms 95:8–11.

The writer's point: you do not offer a rest that has already been given.

## The name

In Greek, Joshua and Jesus are the same name — **Iesous**. The older English versions
print *Jesus* here, which is why the verse used to confuse readers.

Both mean *the LORD saves*.

## What follows

*So **there is still a Sabbath rest waiting to be kept** by the people of God.*

The Greek is **sabbatismos** — a Sabbath-keeping. The word appears once in the New
Testament, here, and the writer appears to have coined it.

## What it consists of

*Anyone who has entered His rest **has stopped working himself**, the way **God stopped
when His own work was done**.*

The comparison is Genesis 2. God rested on the seventh day, and He did it because the
work was finished, not because He was tired.

That is the model given: rest as the response to completion.

## The instruction

*So **let us work hard at entering that rest**.*

The Greek is **spoudazo** — be diligent, make every effort, hurry.

An exertion, aimed at a rest. The writer puts the two words in one sentence and leaves
them there.

## What the effort is against

*So that **nobody falls by copying the same refusal to believe**.*

The Greek is *the same example of disobedience* — and the word for disobedience in this
letter is consistently tied to unbelief. Three verses earlier: *they could not enter in
because of unbelief.*

The thing to work at is not falling into the pattern of the generation that would not
go in.

## The pattern being avoided

They stood at the border, sent twelve spies, heard two of them, and would not cross.

Forty years of walking followed, and Numbers records what they said: *let us select a
leader and return to Egypt.*

## Where the chapter goes next

*For the word of God is living and powerful, and sharper than any two-edged sword…
piercing even to the division of soul and spirit, and is a discerner of the thoughts and
intents of the heart.*

And then, immediately: *seeing then that we have a great High Priest… **let us therefore
come boldly to the throne of grace**, that we may obtain mercy and find grace to help in
time of need.*

The chapter about rest ends at a throne with an invitation to come to it without
hesitating.

> **What the writer keeps returning to.** *Today.* He quotes it three times across two
> chapters. The whole argument is that the day the offer is open is the one the reader
> is in.
