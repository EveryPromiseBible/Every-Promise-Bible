Not a separate instruction. In the Greek it is the tail end of the sentence before it.

## The verb

*Throw the whole weight of what worries you onto Him.*

The Greek is **epiripsantes** — an aorist participle. Having thrown upon.

**Epi-** upon, **rhiptō** — to hurl, to fling.

## Where the word comes from

The Septuagint uses it of the disciples throwing their garments on the colt.

And of the psalm: *Cast your burden on the LORD, and He shall sustain you*.

## The grammar

A participle, not an imperative.

It attaches to the verb in the previous line — *humble yourselves under the mighty hand of God*.
See the note on 1 Peter 5:5–6.

## What that means

The casting is how the humbling is done.

Peter does not give two commands, one about pride and one about worry. He gives one, and this is
its method.

## Why worry is a pride problem in this sentence

Carrying it yourself assumes the load is yours to manage.

The sentence puts the handover under the heading of getting low.

## The noun

*What worries you.*

The Greek is **merimnan** — anxiety, care. Singular.

From **merizō**, to divide. The word describes a mind pulled into parts.

The whole of it, as one thing, goes across in one throw.

## The same word elsewhere

*"**Do not worry** about your life… look at the birds of the air."* See the note on Matthew 6:25–34.

And the seed among thorns: *the **cares** of this world… choke the word*.

## The reason

*You are **on His mind**.*

The Greek is **hoti autō melei peri humōn** — because it matters to Him concerning you.

**Melei** — an impersonal verb: it is a care to him.

## The exchange in the sentence

Your **merimna** goes to Him because you are His **melei**.

The same subject matter — being on somebody's mind — moving in one direction.

## What the disciples said with it

*"Teacher, **do You not care** that we are perishing?"*

The Greek is **ou melei soi** — the same verb, in the boat, in the storm. See the note on
Mark 4:37–39.

Peter was in that boat.

## What surrounds the verse

*Be sober, be vigilant; because **your adversary the devil walks about like a roaring lion**.* See
the note on 1 Peter 5:8–9.

The verse before it is about humility; the verse after it is about a lion. The casting sits between
them.

## Why that order

A man carrying everything himself is the one who has no attention left for anything else.

## Where Paul puts the same instruction

*Be anxious for nothing, but in everything **by prayer and supplication, with thanksgiving**, let your
requests be made known to God.* See the note on Philippians 4:6–7.

And the result: *the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your hearts*.

**Phrourēsei** — a military word. Will garrison them.

> **The grammar.** It is a participle, not a command of its own. Throwing the weight across is how
> Peter says the humbling is done.
