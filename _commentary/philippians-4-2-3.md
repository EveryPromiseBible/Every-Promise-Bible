---
key: Philippians 4:2–3
title: "Two names, and what he says over them"
updated: 2026-08-09
---
Two women in Philippi could not agree, and it is in the Bible forever. What Paul does with it is the
whole letter in miniature.

## He pleads with each of them separately

*Euodia, **I plead with you**; Syntyche, **I plead with you**.*

The verb is repeated, once for each name. He will not write *I urge you both* and let the shorter
form imply that one of them is the problem.

And he never says who is right. In a letter where he is perfectly willing to name error and deal
with it, he declines to adjudicate this. He does not ask what it was about.

## What he asks for instead

*Think the same way together **in the Lord**.*

**To auto phronein** — the same verb he used in chapter two, where the mind to be had is *the mind
that was in Christ Jesus*, who did not treat equality with God as a thing to be grasped.

That is the answer to this quarrel, laid down two chapters before the quarrel is mentioned. Paul
does not give them a procedure for conflict. He gives them a Person going downstairs, and then a
name to say.

And the common ground he names is not the issue. It is **en Kyrio** — in the Lord. Agreement is not
found by working the disagreement until it yields; it is found somewhere else, in Someone both of
them are already in.

## Not troublemakers

*They **laboured at my side** in the good news.*

**Synethlesan** — contended alongside me, an athletic word, the same root as *athlete*. These are
veterans. They fought beside him in the founding of the church, probably in Lydia's house, and now
they cannot be in the same room comfortably.

Paul's assessment of them does not change because of it, and he asks his *true partner* to lend
them a hand rather than to settle the matter over them.

## The sentence he adds

*And their names are **written in the Book of Life**.*

Said over two women in the middle of an unresolved falling-out. Not *if they sort this out*. Not
*though they are behaving badly*. Written — and the tense in the Greek is a perfect, standing on
the page.

*Rejoice that your names are written in heaven*, He told the seventy when they came back excited
about the wrong thing. The most settled fact about a Christian is usually the one that has nothing
to do with how the week is going.

> **What he says over them while it is still going on.** Their names are in the book. That is the
> ground he stands them on before he asks them for anything, and it is the reason the ask is a plea
> rather than a threat. Grace does not wait for the quarrel to be over to say what is true about
> the people in it.
