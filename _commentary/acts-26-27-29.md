The prisoner turns the hearing round and starts questioning the king.

## What has just happened

Paul has told his story — Tarsus, the Pharisees, the persecution, the Damascus road, the
commission.

And Festus has shouted: *Paul, you are beside yourself! Much learning is driving you mad!*

Paul's reply: *I am not mad, most noble Festus, but speak the words of truth and reason. For the
king, before whom I also speak freely, knows these things… **for this thing was not done in a
corner***.

## The question

*"King Agrippa, **do you believe the prophets?**"*

A prisoner cross-examining the man judging him.

The question is designed. Agrippa cannot say no — he is the custodian of the temple and the
appointer of high priests, and the room knows it.

And he cannot say yes without conceding Paul's whole argument, which has been that the prophets
said the Messiah would suffer and rise.

## The answer Paul supplies

*"**I know that you believe**."*

He does not wait.

## Agrippa's reply

*"**You almost persuade me to become a Christian**."*

The Greek is **en oligo me peitheis Christianon poiesai**, and it is genuinely ambiguous.

It can be read as the King James has it — *almost you persuade me* — or as *in a short time you
would make me a Christian*, which most modern versions prefer, and which reads as deflection or
irony.

The Greek does not settle it, and the note does not either.

## The word

**Christianon** — Christian.

It occurs three times in the New Testament. Once at Antioch, where the disciples *were first
called Christians*. Once in Peter's letter: *if anyone suffers as a Christian, let him not be
ashamed*.

And here, in a Herod's mouth.

## The answer

*"**Whether it takes little or much**, I would pray to God that **not only you but everyone
listening to me today** might become **what I am**."*

He takes the ambiguity and covers both readings — little or much, either way.

*Not only you but everyone listening* — the governor, the tribune, the commanders, the prominent
men of the city, and whoever was standing at the back.

## The clause

*"**Except for these chains**."*

The Greek is **parektos ton desmon touton**.

He has been wearing them for two years and has just been talking with his hands.

It is a joke, and it is the only thing in the sentence he does not wish on them.

## What it tells you about him

He would give away everything he has except the one thing costing him something — and he says so
in front of the man who could release him.

## What the room decides

*"**This man is doing nothing deserving of death or chains**."*

And Agrippa to Festus: *this man might have been set free if he had not appealed to Caesar*.

Innocent, and going to Rome anyway.

## Why he had appealed

Because Festus had asked whether he would go to Jerusalem for trial, and Paul knew about the
forty men who had sworn not to eat until he was dead.

And because he had been told: *be of good cheer, Paul; for as you have testified for Me in
Jerusalem, **so you must also bear witness at Rome***.

## Where he ends up

Rome, in a rented house, receiving all who came to him, *unhindered*. See the note on
Acts 28:28–29.

> **The one exception.** He wishes everybody in the room were what he is — and then names the
> single item he is keeping to himself.
