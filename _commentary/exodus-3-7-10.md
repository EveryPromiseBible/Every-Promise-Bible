Four hundred years of silence, and then four verbs in a row.

## The four

*I have seen it — I have truly seen the misery of my people… I have heard them
crying out… **and I know their pain**. So I have come down to rescue them.*

Seen, heard, known, come down. The Hebrew doubles the first — *seeing I have
seen* — which is how that language says *thoroughly*.

The third is the one to sit with. **I know their pain.** Not *I am aware of the
situation*. The verb is the ordinary word for knowing by experience, the same one
used of intimacy.

## What He was doing during the silence

Nothing in the chapter suggests He had been absent. The claim is the opposite: He
had been watching the whole time, and the seeing is recounted before any action
is described.

That matters for a people who had been in Egypt four centuries and would
reasonably have concluded otherwise.

## Where He is bringing them

*Out of that land into a good land, a wide-open land, a land flowing with milk and
honey.*

The Hebrew for *wide-open* is spacious, broad — a word used elsewhere of being
brought out into a large place after being hemmed in. Milk and honey is the
shorthand for a land that produces without being forced: pasture rich enough for
herds, and wildflowers enough for bees.

He does not describe it as adequate. He describes it as generous.

## The last line

*Now go. I am sending you to Pharaoh.*

Four verbs of divine action, and then the fifth verb is Moses'. The rescue God
has just described in the first person arrives through a reluctant eighty-year-old
with a stick.

> **What is not offered.** Nowhere is Egypt made comfortable. The answer to the
> misery is not improved conditions in the place they were in — it is out, and
> then up into somewhere else entirely.
