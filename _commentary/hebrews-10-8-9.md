The writer takes the psalm he has just quoted and separates it into two halves.

## The method

*First He says… **then** He says.*

He has quoted Psalm 40 whole in the verses before. See the note on Hebrews 10:5–7.

Now he goes back over it and splits it, because the order of the two statements is the
argument.

## The first half

*"Sacrifices and offerings, whole animals burned, offerings made for sin — **You did not
want them, and they gave You no pleasure**."*

Four categories, listed twice in the chapter.

## The clause he adds

*And **those are the very things the law requires**.*

The writer inserts this himself. It is not in the psalm.

Everything just dismissed was commanded in Leviticus, by God, in detail — the species,
the age, the handling of the blood, the portions burned.

He wants the reader to notice that the psalm is dismissing what the law demands.

## The second half

*Then He says, "**Here I am. I have come to do what You want**."*

The Greek is the standard reply of a person reporting for duty — the answer Abraham,
Moses, Samuel and Isaiah all give.

## The conclusion

*He **cancels the first arrangement** in order to **put the second one in its place**.*

The Greek is **anairei** — takes away, removes, abolishes. It is also the word for
putting a person to death.

And **histesin** — sets up, causes to stand.

Two verbs, and the second is the purpose of the first.

## What is being replaced

Not sacrifice by nothing. Sacrifice by a will.

The first arrangement was a system of offerings. The second is a person saying *I have
come to do what You want*.

## Why it has to be one or the other

The writer does not allow both to run. The first is removed **in order that** the second
may stand.

The Greek makes the purpose explicit.

## What it accomplishes

The next verse: *by that will **we have been sanctified** through the offering of the
body of Jesus Christ **once for all**.*

**By that will** — not by the blood alone, in the writer's phrasing. By the willingness
that carried it.

**Once for all** — **ephapax**, once, finally, never repeated.

## Who is speaking in the psalm

David wrote it. The writer of Hebrews puts it in Christ's mouth *as He came into the
world*, and reads the whole thing as spoken at the incarnation.

He does this repeatedly in the letter — Psalm 2, Psalm 45, Psalm 110, Psalm 40.

## What the readers were being pulled back toward

The temple, which was still standing and still functioning. The whole letter is written
to people considering going back to it.

The argument here is that the first arrangement was removed by the second, and not by
neglect.

> **What the two halves are.** A rejection of offerings and a statement of willingness,
> in the same psalm, in that order. The writer says the second one is why the first one
> ends.
