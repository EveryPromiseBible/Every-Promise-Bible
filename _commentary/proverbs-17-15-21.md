Seven proverbs, and the first one names two opposite errors and gives them the same
verdict.

## The two

*Clearing the guilty and condemning the just, **the LORD hates them both alike**.*

The Hebrew is *he who justifies the wicked and he who condemns the just* — the two verbs
are **tsadaq** and **rasha**, the courtroom pair.

Both are miscarriages, and they run in opposite directions. Proverbs says the LORD hates
them equally.

The Hebrew for *both alike* is *even both of them* — the phrase exists to close off any
ranking.

## The same words in the law

*The one **God declares guilty** repays his neighbour double* — **rasha** again. See the
note on Exodus 22:7–9.

And Deuteronomy: *the judges will decide, **clearing the innocent and condemning the
guilty**.* See the note on Deuteronomy 25:1–3.

The law uses the two verbs the right way round. This proverb names what it looks like
when they are reversed.

## The fool with money

*Why should a fool **hold money to buy wisdom**, when **he has no heart to learn**?*

The Hebrew is *a price in the hand of a fool*.

He has the means and not the appetite. Proverbs treats the second as the binding
constraint.

## The friend

*A friend **loves at all times**, and **a brother is born for the hard days**.*

The Hebrew is *a brother is born for adversity*.

Two clauses that are usually read as a contrast and can be read as a pair: constancy in
one, and a relation that exists for the bad stretch.

## Shaking hands

*A man with no sense **shakes hands on a deal** and **takes on his neighbor's debt**.*

Proverbs returns to surety repeatedly and never approves of it — *he who is surety for a
stranger will suffer*.

The one exception in Scripture is Judah offering himself for Benjamin, and Paul offering
to cover Onesimus's debt: *if he owes you anything, put that on my account*.

## The quarrel

*Whoever **loves a quarrel loves sin**. Whoever **builds a high gate invites ruin**.*

The Hebrew for the second is *he who exalts his gate seeks destruction*.

A gate higher than the wall around it is display rather than defence.

## The crooked heart

*A crooked heart **never stumbles onto good**, and a twisted tongue **falls headlong into
trouble**.*

The Hebrew for *crooked* is **iqqesh** — twisted, perverse.

Nobody arrives at good by accident from there. Proverbs makes it a statement about
direction.

## The last pair

*To father a fool brings only sorrow. **The parent of a fool has no joy**.*

The book says the reverse as often: *a wise son makes a glad father*.

## Where the first verse lands in the New Testament

*But to him who does not work but believes on Him **who justifies the ungodly**, his faith
is accounted for righteousness.*

Paul writes that in Romans 4, using the same construction Proverbs condemns — and the
whole of Romans 3 is his account of how it is not a miscarriage: *that He might be **just
and the justifier** of the one who has faith in Jesus.* See the note on
Romans 3:23–24.

> **What the opening verse rules out.** Both directions. Calling a guilty man innocent
> and calling an innocent man guilty are named in the same line and given one verdict.
