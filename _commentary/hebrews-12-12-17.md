This passage gets quoted in two directions at once. One crowd reads *chase
holiness; without it nobody is going to see the Lord* and hears a threshold to
clear. Another reads *nothing bitter takes root* and hears a warning about
holding grudges. Both are picking up something real, and both are reading past
the sentence that ties them together.

## Read the order of the sentence

The warning is one sentence with two halves, and the halves are joined:

- **Watch that nobody misses out on God's grace**
- **and that nothing bitter takes root**

The Greek behind *misses out* is a word for falling short of something, coming
up behind it, being left out — the same word Paul uses in *all have sinned and
fall short*. And the bitterness grows out of that. Not out of what somebody did
to you. Out of somebody missing the grace.

That is the whole diagnosis. Bitterness is what grows in a person who is trying
to get by on something other than grace. It is the fruit, not the root.

## The root is a quotation

The phrase is not the writer's own image. He is reaching back to Deuteronomy,
where Moses warns against a root among the people producing poison — someone
who hears the covenant and decides privately he will be fine on his own terms.
That is the person in the picture. Not a wounded person. A self-sufficient one.

Which is why the next line lands where it does: *nobody sexually immoral or
godless like Esau*. Esau is not introduced as an example of lust. He is
introduced as a man who traded what was already his for something immediate,
because he did not value it. Missing the grace comes first. The visible failure
comes after.

## The other half: what "holiness" is doing here

*Chase peace with everybody, and chase holiness; without it nobody is going to
see the Lord.* Read alone, that reads like a bar to clear.

But look where it sits. Seven lines earlier, the same writer says God disciplines
us *so that we come to share His holiness* — His, given, shared. The word is the
same one. Holiness here is not a standard the reader manufactures and presents;
it is something being handed to them, that they are told to go after.

The image in the surrounding verses is medical, not judicial: hands gone limp,
knees giving way, a limb about to be thrown out of joint — *healed instead*. The
writer is treating an injury, not setting an entrance exam.

## What it looks like in practice

The passage never says "watch that nobody sins." It says watch that nobody
**misses the grace**, because that is where the trouble starts and everything
else follows. It puts the attention one step upstream of where we usually put
it.

> **On Esau and "no room for repentance."** This line frightens people badly. In
> the Greek the sentence is about *Isaac's* decision, not God's: Esau found no
> way to reverse the blessing his father had already spoken over Jacob, though he
> begged for it with tears. It is a story about a transaction that could not be
> undone, not a statement that a person can want God and be turned away.
