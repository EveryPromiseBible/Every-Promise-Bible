Four short sayings, and Luke sets them between two parables about money.

## Who is listening

*The Pharisees were listening to all this, and **because they loved money they sneered at
Him**.*

The Greek is **exemukterizon** — literally *turned up the nose*. It occurs twice in the
New Testament, here and at the cross.

They have just heard the parable of the unjust steward and *you cannot serve God and
mammon*. See the note on Luke 16:9–13.

## The charge

*"You are the ones who **make yourselves look righteous in front of other people**, but
**God knows what is inside you**."*

The Greek is *you justify yourselves before men*.

Not that they were failing to keep the standard. That the audience was wrong.

## The reversal

*"**What men hold up as impressive is detestable in the sight of God**."*

The Greek is **bdelugma** — abomination. It is the word used in Daniel of the abomination
of desolation, and in Leviticus of the things that defile.

## The boundary

*"**The law and the prophets ran up to the time of John**."*

The same statement Matthew records: *all the Prophets and the Law spoke their prophecies
right up to John.* See the note on Matthew 11:12–15.

Both Gospels put John at the join.

## What has been happening since

*"**Since then the good news of the kingdom of God has been announced**, and **everyone is
pushing to get in**."*

The Greek is **biazetai** — the same force-word as Matthew's version.

## The clause immediately after

*"But it would be **easier for heaven and earth to disappear** than for **the smallest
stroke of a letter in the law to fail**."*

The Greek is **keraia** — a horn, the little serif that distinguishes one Hebrew letter
from another.

He says the era has ended and in the next breath says not one pen-stroke will fail.

Both stand. He says the same in Matthew 5: *do not think that I came to destroy the Law
or the Prophets. I did not come to destroy but **to fulfil**.*

Nothing drops off. It is carried to its end.

## The example

*"Anyone who **divorces his wife and marries another woman commits adultery**."*

An abrupt landing after a statement about eras.

It is put there as the demonstration: the Pharisees had built a permissive divorce
practice out of a clause in Deuteronomy, and He applies the standard they claimed to hold
to the thing they had loosened.

They had just been told they justify themselves before men. This is what that looked like
in practice.

## What Luke does with the arrangement

Parable about money — sneering — this — and then straight into the rich man and Lazarus,
which is about a man in fine linen who fared sumptuously every day.

Luke frames the sayings with two stories about wealth, and the men sneering are described
as lovers of money.

## The last line of the next parable

*"**If they do not hear Moses and the prophets, neither will they be persuaded though one
rise from the dead**."*

Which is where the chapter ends.

> **The two halves nobody can drop.** The law and the prophets ran up to John, and not
> one serif will fail. He says both in consecutive sentences.
