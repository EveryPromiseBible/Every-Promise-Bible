The last chapter of Isaiah, and it opens with an impossible delivery.

## The order of events

*Before she was in labor, **she gave birth**. Before the pains came upon her, **she
delivered a son**.*

Birth without labour. The one part of the process nobody skips.

Everything in the sentence happens in the wrong order on purpose.

## The questions

*Who has **ever heard** of such a thing? Who has **ever seen** the like?*

Two, and both are rhetorical. Nobody has.

## The one that got answered

*Can **a land be born in a single day**? Can **a nation be brought forth all at once**?*

On 14 May 1948 the British Mandate ended and Israel declared statehood, and a nation that
had not existed for nineteen centuries existed by the end of the afternoon.

Isaiah asks it as an absurdity. History filed a reply.

## The name

*Yet no sooner was **Zion** in labor than she gave birth to her children.*

Zion is the mother in the sentence. The city, personified, delivering the people who live
in her.

Isaiah has used the image before — *sing, O barren, you who have not borne… for more are
the children of the desolate*. See the note on Isaiah 54:1–3.

## The two questions God asks

*Shall I bring to the **moment of birth and not deliver**? says the LORD.*

*Shall I, **who deliver, close the womb**? says your God.*

A midwife who walks out at the last minute, and a midwife who shuts what she came to open.

Both are unthinkable, which is the point. He is arguing from His own character rather than
from Israel's.

## The two titles

*Says **the LORD*** — and then *says **your God***.

The second is personal. He changes how He signs the sentence between the two questions.

## What comes immediately after

*Rejoice with Jerusalem, and be glad with her, all you who love her… **that you may feed
and be satisfied** with the consolation of her bosom.*

And then: *as one whom his mother comforts, so I will comfort you; and **you shall be
comforted in Jerusalem**.*

The nursing image runs on for four more verses.

## Where the chapter ends

*"For as **the new heavens and the new earth** which I will make shall remain before Me,"
says the LORD, "**so shall your descendants and your name remain**."*

See the note on Isaiah 65:21–23.

Sixty-six chapters that opened with *hear, O heavens* close with new ones.

## The other use of the image

Paul: *the whole creation groans and labours with birth pangs together until now.* And
Jesus, on the wars and earthquakes: *all these are the beginning of sorrows* — the Greek is
**odin**, birth pains. See the note on Matthew 24:4–8.

The New Testament keeps the metaphor and keeps the labour. Isaiah 66 is the one place the
labour is skipped.

> **The question that got an answer.** *Can a nation be brought forth all at once?* Isaiah
> asked it to make a point about the impossible, and a certificate was signed in Tel Aviv
> on a Friday afternoon.
