Isaiah asks four questions and then says something about the shape of the world.

## The line

*He sits enthroned above **the circle of the earth**.*

The Hebrew is **chug** — a circle, a sphere, a compass, the shape drawn by
swinging a fixed radius.

Isaiah wrote it around 700 BC. Every culture around him thought the earth was
flat, and it stayed that way in the popular mind for two thousand years.

## The four questions

*Do you not know? Have you not heard? Was it not told to you from the beginning?
Have you not understood since the earth was founded?*

He asks them the way you ask someone who ought to know already.

## The grasshoppers

*And its people are **like grasshoppers**.*

The view described is from above the circle, looking down, and from there the
people are insects.

That is a change of altitude in the middle of a sentence.

## The tent

*He stretches out the heavens **like a curtain**, spreads them **like a tent** to
live in.*

The verb is used elsewhere in Isaiah of the heavens being stretched out, and it is
the word for pitching a tent — pulling fabric taut.

## The princes

*He reduces princes to nothing… **No sooner are they planted, no sooner are they
sown, no sooner do they take root** — than He breathes on them and they wither.*

Three clauses to say how briefly. The whole career of a ruler is compressed into
the time between sowing and the first root.

He *breathes* on them. It takes nothing.

## What the chapter is for

Isaiah 40 opens with *comfort, comfort My people*, and this passage is inside that
comfort.

The point of the circle, the tent and the grasshoppers is not astronomy. It is that
the people frightening you are seed that has not rooted yet, and the One above the
circle knows your name.

The chapter's last lines are the famous ones: *those who wait on the LORD shall
renew their strength; they shall mount up with wings like eagles.* See the note on
Isaiah 40:29–31.

## The breath that withers, and the breath that does not

*He **breathes on them and they wither**.* Three clauses to say how briefly a ruler lasts, and
then a breath.

*And when He had said this, **He breathed on them, and said to them, "Receive the Holy Spirit."***

**John** 20, in a locked room, to men who had run away. The Greek is **enephysesen** — the verb
the Greek Old Testament uses in Genesis 2 when God breathes into the man's nostrils.

Isaiah's breath ends careers. The same breath in an upper room starts one.

## Above the circle, and inside it

*He sits enthroned above the circle of the earth… and its people are like grasshoppers.*

*And **the Word became flesh and dwelt among us**.*

**John**'s verb is **eskenosen** — pitched His tent, from the same noun Isaiah uses two lines
later of the heavens spread *like a tent to live in*. The One who stretches out the tent moved
into one.

*For **He is before all things, and in Him all things consist**.*

## What is not shaken

*"You, LORD, in the beginning laid the foundation of the earth… **They will perish, but You
remain**… like a cloak You will fold them up, and they will be changed. But **You are the same,
and Your years will not fail**."*

**Hebrews** 1 applies it to the Son, in a chapter arguing that He is not an angel.

> **Where else the same word turns up.** Proverbs 8, the passage about Wisdom
> present at creation: *when He drew a **circle** on the face of the deep, I was
> there.* And Job says the same thing in his own words — *He drew a circle on the
> surface of the waters.* See the note on Job 26:5–10.
