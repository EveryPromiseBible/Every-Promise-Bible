The writer's argument turns on a question he expects the reader to answer for
themselves.

## The image

*The law **threw a shadow** of the good that was coming; **it never showed the thing
itself**.*

The Greek is **skia** (shadow) against **eikon** — the image, the actual form.

A shadow is not a lie. It is cast by something real, has the shape of it, and proves it
is there.

It also cannot be used. Nobody sits in the shade of a shadow.

## What the law was a shadow of

*The good that was coming.* Paul says the same in Colossians about food, drink, feasts
and sabbaths: *these are a shadow of things to come, **but the substance is of Christ**.*

## The repetition

*So **year after year**, with **the same sacrifices offered without end**.*

The Day of Atonement, every year, the same ritual. And the daily offerings, morning and
evening, for centuries.

## What it could not do

*It could **never bring the people who came to worship all the way to completion**.*

The Greek is **teleiosai** — to bring to the end, to perfect, to finish.

The problem named is not that the sacrifices were fraudulent. It is that they never
arrived anywhere.

## The question

*If it could, **would not the offerings have stopped?**"*

The writer puts the test in the reader's hands. A treatment that works is discontinued.

The temple was still running when this letter was written, and the smoke went up every
day, which was the visible proof of his point.

## The consequence

*Once the worshipers had been cleansed, **they would not have gone on carrying any sense
of their sins**.*

The Greek is **suneidesin hamartion** — a consciousness of sins.

That is what the writer says the arrangement failed to remove. Not the guilt in the
abstract. The awareness of it in the worshipper.

## What the sacrifices did instead

The next verse: *but in those sacrifices **there is a reminder of sins every year**.*

The Greek is **anamnesis** — the same word Jesus uses of the bread: *do this in
remembrance of Me.* See the note on Luke 22:19–20.

One annual reminder of sins; one ongoing remembrance of Him.

## The blunt statement

*For it is **not possible that the blood of bulls and goats could take away sins**.*

The writer says it outright, about a system God Himself commanded.

## Where the argument goes

Into Psalm 40 in the mouth of Christ — *sacrifices and offerings You did not want, but a
body You prepared for Me.* See the notes on Hebrews 10:5–7 and Hebrews 10:8–9.

Then the priests who stand daily and the one who sat down. See the note on
Hebrews 10:11–13.

Then Jeremiah: *their sins and their lawless deeds I will remember no more.* See the note
on Hebrews 10:15–17.

> **The test he offers.** If it worked, it would have stopped. He does not argue the
> point further — he asks the question and moves on to what did stop.
