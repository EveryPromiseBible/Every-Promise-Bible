The dinner that follows a tax collector's call, and the objection is about seating.

## Whose house

*Jesus was at the table **in Levi's house**.*

Levi is Matthew. Mark and Luke use the Levi name; the man's own Gospel uses Matthew and
adds *the tax collector* to it in the list of the twelve.

He had been sitting at the tax booth in Capernaum when Jesus walked past and said *follow
Me*.

## Who was there

*A whole crowd of **tax collectors and people with bad reputations** sat down and ate with
Him and His disciples.*

Tax collectors worked for Rome, could set their own margin, and were classed with thieves.
A Jew who took the job was outside respectable society permanently.

## The clause Mark adds

*For **there were many of them who followed Him**.*

Not a few curious guests. Mark says there was a crowd of them, and that they were already
following.

## The objection

*"**Why does He eat with people like that?**"*

The Greek is *why does He eat and drink with tax collectors and sinners*.

They ask the disciples, not Him.

## What eating meant

A shared table in that world was not a neutral act. It signified acceptance, and it made
you ritually vulnerable to whatever the other party carried.

The complaint is not that He talks to them. It is that He is reclining at a meal with
them.

The same charge is made against Him repeatedly: *this Man receives sinners and eats with
them* — which is what provokes the three parables of Luke 15. See the note on
Luke 15:11–16.

## The answer

*"It is **not the healthy who go looking for a doctor. It is the sick**."*

A proverb, and one that circulated in the ancient world in various forms.

He does not defend the company. He explains the profession.

## The second half

*"I did not come to call **the ones who think they are already righteous**. I came to call
**sinners**."*

Matthew's version adds a quotation: *go and learn what this means: "I desire mercy and not
sacrifice."* — from Hosea.

## Where the call goes

*To bring them **to a whole new way of thinking**.*

The Greek is **metanoia** — a change of mind.

## What Mark records around it

Chapter 2 is a run of four confrontations: the paralytic through the roof and the
forgiveness of sins, this dinner, a question about fasting, and the disciples picking grain
on the sabbath. See the note on Mark 2:6–12.

Four scenes, four objections, and the opposition is assembled by the end of chapter 3:
*then the Pharisees went out and immediately plotted with the Herodians against Him, how
they might destroy Him.*

## The order of the two clauses

The doctor line explains why He is there. The second line says who He came for.

Neither is an apology.

> **What the objection was about.** Not the teaching. A meal — and Mark notes in passing
> that there was a crowd of such people already following Him.
