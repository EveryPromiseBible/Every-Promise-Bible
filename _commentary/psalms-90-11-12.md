The turn in Moses' psalm. Everything before it is description; this is where it
becomes a request.

## The request

*Teach us to count our days.* Not *let us live longer*, and not *explain why*.
Teach us to reckon them — the verb is arithmetical, the ordinary word for
counting a number of things.

The premise is that a person does not naturally do this. Days are spent as
though the supply were unlimited, and the psalm asks for the ability to hold the
real figure in mind.

## What it is for

*So that we may get a heart of wisdom.* The counting is not the goal. It is the
instrument.

The Hebrew is literally *bring in* a heart of wisdom — like a harvest gathered
into a barn. Numbering the days is how the crop gets in.

## Where it sits

It follows immediately on the verse about God's anger and the fear owed to Him,
and it precedes a series of requests that get steadily warmer: *satisfy us in the
morning with Your unfailing love… make us glad for as many days as You have
afflicted us… establish the work of our hands.*

The psalm moves from lament to petition, and this verse is the hinge. A man
watching a generation die in the desert asks first to be taught how to count.

> **Read with the verses above it.** The famous *seventy years, or eighty if we
> are strong* is four lines earlier, and it is description, not decree — see the
> note on Psalms 90:7–10. This request is what Moses does with that observation:
> not resignation, but a prayer to be taught how to spend what there is.
