Three sentences with the same shape, and each one puts something bigger on the scale.

## Where the chapter sits

In the middle of three chapters about spiritual gifts.

Chapter 12 lists them. Chapter 14 sorts out the disorder in the meetings. And between them,
without warning, this.

The last line of chapter 12 is the hinge: *and yet I show you a more excellent way*.

It is not a digression. It is the middle term of the argument.

## The first

*If I speak in **the languages of men, or even of angels**, and do not have love, I am **banging
metal, a cymbal clashing**.*

The Greek is **chalkos echon** — sounding brass. And **kumbalon alalazon** — a clanging cymbal.

Both are noise-makers used in pagan temple worship in that region. Loud, hollow, and requiring
no skill.

## The second

*If I can **prophesy, and understand every mystery and everything there is to know**, and if I
have **faith enough to move mountains**, and do not have love, **I am nothing at all**.*

Four things: prophecy, mysteries, knowledge, and mountain-moving faith.

Paul takes the highest gift on his own list and the largest promise Jesus made about faith,
and weighs them.

The Greek is **outhen eimi** — I am nothing.

The first sentence said what he *sounds* like. This one says what he *is*.

## The third

*If I **give away everything I own** to feed the poor, and **hand over my body to be burned**,
and do not have love, **it does me no good whatever**.*

Total divestment and martyrdom.

The Greek is **ouden ophelymai** — I am profited nothing.

## The three verdicts

Noise. Nothing. No profit.

The scale goes: what I sound like, what I am, what I gain. Each sentence takes something away
at a deeper level.

## What is not being said

Not that the gifts are worthless. Paul spends two chapters regulating them and says *I wish
you all spoke with tongues*, and *desire earnestly the best gifts*.

He is not against any item on the list. He is saying that each of them, without one thing, is
a zero.

## The word

**Agape** — the word the New Testament writers took over and filled.

It was a rare and colourless word in classical Greek. **Eros** and **philia** were the ones
with the weight, and both were about response to something attractive.

## What he says it is

The next paragraph, and every clause is a verb rather than a feeling:

*Love **suffers long** and **is kind**; love **does not envy**; love **does not parade itself**,
is not puffed up; does not behave rudely, **does not seek its own**, is not provoked, **thinks
no evil**; does not rejoice in iniquity, but rejoices in the truth.*

*Bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things.*

## The permanence

*Love never fails. But whether there are prophecies, they will fail; whether tongues, they will
cease; whether knowledge, it will vanish away.*

The three things he weighed in verses 1 and 2, listed as the things with an expiry date.

## The last line

*And now abide faith, hope, love, these three; but **the greatest of these is love**.*

## Who it was written to

The most gifted congregation Paul dealt with — *you come short in no gift* — and the most
disorderly. See the note on 1 Corinthians 1:4–9.

Factions, lawsuits, drunkenness at the table, chaos in the meetings.

He does not tell them to want fewer gifts.

> **What the three verdicts measure.** Sound, being, and profit. Tongues without it are noise,
> prophecy and mountain-moving faith without it make the man nothing, and giving everything
> away without it gains him nothing.
