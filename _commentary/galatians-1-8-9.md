Paul says the hardest thing in his letters, and then says it a second time in case it read
as temper.

## Where it comes

Two verses after the opening. He has skipped the thanksgiving entirely — the only letter
of his that does — and gone straight to *I am astonished at how quickly you are walking
away*. See the note on Galatians 1:6–7.

## The first party named

*But even if **we ourselves**.*

He starts with himself and his own team.

The rule he is about to state binds the man stating it.

## The second

*Or **an angel out of heaven**.*

The highest possible authority short of God.

Paul had received his gospel *by the revelation of Jesus Christ* — he says so in the next
paragraph — and he still puts an angel under the rule.

Later in the same letter: *Satan himself transforms himself into an angel of light.*

## The condition

*Were to announce to you **any good news other than the good news we announced to you**.*

The Greek is **par' ho** — alongside, contrary to, beside what we preached.

Not *the opposite of*. Beside it.

The Galatian teachers were not denying Christ. They were adding circumcision.

## The verdict

*Let him be **accursed**.*

The Greek is **anathema** — devoted to destruction, handed over.

It is the word the Greek Old Testament uses for what was under the ban at Jericho.

## The repetition

*I have said it before and **I say it now again**.*

He says the whole thing twice, and the second time he changes one word: *if anyone
announces to you any good news other than **the one you received**.*

First time: what we preached. Second time: what you received.

He shifts the standard from the messenger to the message as it landed.

## Why say it twice

Because it reads as anger the first time. Repeating it makes it a considered position
rather than an outburst.

He does the same thing at the end of the letter with the summary: *for in Christ Jesus
neither circumcision nor uncircumcision avails anything, but a new creation.*

## What he says immediately after

*For do I now persuade men, or God? Or **do I seek to please men?** For if I still pleased
men, I would not be a bondservant of Christ.*

That is the answer to the obvious reaction. He is aware of how it sounds.

## What was at stake

The letter's own summary of the alternative: *you have become estranged from Christ, you who
attempt to be justified by law; you have fallen from grace.* See the note on
Galatians 5:2–4.

## The letter's tone

*O foolish Galatians! Who has bewitched you?* And: *I am afraid for you, lest I have
laboured for you in vain.* And: *I could wish to be present with you now and to change my
tone.*

And in the middle of it, the paragraph about arriving ill and being welcomed like an angel
of God. See the note on Galatians 4:12–16.

> **Who the rule binds first.** Himself. Paul puts his own name at the head of the
> sentence, then an angel — and then says the whole thing again so nobody can put it down
> to heat.
