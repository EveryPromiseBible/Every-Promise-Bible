This is the passage people are quietly reaching for when they ask whether they
can be lost. It answers, and the way it answers is worth seeing in detail,
because Jesus stacks the assurance four times over in three sentences.

## The four layers

- **I give them life that lasts forever** — given, not lent
- **they will never be lost** — the Greek uses a double negative, the strongest
  form of denial the language has: not ever, under any circumstance
- **no one will ever tear them out of My hand**
- **no one is able to tear them out of My Father's hand**

Then, having named two hands, He closes with *I and the Father are one*.

The security is not asserted once and left. It is stated, strengthened,
transferred to a second pair of hands, and then sealed by saying the two are
one.

## What "no one" covers

The word is unrestricted. It is not *no enemy* or *no other person*. The grammar
does not carve out an exception for the sheep itself, and it would be strange to
read one in, since the point of the sentence is the strength of the hand rather
than the grip of the sheep.

And notice the word Jesus uses for what cannot happen: *tear out*, snatch,
seize by force. It is the language of a predator — the same word John uses
earlier in the chapter for the wolf.

---

## Read the first line again

*My sheep listen to My voice. I know them, and they follow Me.*

Three verbs. Only one of them is the sheep's, and it is *listen* — and even that
is described as a recognition rather than an achievement. Sheep are not clever
animals. Their whole competence in this picture is knowing which voice is
theirs.

The chapter has already said why they can: the shepherd calls them by name and
goes ahead of them. Recognition is possible because He speaks first.

## Where the passage is going

*I and the Father are one* is not an aside tacked onto a comfort. It is the
conclusion of the argument, and the people listening understood it perfectly —
the next verse has them picking up stones.

Which means the assurance and the claim about His identity are the same
statement. Whether you can be held depends on who is holding you, and Jesus
answers that question by saying whose hand it is.

> **On the sheep who wander.** Nothing here says a sheep never strays; the
> chapter next door in Luke is about one that does. What this passage rules out
> is the sheep being *taken*, and it rules it out with the strongest negative
> Greek can build. Straying and being snatched are not the same thing, and only
> one of them is addressed here.
