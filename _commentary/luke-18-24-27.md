A rich man has just walked away sad, and Jesus watches him go before He says
anything.

## What He saw

*Jesus saw the sorrow settle over him.*

The comment that follows is not aimed at the man's back as a rebuke. Luke records
Jesus watching the grief land, and Mark's account adds that He had looked at him
and loved him.

## The camel and the needle

*It is easier for a camel to squeeze through the eye of a needle than for a rich
man to get into the kingdom of God.*

Attempts have been made to soften this — a small gate in Jerusalem called the
Needle's Eye, or a rope rather than a camel. There is no evidence for the gate,
and the rope theory rests on a variant reading.

The image is meant to be impossible. That is why the disciples react the way they
do.

## Their question

*Then who can possibly be saved?*

It is the right response, and it tells you what they had assumed. Wealth was read
as evidence of God's blessing; if the obviously blessed cannot get in, nobody
can.

Jesus does not correct the logic. He accepts it.

## The answer

*What is impossible for people is not impossible for God.*

He does not say it is difficult, or that rich people need to try harder. He moves
the whole question out of the category of human capacity.

Which means the answer covers the disciples too — the ones who had just pointed
out that they left everything.

> **Who it turns out to be possible for.** Two chapters later Luke gives us
> Zacchaeus, a rich tax collector, up a tree, and Jesus inviting Himself to
> dinner. Luke tells the impossible saying and then tells the story of it
> happening.
