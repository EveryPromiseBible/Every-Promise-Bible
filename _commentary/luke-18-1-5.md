Luke states the point of the parable before telling it, which he rarely does.

## The stated purpose

*Then Jesus told them a story to show them that **they should keep praying and never give up**.*

The Greek is **pros to dein pantote proseuchesthai autous kai mē enkakein** — that they ought always
to pray and not to lose heart.

**Enkakeō** — to become weary, to turn coward, to give in to evil circumstances.

## Why he says it up front

The parable's logic is easy to invert. Read carelessly, it makes God the reluctant judge.

Luke closes that reading before it can start.

## The judge

*"There was a judge who had **no fear of God** and **no regard for anybody at all**."*

The Greek is **ton Theon mē phoboumenos kai anthrōpon mē entrepomenos**.

The two-part description was a stock phrase for a corrupt official. Josephus uses something like it.

## What that leaves

Neither of the two motives that make a judge do his job.

He cannot be shamed and he cannot be frightened.

## The widow

*"And in that same town there was **a widow who kept coming to him**."*

## What she has

No husband, no advocate, and no bribe.

The law's standing concern: *you shall not afflict any widow or fatherless child*. And the prophets'
recurring charge against judges: *they do not defend the fatherless, nor does the cause of the widow
come before them*.

## Her one asset

Persistence. It is the only thing in the story on her side.

## What she says

*"**Give me justice against the man who is wronging me**."*

The Greek is **ekdikēson me** — vindicate me, do me justice.

One sentence, repeated. She does not vary the request.

## The delay

*"**For a long while he refused**."*

## The turn

*"But finally he said to himself, '**I do not fear God and I do not care what people think**.'"*

He quotes the narrator's description of himself, accurately.

## The reason he gives in

*"But **this widow will not leave me alone**. I will see that she gets her justice, or **she will keep
coming back until she has worn me out completely**."*

The Greek is **hupōpiazē me** — literally, *lest she give me a black eye*, or wear me down.

A boxing word. **Hupōpion** — the area under the eye.

## What that is

Not compassion. Self-defence.

## The argument of the parable

Contrast, not comparison.

If a man with no fear of God and no shame will do justice to get some peace, the reasoning runs,
what about a Father who is neither corrupt nor unwilling?

## The application

*"And **will God not bring justice to His own chosen people, who cry out to Him day and night**? Will
He keep putting them off?"* See the note on Luke 18:6–8.

*"I tell you, He will see that they get justice, and **He will not be slow about it**."*

## The sting at the end

*"But when the Son of Man comes, **will He find faith on the earth**?"*

The parable about praying ends with a question about whether anyone will still be doing it.

## The companion parable

*"Suppose one of you has a friend, and you go to him **in the middle of the night**."* See the note on
Luke 11:5–8.

Same structure, same argument, same author. Luke is the only evangelist who records either.

> **The warning label.** Luke gives the moral before the story — *always pray and do not lose heart*
> — because without it the judge would be mistaken for God.
