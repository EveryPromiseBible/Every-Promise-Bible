Paul argues a right at length and then, in the same chapter, declines to use it.

## The three questions

*Who ever serves as a **soldier at his own expense**? Who **plants a vineyard** and
does not eat its fruit? Who **tends a flock** and does not drink of the milk?*

Three trades, three self-evident answers. Nobody funds their own army service,
plants a vineyard for someone else's grapes, or shepherds without milk.

The principle is so ordinary that Paul does not argue it — he just asks.

## Then the law

*Am I saying these things **only as a man would**? **Does the law not say the
same**?*

He will not rest the case on common sense. He goes to Moses.

*"**Do not muzzle an ox while it treads out the grain.**"*

An ox walking in circles over the threshing floor, with grain under its feet all
day, and the law forbids tying its mouth shut.

## The question he asks about it

*Is it **oxen that God is concerned about**, or does He say it entirely for our
sake?*

A striking piece of interpretation. Paul takes an agricultural regulation and says
the point of it was never really the ox.

*For our sake it was written, that **the one who ploughs ought to plough in hope**,
and the one who threshes in hope of sharing in the harvest.*

Whoever does the work should have grounds to expect a share of what the work
produces.

## What Paul concludes from it

*If we have sown spiritual things for you, is it a great thing if we reap your
material things?*

And a few verses later: *the Lord has commanded that those who preach the gospel
should live from the gospel.*

He establishes the right about as thoroughly as it can be established.

## And then

*But **I have used none of these things**… For it would be better for me to die than
that anyone should make my boasting void.*

He had the right and would not take it, and the reason he gives is *lest we hinder
the gospel of Christ.*

In Corinth he made tents. In Thessalonica he *worked night and day, that we might
not be a burden.*

## Both things at once

Paul holds two positions without embarrassment: that ministers ought to be
supported, and that he personally would not be.

He argues the first for other people's sake and declines it for the gospel's.

Timothy gets the same principle as instruction: *the elders who lead well should be
counted worthy of a double portion of honour… the worker has earned his pay.* See
the note on 1 Timothy 5:17–18.

> **What he says he is doing instead.** *What is my reward then? That when I preach
> the gospel, I may present the gospel of Christ **without charge**.* The pay he
> wanted was the pleasure of not charging.
