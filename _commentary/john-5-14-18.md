The sentence that made them want to kill Him is about the Sabbath, and then it turns out
not to be.

## Where He finds him

*Later Jesus **found him in the temple**.*

The man had been at the pool by the Sheep Gate, just outside. It is the first place he can
go now that he can walk — the courts a paralysed man could not reach.

Jesus *found* him. The Greek is **heuriskei**, and it is the word John uses for deliberate
searching: *He found Philip*, *he first found his own brother Simon*.

## What He says

*"**Look — you are well now**."*

The Greek is **ide hugies gegonas** — a perfect: you have become whole and stand whole.

## The warning

*"**Do not go back to your sinning**, or something worse may come to you."*

The Greek is **meketi hamartane** — sin no more.

Jesus says the same to the woman in chapter 8 — *neither do I condemn you; go and sin no
more*. In both cases the sentence comes after the verdict, not before it.

And He explicitly refuses the inference elsewhere: of the man born blind, *neither this man
nor his parents sinned*. See the note on John 9:1–5.

## What the man does

*The man went off and **told the Jewish leaders that it was Jesus** who had made him well.*

He had been asked *who told you to carry it* and could not answer. See the note on
John 5:10–13.

Now he can, and he goes back and tells them.

## The consequence

*And that is why they began to **hound Jesus and to look for a way to kill Him**: because He
did such things **on the Sabbath**.*

The Greek is **edioken** — an imperfect: they kept on persecuting. It starts here and does
not stop.

## The answer

*"**My Father is working right up to this moment, and I am working too**."*

The Greek is **heos arti ergazetai** — He is working until now.

God rested on the seventh day, and the rabbis knew perfectly well that He had not stopped
sustaining the world since. Children are born on Sabbaths and men die on them.

Jesus takes the one activity nobody denied God does on the Sabbath and puts Himself inside
it.

## What they heard

*At that they wanted to kill Him **all the more**, because in their eyes He was **not only
breaking the Sabbath**, He was **calling God His own Father, making Himself equal with
God**.*

The Greek is **patera idion** — His own Father, in a private sense.

They understood Him exactly. Nothing in the next forty verses corrects them.

## What He says immediately after

*"Most assuredly, I say to you, **the Son can do nothing of Himself, but what He sees the
Father do**; for whatever He does, the Son also does in like manner."*

And: *"as the Father raises the dead and gives life to them, even so the Son gives life to
whom He will."*

And: *"that all should **honour the Son just as they honour the Father**."*

## The four witnesses

The rest of the chapter lists them: John the Baptist, the works themselves, the Father, and
the Scriptures.

*"You search the Scriptures, for in them you think you have eternal life; and **these are
they which testify of Me**."*

## The Sabbath argument

He makes it again over the woman bent for eighteen years: *does not each one of you on the
Sabbath loose his ox from the stall and lead it away to water it?* See the note on
Luke 13:10–17.

And *the Sabbath was made for man, and not man for the Sabbath*.

> **What the charge became.** It started as carrying a mat. By the end of the paragraph the
> accusation is that He called God His own Father — and John reports it as the reason, not
> as a misunderstanding.
