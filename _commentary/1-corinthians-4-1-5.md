Paul has just spent three chapters dismantling a personality contest. Here he tells them what he
actually is, and then does something more surprising: he tells them what he does with their opinion
of him.

## Under-rowers

*Let people think of us in this way: as **servants of Christ**.*

The word is **hyperetes** — literally an under-rower, a man on the lower bank of oars in a ship. He
cannot see where the vessel is going. He is not consulted about the course. He pulls when the beat
sounds, and the destination is somebody else's business.

Beside it Paul puts **oikonomos**, a steward — the household manager who spends money that is not
his, out of a store he did not fill.

Neither word has any glory in it, and neither has any anxiety in it either.

## The one thing asked of a steward

*And what is looked for in stewards is that each one be found **faithful**.*

Not fruitful. Not impressive. Not successful. **Pistos** — trustworthy with what he was handed.

A steward is never asked to produce the goods. He is asked not to be false with goods that arrived
in his care. That is the whole job description, and it is one every believer can meet, because the
supply is not his responsibility.

## A clear conscience does not justify

*My conscience brings nothing against me, yet that is not what clears me.*

This is one of the most under-read sentences Paul ever wrote. The verb is **dedikaiomai** — the
perfect passive of **dikaioo**, the great word of Romans. *I am not by this justified.*

Paul had a clean conscience, and he refuses to stand on it. He knew what everyone discovers
eventually: a conscience is a witness, not a judge, and it can be quiet for the wrong reasons.

So he will not let his own good feeling about himself carry any weight — and he will not let their
bad feeling about him carry any either. The verb he uses of their examining, **anakrino**, is a
courtroom word for the pre-trial hearing. He calls it *a very small thing*.

A man who has stopped needing his own verdict has stopped needing yours.

## Why he can afford to say that

He can only talk this way because the case has already been heard. *There is therefore now no
condemnation to those who are in Christ Jesus.* The judgment Paul is waiting for is not the one
that decides whether he is accepted; that was settled in the blood covenant, and the sentence was
served by somebody else.

What is left is the assessment of a steward's work by a Master who is glad to have him.

> **How the paragraph ends.** *And then each one will have **his praise** from God.* Not his
> verdict. Not his reckoning. **Epainos** — praise. Paul expects the day the hidden things are
> brought to light to end with commendation for each one, and he says *each*. That is what a man
> believes about the day of examination when he already knows what Jesus did about the judgment.
