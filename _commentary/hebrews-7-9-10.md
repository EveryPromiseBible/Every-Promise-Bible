The writer makes an argument about a man who was not born yet, and he knows how it
sounds.

## "You could even put it this way"

He flags it himself — *so to speak*, in most translations. He is aware he is
pressing something.

And then he presses it.

## The claim

*Levi, **who collects the tenth, paid it himself** — paid it through Abraham. **He
was still inside his forefather's body** when Melchizedek came out to meet him.*

Levi is Abraham's great-grandson. He is four generations away and centuries from
existing.

The writer says he was there, in Abraham, and that what Abraham did that afternoon
counted for him.

## What the argument is for

The whole of Hebrews 7 is establishing that Melchizedek's priesthood outranks
Levi's, and therefore that Christ's does — *You are a priest forever according to
the order of Melchizedek.*

The logic: the lesser is blessed by the greater. Abraham gave a tenth to Melchizedek
and received a blessing from him. So Melchizedek is the greater — and Levi, still
in Abraham's body, was on the paying end of that transaction.

## The principle underneath it

A man's act reached forward into descendants who did not exist.

Paul uses the same structure for Adam — *by one man's disobedience many were made
sinners* — and for Christ: *by one Man's obedience many will be made righteous.*

What one man did, a whole line was counted in.

## Melchizedek

*King of Salem, priest of God Most High* — Genesis 14, and he appears in three
verses and vanishes.

He brings out **bread and wine** to Abraham coming back from the slaughter of the
kings, and blesses him. Then Abraham gives him a tenth of everything.

Hebrews notes what the record leaves out: no father, no mother, no genealogy, no
beginning of days, no end of life — *made like the Son of God, he remains a priest
continually.*

## Where the tithe stands in the argument

Abraham gave it before the law existed. Sinai was four hundred years off; there was
no commandment, no tabernacle and no Levitical system to fund.

He gave it to a priest-king who came out with bread and wine, and the writer of
Hebrews uses that afternoon to prove a priesthood that has no end.

> **What Abraham refused the same day.** The king of Sodom offered him the goods and
> he would not take a thread or a sandal-strap — *lest you should say, "I have made
> Abram rich."* He gave a tenth to the priest and turned down the king, on one
> evening.
