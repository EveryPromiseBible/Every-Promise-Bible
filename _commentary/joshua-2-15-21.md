A woman in a doomed city is given a set of conditions, and the conditions are
worth reading closely, because every one of them turns up again in how the Bible
talks about rescue.

## The three terms

1. **the cord in the window** — the same rope she let them down by, tied where it
   can be seen
2. **everyone must be inside her house** — *if anyone steps outside your doors
   into the street, their blood is on their own head*
3. **say nothing about the arrangement**

The safety is not spread across the city or attached to her personally wherever
she goes. It is located: this house, this mark, everyone in it.

## Where you have read this before

A household. A doorway. A red mark on the outside. Everyone who stays in is
safe, everyone who goes out is not — and the destruction passes through the city
regardless.

Exodus 12 is the same shape, and Rahab's night is the only other place in
scripture where the pattern appears whole. Whether she knew that is not the
point; the text is built so that the reader does.

## Rahab's own words, three verses earlier

*I know that the LORD has given you this land.* Everyone in Jericho had heard
the same reports about the Red Sea and the two Amorite kings, and the whole city
was melting with fear. She is not better informed than her neighbours.

She is the only one who acts on it.

> **What she was.** The text never softens her trade, and neither do the two New
> Testament writers who name her — Hebrews lists her among the faithful, James
> as an example of faith that did something. Matthew puts her in the genealogy of
> Jesus. Nobody in scripture is embarrassed about Rahab, which is itself worth
> noticing.
