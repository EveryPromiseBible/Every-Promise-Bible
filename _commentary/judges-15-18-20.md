Samson has just won a battle single-handed, and the next thing recorded is that
he nearly died of thirst.

## The complaint

*You have given Your servant this great victory. Must I now die of thirst and
fall into the hands of these uncircumcised men?*

Two things worth noticing. He credits the victory to God without hesitation —
which, for Samson, is not guaranteed. And then he complains, bluntly, about the
state he has been left in.

It is not a polite prayer. It is a man at the end of his strength saying so.

## The answer

*So God split open the hollow place at Lehi, and water came pouring out. Samson
drank, his strength returned, and he revived.*

No rebuke. No lesson about attitude. Water.

The Hebrew for *revived* is the word for a spirit returning — the same idea used
of someone coming round after a faint.

## The name

*That is why the spring is called En-hakkore, the Spring of the One Who Cried
Out — and it is there in Lehi to this day.*

The place is named after the cry, not the victory. Not after the thousand
Philistines or the jawbone, both of which the chapter has just described at
length. After a man shouting for help because he was thirsty.

> **Who Samson was.** This is not a model believer. His story is a catalogue of
> appetite and broken vows, and the book knows it. The passage is worth reading
> precisely because of that: God answers a complaint from a man in the middle of a
> badly lived life, and the memorial left behind is of the asking.
