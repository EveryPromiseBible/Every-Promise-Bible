The shortest Gospel starts without a birth, a genealogy, or a childhood.

## The opening

*Here begins **the good news of Jesus Christ, the Son of God**.*

The Greek is **arche tou euangeliou** — the beginning of the gospel.

**Euangelion** was not a religious word. In the Roman world it announced a military victory or an
imperial birthday — a proclamation from the emperor's court.

Mark uses it in the first line about somebody else.

## The title

**Iesou Christou huiou Theou** — of Jesus Christ, the Son of God.

Everything Mark's Gospel will spend sixteen chapters demonstrating is stated in the first
sentence.

## Where the phrase reappears

At the baptism, from heaven: *You are My beloved Son*.

At the transfiguration: *this is My beloved Son*.

And at the cross, from a Roman soldier: *truly this Man was the Son of God*. See the note on
Mark 15:37–39.

Mark's opening line is echoed at three points, and the last one comes from an executioner.

## No beginning at the beginning

Matthew starts with a genealogy. Luke starts with an annunciation. John starts before creation.

Mark starts with a grown man walking out of the desert.

There is no infancy narrative anywhere in this Gospel.

## The quotation

*It starts **the way the prophets said it would**.*

Mark attributes it to Isaiah, and then quotes two prophets — Malachi first, then Isaiah.

*"Look — **I am sending My messenger on ahead of You**, and he will get the road ready before You
come."*

That is Malachi 3: *behold, I send My messenger, and he will prepare the way before Me*.

## What Malachi's line does

In Malachi, the one whose road is prepared is God. *The Lord, whom you seek, will suddenly come
to His temple.*

Mark applies it to Jesus, and changes *before Me* to *before You*.

## The second

*"**A voice is calling out in the wilderness: Clear a road for the Lord. Make the way straight for
Him**."*

Isaiah 40, the chapter that opens *comfort, yes, comfort My people*. See the note on
Isaiah 40:1–2.

Written to exiles in Babylon about a road home across the desert.

## What a road-clearing meant

When a king travelled, work parties went ahead to level the route. The image is of an engineering
crew flattening ground.

## Who the voice turns out to be

*John came baptizing in the wilderness and preaching a baptism of repentance… **clothed with
camel's hair and with a leather belt around his waist**, and he ate locusts and wild honey.*

The description is Elijah's, word for word from 2 Kings: *a hairy man wearing a leather belt
around his waist*.

## Mark's pace

*And immediately* — **euthus** — appears more than forty times in sixteen chapters.

The Gospel moves. There are fewer discourses than in Matthew and more incidents, and the whole
thing can be read aloud in an hour and a half.

## What he asks in the middle

*"But who do you say that I am?" Peter answered and said to Him, "You are the Christ."*

The exact midpoint of the book, and it is the reader being asked the question the first verse
already answered. See the note on Mark 8:31–33.

## The end

*And they went out quickly and fled from the tomb, for they trembled and were amazed. **And they
said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid**.*

The oldest manuscripts end there, mid-fear, on a Greek conjunction.

A Gospel that opens *here begins* closes without a proper ending.

> **What Mark leaves out.** A birth, a genealogy, a childhood, and — in the oldest manuscripts —
> a conclusion. The first sentence states the claim and the rest is the evidence.
