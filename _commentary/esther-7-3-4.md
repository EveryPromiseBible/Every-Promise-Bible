Two banquets, an invitation held back for a day, and then the request.

## What she had been doing

The king had asked her twice what she wanted, and twice offered up to half his
kingdom. Both times she asked only that he and Haman come to dinner.

In between the two banquets: a sleepless night for the king, the royal records
read aloud, Mordecai's unrewarded loyalty discovered, and Haman paraded through
the streets leading the horse of the man he meant to hang.

None of that happens if she asks on the first night.

## How she asks

*If I have found favour in your eyes, my king, and if it pleases the king, let my
own life be given me as my petition, and my people as my request.*

She asks for her own life first. Until this moment the king does not know she is
Jewish — the decree he sealed includes her.

She puts herself inside the sentence before she mentions anybody else.

## The line about slavery

*Had we only been sold as slaves, men and women, I would have kept silent, for
such a trouble would not be worth disturbing the king.*

A deliberate piece of restraint. She tells a Persian king that she would not have
troubled him over slavery — which makes clear that what she is troubling him
about is worse.

## The verbs

*To be destroyed, to be killed, to be wiped away.* Three, and they are the exact
three from Haman's decree, quoted back.

She is not describing the danger in her own words. She is reading his own
paperwork to him.

> **The book that never names God.** Nothing miraculous happens in Esther. A
> queen waits a day, a king cannot sleep, a record is read at the right hour. Only
> from the far end does the shape become visible — which is the whole method of
> the book. See the note on Esther 2:15–16.
