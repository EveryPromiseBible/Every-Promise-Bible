James picks the most spectacular prayer in Israel's history and then goes out of
his way to say the man who prayed it was nobody special.

## The phrase

*Elijah was made of exactly the same stuff we are.*

The Greek is one word — **homoiopathes**, of like feelings, subject to the same
things. Paul and Barnabas use it at Lystra when a crowd tries to sacrifice to
them: *we are men of the same nature as you.*

James is heading off the obvious objection before anyone makes it. Yes, but he
was Elijah. No — he was not a different kind of creature.

And the man James chose is one whose recorded low point is asking God to let him
die under a broom tree.

## What the Greek word covers

**Homoiopathes** is built from *homoios*, alike, and *pathos*, feeling, suffering,
what happens to a person.

It is not a statement about ability. It is a statement about what he was subject to —
fear, exhaustion, hunger, and the wish to be finished.

All four are in one chapter of 1 Kings, and James knew it. See the note on
1 Kings 19:1–8.

## The prayer described

*He prayed hard that the rain would stop… then he prayed again, and the sky let
the rain down.*

The Greek behind *prayed hard* is a Hebrew-flavoured construction: he prayed with
prayer. Not a technique — earnestness, the same thing James means when he says a
righteous man's prayer works *when he means it all the way down*.

## What makes a prayer effective

*When a man who stands right with God prays… it puts real power to work.*

The word for *stands right* is the righteousness word. In the New Testament that
standing is a gift, received rather than achieved — which is what makes this
verse usable by ordinary people rather than only by the very good.

James has just spent a letter on practical faith, and he lands here: confession
to one another, prayer for one another, healing, and an ordinary man's prayer
shutting the sky.

> **Where the detail comes from.** The three and a half years is not stated in
> 1 Kings — James supplies it, and Jesus gives the same figure in Luke 4. The
> length of that drought was evidently well known, and both of them count it the
> same way.
