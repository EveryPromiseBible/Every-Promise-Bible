Two images joined, and the join is the argument.

## The lamp

*"**Nobody lights a lamp and then hides it away** or covers it with a bowl. You put it up on a
stand so that **everyone coming in can see by its light**."*

The Greek is **krupten** — a cellar, a hidden place.

Luke records this saying twice. The other has the same conclusion attached to hearing: *so pay
attention to how you listen*. See the note on Luke 8:16–18.

## The turn

*"**Your eye is the lamp of your body**."*

The image moves inside.

A lamp lights a room; an eye lights a person. The comparison is about what lets light in rather
than what gives it out.

## The condition

*"When your eye is **clear and single**, your whole body is **full of light**."*

The Greek is **haplous** — single, simple, uncompounded. And by extension generous.

That second meaning is worth noting. The word and its noun are used in the New Testament of
liberality: *he who gives, with liberality*, and *the riches of their liberality*.

A single eye, in the Greek of the period, could describe a person who was not calculating.

## The alternative

*"But when your eye is **bad**, your body is full of darkness."*

The Greek is **poneros** — evil, and it is the standard word for wicked.

But there is a Hebrew idiom underneath it. *An evil eye* means stingy: *do not eat the bread of
a miser; for as he thinks in his heart, so is he… **he who has an evil eye**.*

And Jesus uses it that way in the vineyard parable: *is your eye evil because I am good?* — said
to a man complaining about somebody else's wages.

## The warning

*"So be careful that **the light in you is not actually darkness**."*

The Greek is **skopei oun** — look out, therefore.

The most unsettling sentence in the paragraph. A man with a bad eye does not experience himself
as being in the dark. He experiences himself as seeing normally.

## The promise

*"If your whole body is filled with light, **with no dark corner left in it**, then you will be
**radiant all through, like a room with a bright lamp shining in it**."*

The Greek is *having no part dark*.

## What sits either side of it in Matthew

Matthew has the same saying in the Sermon on the Mount, and its neighbours settle the reading:

*"Do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth… for where your treasure is, there your heart
will be also."*

Then the eye.

Then: *"**No one can serve two masters**… you cannot serve God and mammon."*

Treasure, eye, mammon — in that order. The eye saying is sandwiched between two passages about
money.

## What Luke has just been dealing with

The sign of Jonah, and the crowds asking for more.

And immediately after this, dinner with a Pharisee who is surprised He did not wash — and the
woes: *you cleanse the outside of the cup and dish, but your inward part is full of greed and
wickedness*.

## What the two halves share

Both are about direction of travel.

The lamp is put where light gets out. The eye is what lets light in. And the paragraph's
question is whether what is inside is actually light.

> **The word that carries both senses.** *Haplous* — single, and generous. And its opposite in
> the Hebrew idiom is not blindness but stinginess.
