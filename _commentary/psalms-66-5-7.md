title: Come and see what God has done

An invitation to look at something that happened to somebody else.

## The command

*"**Come and see** what God has done, His awesome works among the children of men."*

The Hebrew is **lekhu ur'u** — go and look. It is an instruction to inspect the evidence.

*"**Come and see**," said Philip.* Nathanael had just asked whether anything good could come out
of Nazareth, and the answer he is given is not an argument.

*He said to them, "**Come and see**."* **Jesus**, to two men who asked where He was staying.

## What they are told to look at

*He turned the sea into dry land, **they walked through the river on foot**.*

Two crossings — the Red Sea and the Jordan, forty years apart. Both are dry ground appearing out
of water.

## The pronoun that should not be there

*There **we** rejoiced in Him.*

The psalm is written centuries after the exodus, by somebody who was not there, and it says
*we*.

Israel's memory is corporate. The Passover liturgy still does it — *we were slaves in Egypt* —
and Deuteronomy tells the second generation to say *the LORD brought us out* about a night none
of them saw. See the note on Deuteronomy 9:7–8.

*For **we were all baptised into one body**.* The New Testament does the same thing with a death
none of its readers attended.

## What is said about the watching

*His eyes **keep watch on the nations**.*

The verse before has Him ruling by His might forever. The psalm puts the sovereignty and the
attention together and does not treat them as competing.

## What follows in the psalm

*For You, O God, have tested us; You have refined us as silver is refined… **but You brought us
out to rich fulfilment**.* See the note on Psalms 66:8–12.

The invitation to come and see is issued by a man who has been through fire and water and is
telling other people to look at what God did.

> **The word that gives it away.** *There **we** rejoiced.* Written by somebody centuries too
> late to have been at the sea, who counts himself among the people who crossed it.
