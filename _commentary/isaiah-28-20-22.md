Isaiah calls judgement two things, and both of them mean *not His*.

## The bed

*The bed is **too short** to stretch out on, the blanket **too narrow** to wrap
yourself in.*

A proverb about inadequate arrangements. Whatever they had made for themselves would
not cover them — a treaty with Egypt, in the chapter's context, described a few
verses earlier as *a covenant with death*.

You can lie on a short bed. You cannot rest on it.

## The two battles

*"For the LORD will rise up as He did at **Mount Perazim**. He will rouse Himself as
in the **Valley of Gibeon**."*

Both are places where God fought **for** Israel.

Perazim is where David defeated the Philistines and said *the LORD has broken through
my enemies before me like a breakthrough of water.*

Gibeon is where the hailstones fell on the Amorites and the sun stood still for
Joshua.

The threat in this verse is that He will do the same thing again — with His own
people on the receiving end.

## The two names for it

*To do **His work, His strange work**, to perform **His task, His alien task**.*

The Hebrew for *strange* is **zuwr** — foreign, from somewhere else, not native. And
*alien* is **nokri** — belonging to another.

Isaiah names judgement twice, and both times as something out of character. Not that
God does not do it — that it is not the work that comes naturally to Him.

## Set beside what is called natural

*The LORD, the LORD — a God tender and kind, gracious and full of compassion, slow to
anger, overflowing with steadfast love.* That is God's own self-description, and
none of it is called strange. See the note on Exodus 34:5–7.

*His anger lasts only a moment, but His favour lasts a lifetime.* See the note on
Psalms 30:4–5.

Lamentations says it plainly: *He does not afflict willingly, nor grieve the children
of men.*

## The warning

*"So **do not scoff now**, or your chains will grow heavier."*

The Hebrew is that their bonds will be made strong. Mockery tightens what is already
holding them.

## Where the chapter is going

Two verses earlier is one of the great promises in Isaiah: *Behold, I lay in Zion a
foundation stone, a tried stone, **a precious cornerstone**, a sure foundation.
Whoever believes will not act hastily.*

Peter quotes it, and Paul quotes it. In the middle of a chapter about drunkards,
short beds and strange work, a cornerstone is laid.

> **What Isaiah is arguing against.** *We have made a covenant with death, and with
> Sheol we are in agreement* — the leaders' own words, quoted back at them. They had
> made an arrangement they thought would hold, and the bed was too short.
