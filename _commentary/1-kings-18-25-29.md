Half a day of noise, and the historian keeps returning to the same three words.

## The offer

*"**You go first, since there are so many of you**. Choose your bull and prepare it."*

Four hundred and fifty of them against one. Elijah gives them first pick, the first
turn, and the whole morning.

*"Then call on your god — **but light no fire**."*

That is the entire rule of the contest, and both sides are bound by it.

## The morning

*They called on Baal **from morning until noon**. "**O Baal, answer us!**" they
shouted.*

Baal was the storm god — the one who was supposed to bring rain and lightning. Fire from
the sky was precisely his department, and Israel had been in drought for three years
under his management.

## The first refrain

*But **there was no voice. No one answered**.*

## The dancing

*They **danced around the altar** they had made.*

The Hebrew is a limping, hobbling dance — the same verb Elijah used on the crowd earlier
in the chapter: *how long will you **falter** between two opinions?*

He had accused Israel of limping between two positions, and now the prophets of Baal
limp around their altar.

## The mockery

*At noon Elijah began to mock them.*

Four suggestions, and each one is worse for Baal than the last:

- *"**Surely he's a god!**"*
- *"Maybe he's **deep in thought**, or **busy**, or **off on a journey**."*
- *"Maybe he's **asleep** and needs to be woken up."*

The Hebrew of *busy* is a euphemism — he has withdrawn, he is occupied privately.

Elijah is not arguing theology. He is asking practical questions about where the god
has got to.

## The escalation

*So they **shouted louder** and, in their custom, **cut themselves with swords and
spears until the blood ran**.*

Self-laceration was standard Canaanite practice and the law forbade it: *you shall not
make any cuttings in your flesh.*

More volume, then more blood. When nothing answers, the only thing left is to raise the
cost.

## The afternoon

*Noon passed, and they **raved on into the afternoon**, all the way to **the time of the
evening offering**.*

Six more hours. And the hour it ends at is the hour of the sacrifice in Jerusalem — the
hour Elijah then steps forward and prays. See the note on 1 Kings 18:30–39.

## The last line

*But **there was nothing. No voice. No answer. No one paying attention**.*

The historian says it twice in five verses, and the second time he adds a third clause.

The Hebrew for *no one paying attention* is *none regarded* — nobody there to notice.

## What it took to answer Elijah

One prayer, sixty-three words in the Hebrew, and no shouting.

And before it he drenched the altar with twelve barrels of water in the third year of a
drought.

> **What the two halves of the day measure.** From morning to evening on one side, and
> the time it takes to say one prayer on the other. The historian gives four hundred and
> fifty men five verses and Elijah's request three.
