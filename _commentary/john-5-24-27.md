Three statements, stacked, and each one is stronger than the last. Jesus is not
building toward a promise here; He is closing a question.

## Read the three parts

> whoever hears My word and trusts the One who sent Me **has** eternal life.
> He **does not come up** for condemnation.
> He **has already crossed over** out of death and into life.

- *has* — present tense. Not will have.
- *does not come up for condemnation* — the word is the courtroom one. He is not
  saying the verdict will be favourable. He is saying the person does not appear
  on that docket.
- *has already crossed over* — and this is the one that carries the weight.

## The tense in that last phrase

The Greek verb is in the perfect. English does not have a good single word for
it: it means a thing was done at a point in the past and the state it produced
is still the case. Not "crossed over and we shall see," but crossed, and standing
on the other bank now.

It is the same tense as *it is finished* on the cross. A completed action whose
result stands.

So the sentence is not describing something a person is working toward or could
slip back out of. It describes a border already behind them.

---

## What it is conditioned on

Worth being exact, because the condition is stated and it is short: *hears My
word and trusts the One who sent Me.* Hearing and trusting. There is no third
item, and nothing about sustaining it by effort.

That is the whole entry requirement in a sentence that then hands over eternal
life, immunity from the courtroom, and a completed crossing.

## The strange middle of the passage

Then Jesus says a time is coming *and it is here now* when the dead hear His
voice and live. Both at once — future and present in the same breath.

He means it in both senses, and John's Gospel goes on to show it: Lazarus called
out of a tomb, and people all through the book crossing from death to life while
still walking around. The resurrection at the end is not a different event from
what happens when someone hears Him now. It is the same voice.

> **Why this passage gets used the way it does.** People reach for it when they
> are afraid of losing what they have. It answers that fear, but notice how — not
> by promising to hold on tightly enough, but by pointing out that the crossing
> is in the past tense. The question the verse settles is not *will I make it*.
> It is *where am I standing*, and it answers: on this side already.
