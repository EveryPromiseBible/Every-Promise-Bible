Paul stops assessing people by what can be seen, and gives the reason.

## The new measure

*From this moment on we **do not size anyone up by what the flesh can see**.*

The Greek is *according to the flesh* — by outward appearance, by natural
reckoning: background, record, credentials, family, what a man looks like he is.

## Even about Christ

*Even if we did once size up Christ that way, **we do not know Him that way any
longer**.*

Paul had. He had assessed Jesus of Nazareth by the visible facts — a carpenter's
son from a bad village, executed as a criminal — and drawn the obvious conclusion,
and gone to Damascus with letters.

He is admitting his own worst misjudgement as the example.

## The sentence

*So if anyone is in Christ — **a whole new creation**.*

There is no verb in the Greek. It reads *if anyone in Christ, new creation* — the
words land without a connective, which is why translators supply one.

**Ktisis** is the creation word. Paul does not say improved, reformed, forgiven, or
repaired. New — **kainos**, new in kind rather than merely recent.

## The two clauses

*The old things **are gone**; look, all things have **become new**.*

*Are gone* is a completed action, standing done. *Have become new* likewise.

Neither is in progress and neither is future.

## What that reaches

The old things being gone is stated without a list of exceptions — which is Paul's
habit whenever he describes what happened at the cross. In Colossians the record
against us was *wiped out… nailed to the cross*; in Romans, *there is therefore now
no condemnation.*

Whatever came down the line before — a family's history, a father's diagnosis, a
past that had a claim — the sentence puts it under *the old things are gone.*

## "In Christ"

The whole thing is located there. Not *if anyone tries hard* — *if anyone is in
Christ.*

It is Paul's most-used phrase, some two hundred occurrences across his letters, and
it is where every benefit in his theology sits.

## What comes next

*Now all things are of God, who has reconciled us to Himself through Jesus Christ.*

And then the sentence the chapter is famous for: *He made Him who knew no sin to be
sin for us, that we might become the righteousness of God in Him.*

The new creation is not a self-improvement claim. It is the result of an exchange
described eleven verses later.

> **What Paul had been.** *A blasphemer, a persecutor, and an insolent man* — his own
> description, in his own letter. The man who wrote *the old things are gone* had
> more old things than most.
