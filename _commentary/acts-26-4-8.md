Paul's last recorded defence, before a king, and he turns it into something else.

## Who is in the room

Agrippa, Bernice, the governor Festus, the commanders and the prominent men of
Caesarea, *with great pomp*.

And Paul, in chains, given permission to speak.

## What he says about his past

*"They could testify, if they were willing, that **I lived as a Pharisee, by the
strictest party of our religion**."*

He does not soften it. Elsewhere he puts it harder: *I persecuted this Way to the
death, binding and delivering into prisons both men and women.*

Benjamin's blessing was one line: *in the morning he devours the prey, and in the
evening he divides the spoil.* Paul names his own tribe twice in his letters, and
the morning is what Luke describes with the word used of an animal mangling a
carcass. See the note on Genesis 49:27.

## What he says he is on trial for

*"And now I stand here on trial **for the hope of the promise God made to our
fathers**."*

Not for a crime, a riot, or a sect. For a hope, and he says it is the hope *our
twelve tribes* are serving God night and day to see.

He puts himself on the same side as his accusers and says the disagreement is about
whether it has happened.

## The question he asks the king

*"**Why should it be judged something incredible by any of you that God raises the
dead?**"*

A prisoner asking the court a question. It is not a defence — nothing in it is
about his innocence.

## What he does with the hearing

He tells them about the road to Damascus: the light above the brightness of the
sun, the voice, and the commission — *to open their eyes, to turn them from
darkness to light… that they may receive forgiveness of sins.*

Festus interrupts him: *Paul, you are beside yourself! Much learning is driving you
mad.*

And then Agrippa: *you almost persuade me to become a Christian.*

## His answer

*"I would to God that not only you, but also all who hear me today, might become
both almost and altogether such as I am — **except for these chains**."*

He is in irons, and he wishes the whole room were as he is. The only thing he
excludes is the chains.

That is the evening dividing the spoil. Everything he took in the morning he is
handing out, from a prisoner's dock, to the men who are trying him.

> **What Agrippa concluded.** *This man might have been set free if he had not
> appealed to Caesar.* He appealed, and it took him to Rome — which is where he
> wrote the letters. Philippians, Ephesians, Colossians and Philemon come out of
> that appeal.
