The harshest address in the letter, and four verses later the sentence turns.

## The address

*You **adulterous men, you adulterous women** — has it not dawned on you yet?*

The Greek is **moichalides** — adulteresses. Feminine plural.

## Why that word

Not sexual accusation. It is the prophets' vocabulary.

Israel is the wife, God is the husband, and idolatry is adultery. Hosea's whole marriage is
built on it, and Jeremiah and Ezekiel use it at length.

James is using a covenant category, not making a moral charge about anyone's conduct.

## The claim

*Being **this world's friend means being God's enemy**.*

The Greek is **philia tou kosmou echthra tou Theou estin** — friendship of the world is enmity
with God.

## The second statement

*Sign up as a friend of this world and **you have put yourself in the field against God**.*

The Greek is **kathistatai** — is constituted, is appointed.

The construction makes it a status rather than a feeling. The person does not have to intend the
opposition.

## What *world* means here

**Kosmos** — the ordered system, human society organised without reference to God.

Not the created earth. James has already said *every good gift and every perfect gift is from
above*.

## The quotation nobody can find

*Or do you think Scripture speaks for nothing when it says **the spirit God settled inside us
yearns, and yearns jealously**?*

The Greek is **pros phthonon epipothei to pneuma**.

No Old Testament verse matches. James may be summarising the whole prophetic theme of God's
jealousy rather than quoting a text.

## The ambiguity

The Greek can be read two ways: the spirit He put in us yearns jealously *for Him*, or God yearns
jealously *over* the spirit He put in us.

Translators divide. Both readings say the relationship is not casual.

## The turn

*But **He gives more grace than that**.*

The Greek is **meizona de didosin charin** — but He gives greater grace.

Three words in Greek, after twelve verses of murder, war, envy and adultery.

## What it answers

Everything above it.

The paragraph has established that the reader is on the wrong side, and the next clause does not
qualify or narrow.

## The proverb

*Which is why it says: **God squares up against the proud, and pours grace over the lowly**.*

Proverbs 3:34, in the Septuagint's wording.

The Greek is **antitassetai** — sets Himself in battle array against. A military verb, the same
root as the one in Romans 13 for setting oneself against an authority.

**Tapeinois** — the low, the humble, the ones with nothing to bring.

## Who else quotes it

*Likewise you younger people, submit yourselves to your elders… for "**God resists the proud, and
gives grace to the humble**."* — Peter, in his letter.

Two apostles quote the same verse in the same form.

## What follows

*Therefore **submit to God. Resist the devil and he will flee from you. Draw near to God and He will
draw near to you**.*

*Humble yourselves in the sight of the Lord, and **He will lift you up**.* See the note on
James 4:7–10.

Ten imperatives in four verses, and the last one is passive: He does the lifting.

## Where the grace goes

Toward the lowly, which is the one direction water runs by itself.

> **The three words.** **Meizona didosin charin** — He gives greater grace. Placed immediately
> after the sentence that says the reader is in the field against Him.
