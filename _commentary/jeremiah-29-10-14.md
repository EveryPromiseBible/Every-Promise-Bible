The most quoted verse in Jeremiah, and it begins with a number that never gets
quoted with it.

## The sentence it belongs to

*When seventy years are completed for Babylon, I will come to you and make good
on My gracious promise to bring you back to this place.*

Seventy years. Most of the people reading the letter would die in Babylon. The
promise is real, and its fulfilment is beyond the lifetime of nearly everyone
being addressed.

That is the setting for *I know the plans I have for you*.

## Who received it

Exiles — and Jeremiah's letter tells them what to do in the meantime: build
houses, plant gardens, marry, have children, and *seek the peace of the city
where I have sent you*.

Not endure it. Settle in it, and pray for the place holding you.

## What the promise says

*Plans for peace and not for harm, to give you a future and a hope.*

The Hebrew for *future* is literally an **end**, an outcome — the far side of
something. Paired with hope, it means: there is a later, and it is good.

Spoken to people whose visible situation was going to last seventy years, that is
not a small thing. It is precisely the promise a person needs when the near view
holds nothing.

## Why it matters that we read it whole

Quoted alone it can sound like a guarantee that plans will go well soon. In place
it is stronger and harder: God's intentions are good, the timescale may be longer
than your life, and you are told to plant gardens while you wait.

## Who was reading this letter

*In the first year of his reign I, **Daniel**, understood by the books the number of the years
specified by the word of the LORD through Jeremiah the prophet, **that He would accomplish seventy
years** in the desolations of Jerusalem.*

**Daniel** 9. An old man in Babylon doing arithmetic on this letter and finding the seventy years
nearly up — and what he does with the discovery is pray a confession that never once appeals to
Israel's record: *we do not present our supplications before You **because of our righteous deeds,
but because of Your great mercies**.*

## The plans

*I know the plans I have for you.*

*In Him also we have obtained an inheritance, being **predestined according to the purpose of Him
who works all things according to the counsel of His will**.*

*And we know that **all things work together for good** to those who love God, to those who are
the called according to His purpose.*

**Paul** makes Jeremiah's claim general and gives it a name: *for whom He foreknew, He also
predestined **to be conformed to the image of His Son**.* That is what the plans are for.

## The future and the hope

The Hebrew for *future* is an **end**, an outcome — the far side of something.

*Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord **Jesus Christ**, who according to His abundant mercy
has begotten us again to **a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead**,
to an inheritance incorruptible and undefiled and that does not fade away, **reserved in heaven for
you**.*

**Peter** writes it to exiles — he calls them that in the first verse — and the far side he names
is past the end of anybody's life, which is exactly the problem Jeremiah's seventy years posed.

## Seek Me and find Me

*"**Ask, and it will be given to you; seek, and you will find**; knock, and it will be opened to
you."*

*So that they should seek the Lord, in the hope that they might grope for Him and find Him,
**though He is not far from each one of us**.*

**Paul** in Athens adds the clause that makes the seeking bearable. The letter to the exiles says
seek Me with all your heart; **Acts** 17 says the distance was never the difficulty.

> **What follows.** *You will call on Me and come and pray to Me, and I will
> listen. You will seek Me and find Me, when you seek Me with all your heart.*
> The promise about the future is immediately followed by an invitation to talk
> to Him in the middle of the seventy years, not only at the end of them.
