The most quoted verse in Jeremiah, and it begins with a number that never gets
quoted with it.

## The sentence it belongs to

*When seventy years are completed for Babylon, I will come to you and make good
on My gracious promise to bring you back to this place.*

Seventy years. Most of the people reading the letter would die in Babylon. The
promise is real, and its fulfilment is beyond the lifetime of nearly everyone
being addressed.

That is the setting for *I know the plans I have for you*.

## Who received it

Exiles — and Jeremiah's letter tells them what to do in the meantime: build
houses, plant gardens, marry, have children, and *seek the peace of the city
where I have sent you*.

Not endure it. Settle in it, and pray for the place holding you.

## What the promise says

*Plans for peace and not for harm, to give you a future and a hope.*

The Hebrew for *future* is literally an **end**, an outcome — the far side of
something. Paired with hope, it means: there is a later, and it is good.

Spoken to people whose visible situation was going to last seventy years, that is
not a small thing. It is precisely the promise a person needs when the near view
holds nothing.

## Why it matters that we read it whole

Quoted alone it can sound like a guarantee that plans will go well soon. In place
it is stronger and harder: God's intentions are good, the timescale may be longer
than your life, and you are told to plant gardens while you wait.

> **What follows.** *You will call on Me and come and pray to Me, and I will
> listen. You will seek Me and find Me, when you seek Me with all your heart.*
> The promise about the future is immediately followed by an invitation to talk
> to Him in the middle of the seventy years, not only at the end of them.
