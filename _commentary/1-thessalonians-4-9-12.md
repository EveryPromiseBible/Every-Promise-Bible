He starts a paragraph by saying he does not need to write it, and then writes it.

## The subject

*About **loving one another as brothers**, there is no need for anyone to write you a word.*

The Greek is **peri de tes philadelphias** — concerning brotherly love.

**Philadelphia** — the ordinary Greek word for affection between siblings, taken over by the
church for affection between believers.

## The reason he does not need to

*God Himself **has been your teacher** in this.*

The Greek is **theodidaktoi** — God-taught. One word, and it appears nowhere else in the Greek
Bible or in surviving Greek literature before this.

Paul appears to have coined it.

## Where the idea comes from

*And they shall all be taught by God.* — Isaiah, quoted by Jesus in John 6.

And Jeremiah's new covenant: *no more shall every man teach his neighbour… **for they all shall
know Me**, from the least of them to the greatest*. See the note on
Jeremiah 31:31–34.

Paul is saying the promise has landed on a Gentile congregation in Macedonia.

## The evidence

*You are doing it, too — **for every brother in the whole of Macedonia**.*

Not a theory about them. Their reputation had travelled, and Paul had heard it from other
churches.

The Macedonian churches are the ones he later tells Corinth about: *in a great trial of
affliction, the abundance of their joy and their deep poverty abounded in the riches of their
liberality*.

## The request anyway

*Still, dear friends, we urge you: **let there be more of it, and then more again**.*

The Greek is **perisseuein mallon** — to abound more.

He affirms the thing fully and then asks for more of it. The commendation is not a way of leading
into a rebuke.

## The three instructions

*Make it your ambition to **live a quiet life**, to **tend to what is yours to tend**, and to **work
with your own hands**.*

The Greek for the first is **philotimeisthai hesuchazein** — literally *be ambitious to be quiet*.
A deliberate contradiction; **philotimia** was the drive for public honour that ran Greek civic
life.

Paul tells them to compete at being unnoticed.

## The second

**Prassein ta idia** — to attend to your own affairs.

The letter's second half deals with people who had stopped working because they expected the
Lord's return imminently. The second letter is blunter: *if anyone will not work, neither shall
he eat*.

## The third

*Work with your own hands, **just as we told you**.*

Manual labour, which the Greek upper class despised and Paul practised — *labouring night and day,
that we might not be a burden to any of you*.

He had done it in front of them in Thessalonica.

## The first result

*Then **the way you live will earn the respect of those outside**.*

The Greek is **euschemonos** — becomingly, properly, in good form.

**Tous exo** — those outside. Paul is thinking about the neighbours watching.

## The second

*And **you will find yourselves lacking nothing**.*

The Greek is **medenos chreian echete** — have need of nothing.

Which can mean *lack nothing* or *need no one*. Both readings fit a paragraph about working with
your hands.

## What comes immediately after

*But I do not want you to be ignorant, brethren, concerning those who have fallen asleep… **for
the Lord Himself will descend from heaven with a shout**.* See the note on
1 Thessalonians 4:16–18.

The most detailed passage on the Lord's return in Paul, and the paragraph before it is about
minding your own business and holding down a job.

> **The word.** **Theodidaktoi** — God-taught. It occurs nowhere else in Greek before this letter,
> and Paul uses it to explain why a paragraph he is writing did not need writing.
