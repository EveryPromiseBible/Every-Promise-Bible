Three sentences about produce, and the last one moves from orchards to speech.

## The rule

*"**A good tree does not produce bad fruit**, and **a bad tree does not produce good
fruit**."*

The Greek for *bad* is **sapron** — rotten, worthless, decayed.

Stated both ways, so there is no exception in either direction.

## The test

*"**Every tree is known by what it grows**."*

The Greek is *each tree is known by its own fruit*.

Fruit is not what makes a tree good. It is what makes it identifiable.

## The examples

*"Nobody picks **figs off a thornbush**, and nobody gathers **grapes from a briar patch**."*

Matthew's version has grapes from thornbushes and figs from thistles.

Both are absurd on purpose. Nobody in the audience has ever tried it.

## The application

*"A **good person brings good things up out of the good stored away in the heart**, and an
**evil person brings evil up out of the evil stored there**."*

The Greek is **thesaurou** — a treasury, a storehouse. The root of *thesaurus*.

Something is laid up, and what comes out is drawn from it.

## The clause

*"Because **what the mouth says is whatever the heart is full of**."*

The Greek is *out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks* — **ek tou perisseumatos**,
out of the overflow.

Not what the heart contains. What it has too much of.

## What Matthew adds

The same saying in Matthew is aimed at the Pharisees, and he adds: *for **every idle word**
men may speak, they will give account of it in the day of judgment. For by your words you
will be justified, and by your words you will be condemned.*

## What comes before it in Luke

The whole Sermon on the Plain: love your enemies, judge not, give and it will be given to
you, and the blind leading the blind.

And immediately before this, the beam and the speck: *first remove the plank from your own
eye*.

The tree saying follows a passage about seeing clearly.

## What comes after

The two houses — one on rock and one on earth without a foundation — and the flood.

*"Why do you call Me 'Lord, Lord,' and **not do the things which I say**?"*

## What the saying is for

Diagnosis. Neither Gospel uses it to tell people to produce better fruit.

A thornbush cannot be improved into a fig tree, and the sayings are about identification
rather than effort.

## Where the tree image goes elsewhere

*He is like **a tree planted by the water**… **it never stops bearing fruit**.* See the note
on Jeremiah 17:7–8.

The tree there is *transplanted* — set out deliberately by somebody.

And the fruit of the Spirit is singular in Paul, and grows rather than being assembled. See
the note on Galatians 5:22–24.

## What changes a tree

The saying diagnoses and does not prescribe. Nothing in it tells a bramble to produce figs.

*"**I will give you a new heart** and put a new spirit within you; I will take the heart of stone out
of your flesh and give you a heart of flesh."*

*Therefore, if anyone is in **Christ**, he is **a new creation**.*

*Now hope does not disappoint, because **the love of God has been poured out in our hearts by the
Holy Spirit** who was given to us.*

**Paul**'s verb in **Romans** 5 is **ekkechytai** — poured out, and the same image: the heart is a
container and what comes out of it is what was put in.

## The overflow

*Out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks.* **Ek tou perisseumatos** — out of the surplus.

*"**If anyone thirsts, let him come to Me and drink. He who believes in Me, as the Scripture has
said, out of his heart will flow rivers of living water.**"*

**John** 7, and it is the same anatomy — the heart, and what runs out of it — with the source named
and located outside the person.

*But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, kindness, goodness, faithfulness,
gentleness, self-control.*

**Karpos**, singular, and it belongs to somebody. **Paul** does not call it the fruit of effort.

> **What the last sentence measures.** Not what is in the heart, but what there is too much
> of. The Greek is *out of the overflow* — the mouth reports the surplus.
