Five proverbs near the end of the book, and three of them are about other people's opinions.

## The first

*A man's **pride will bring him low**, but a **humble spirit will hold on to honor**.*

The Hebrew is *a man's pride shall bring him low, but the humble in spirit will obtain
honour*.

Both halves end somewhere the man did not aim: the proud man goes down, and the low man
keeps hold of the thing the proud one was after.

Jesus states it twice, in Luke, as a rule: *whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and he
who humbles himself will be exalted*.

## The accomplice

*Whoever **joins a thief hates his own life**; he **hears the curse and says nothing**.*

The Hebrew refers to a specific legal procedure. Leviticus 5: a man who hears a public
adjuration to give evidence and stays silent *shall bear his guilt*.

The proverb is about a witness in court who keeps his mouth shut, and it says the silence is
self-harm.

## The snare

*The **fear of man lays a snare**, but **whoever trusts in the LORD is kept safe**.*

The Hebrew for *snare* is **moqesh** — a bird trap, the trigger stick that springs it.

A trap only works if the bird does not know it is there.

## Where the trap catches people in the Bible

Saul: *I have sinned, for I have transgressed the commandment of the LORD… **because I
feared the people and obeyed their voice***.

Pilate, *wanting to gratify the crowd*.

And the rulers in John who believed but would not say so, *for they loved the praise of men
more than the praise of God*.

Aaron at the golden calf, and Peter at Antioch — *fearing those who were of the
circumcision*, so that Paul withstood him to his face.

The list is long and it includes almost every kind of person.

## The safety

The Hebrew is **yesuggav** — set on high, put out of reach.

The same root as the tower in an earlier proverb: *the name of the LORD is a strong tower;
the righteous run to it and are safe*.

The trap is at ground level. The alternative is elevation.

## The ruler

*Many seek **the ruler's favor**, but **justice for a man comes from the LORD**.*

The Hebrew is *many seek the ruler's face*.

Not wrong to seek it. The proverb says only that the verdict comes from elsewhere, so the
queue at the palace door is aimed at the wrong desk.

Joseph and Daniel both had a ruler's favour, and Genesis and Daniel both attribute it to God
rather than to the king.

## The last one

*The unjust are **an abomination to the righteous**, and the upright are **an abomination
to the wicked**.*

Symmetrical, and the second half is the sting. The disgust runs both ways.

Anybody expecting to be liked by everyone is being told, in the last proverb of the chapter,
that it does not work like that.

## Why the five sit together

Pride, silence in court, fear of man, seeking the ruler's face, and mutual abomination.

Every one is about what other people think, and the collection puts them in a row.

> **What the snare needs to work.** Not to be seen. The proverb names the trap and, in the
> same line, gives the other option — trusting Him, and being set up out of reach.
