*Where two or three come together in My name, I am there among them* is quoted
at small gatherings everywhere, and it is a true and warm thing to say. It is
also the last line of a paragraph about something else entirely.

## What the paragraph is doing

The subject is a Christian who has wronged another Christian, and the process is
laid out in stages:

- go to him **privately** first
- if that fails, take one or two others
- if that fails, tell the church
- if that fails, treat him as an outsider

Every step is designed to keep the circle as small as possible for as long as
possible. The aim is stated at the start: *you have won your brother back*.

## The two or three

The number is not chosen for its smallness. It comes from the previous sentence
— *every matter may be settled on the word of two or three witnesses* — which is
the standard of evidence from Deuteronomy.

So when Jesus says *where two or three come together in My name, I am there*, He
is promising His presence to the hardest thing a church ever does: the
conversation where somebody is confronted about a wrong.

## What that does to the promise

It does not shrink it. It makes it braver.

The comfort of a handful of believers meeting with Christ among them is real and
the verse carries it. But it was given first to people about to sit down in a
room and try to win somebody back, and it says that Christ is in the room for
that.

> **On binding and loosing.** The phrase belongs to the same setting — the
> language is from rabbinic practice, where binding and loosing meant declaring
> what was forbidden and permitted. In context it is about the church's judgement
> in exactly the situation being described, not a general formula.
