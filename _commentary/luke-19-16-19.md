Two servants report, and the arithmetic of the reward has nothing to do with the
arithmetic of the return.

## What they made

*"Sir, **your one mina has earned ten more**."*

*"Sir, **your one mina has made five more**."*

Same starting capital — every servant got one mina. Different results.

## Whose money it was

Both of them say *your* mina. Neither claims it.

The Greek is emphatic in the first: *your mina has earned* — the servant credits the
money rather than himself.

## What the master says

*"**Well done. You are a good servant.**"*

## The measure

*"You proved trustworthy with **a very small thing**."*

A mina was roughly three months' wages. Not nothing — and the master calls it *very
small*.

Whatever scale he is working on, that sum is at the bottom of it.

## The reward

*"So **take charge of ten towns**."*

Cities, in exchange for three months' wages well handled.

And the second: *"You take charge of five towns."*

## The proportion

Ten minas earned → ten towns. Five earned → five towns. The reward tracks the return
exactly.

But the *scale* is absurd. Nothing about a small sum of money merits a province, and
the master hands them out without comment.

The money was a test, not a transaction.

## What is not said

No comparison between the two servants. The one who made five is not asked why he
did not make ten.

Both are rewarded on the same principle, and the master's pleasure is not divided
between them.

## The principle

Luke has already recorded Jesus saying it plainly: *he who is faithful in what is
least is faithful also in much… if you have not been faithful in the unrighteous
mammon, who will commit to your trust the true riches?*

Money, in that saying, is the small thing and the test. The *true riches* are
something else.

## The context

The parable is told *because they were expecting the kingdom of God to appear at any
moment*, and because He was near Jerusalem.

He answers an expectation of immediate arrival with a story about a long absence and
what to do during it.

> cloth, and gives his reason: *I was afraid of you. You are a hard man.* See the note
> on Luke 19:20–23.
