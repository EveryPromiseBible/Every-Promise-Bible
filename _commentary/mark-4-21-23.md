Two ridiculous places to put a lamp, and then a promise about what gets found out.

## The question

*"**Does anyone light a lamp and then set it under a bucket or shove it under the bed?**"*

The Greek is **modion** — a measuring basket, about two gallons. A household item, upended
over a flame.

Mark asks it as a question. Matthew and Luke state it as a fact.

## Why both are absurd

A basket over a lamp smothers it. A lamp under a bed sets fire to the bedding.

Neither is a way of hiding light. Both are ways of destroying it or the house.

## The answer

*"Of course not. **It goes up on a stand**."*

The Greek is **luchnian** — a lampstand.

The word is the Greek Old Testament's term for the seven-branched menorah in the tabernacle,
and it is the word Revelation uses for the seven churches.

## The purpose

*"**Nothing is hidden that will not be brought out into the open**, and nothing is covered up
that will not come to light."*

The Greek construction is *for nothing is hidden **except that it should be revealed**.*

That is stronger than the translation suggests. The concealment is temporary by design — the
reason for hiding it was that it would be brought out.

## What is being hidden

The parables themselves.

He has just told the sower to a crowd and explained it only to the disciples. See the note on
Matthew 13:10–17.

The saying about the lamp comes immediately after, and it says the private explanation is
not the final state of things.

## What Jesus keeps quiet

Through Mark's Gospel He silences almost everyone. Demons are told to be quiet, healed men
are told to say nothing, and the disciples are told not to say He is the Christ.

Scholars call it the messianic secret, and Mark reports it more insistently than anyone
else.

This verse is Mark's own note on the practice: what is hidden is hidden in order to be
brought out.

## The one day He refuses to silence anybody

The tenth of Nisan, on the road down the Mount of Olives — *if these people were silent, the
very stones would start shouting*. See the note on Luke 19:36–40.

## The refrain

*"**If you have ears to hear, then hear**."*

The Greek is *if anyone has ears to hear, let him hear*.

Mark uses it twice in this chapter, at the end of the sower and at the end of the lamp.

It is the sentence Revelation attaches to every one of the seven letters.

## What follows immediately

*"**Take heed what you hear.** With the same measure you use, it will be measured to you; and
to you who hear, more will be given."*

Mark's version says *what*; Luke's says *how*. See the note on Luke 8:16–18.

And then the two seed parables — the one that grows while the farmer sleeps, and the mustard
seed. See the note on Mark 4:26–29.

## Where the chapter ends

*And on the same day, when evening had come, He said to them, "Let us cross over to the other
side."*

The storm, the pillow in the stern, and *why are you so fearful?* See the note on
Mark 4:35–41.

A chapter of teaching about hearing, and then a boat.

> **Why anything was covered.** Mark's Greek says it plainly: nothing is hidden *except in
> order that* it should be brought out. The concealing had a deadline built into it.
