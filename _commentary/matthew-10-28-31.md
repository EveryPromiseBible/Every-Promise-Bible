Three times *do not be afraid*, and one thing in between that He says to fear.

## Where this is

The sending of the twelve, and the paragraph before it is not gentle.

*"Behold, I send you out as sheep in the midst of wolves."*

*"You will be hated by all for My name's sake."*

*"A disciple is not above his teacher."*

## The first instruction

*"**Do not be afraid of those who can kill the body but have no power to kill the soul**."*

The Greek is **me phobeisthe apo ton apoktennonton to soma**.

He grants that they can kill the body. The limit named is not on their intent.

## The exception

*"**Be in awe instead of the One who can destroy both soul and body in hell**."*

The Greek is **phobeisthe de mallon** — but fear rather.

**Geenna** — Gehenna, the valley outside Jerusalem where refuse burned.

## What the sentence does

Replaces one fear with another, and the second one displaces the first.

A person who fears the greater thing has nothing left over for the lesser.

## The sparrows

*"**Two sparrows are sold for a small coin**, are they not?"*

The Greek is **assariou** — an assarion, a copper coin worth about an hour's wage for a labourer.

Two birds for the smallest coin in circulation.

Luke's version has five for two coins — buy in bulk and the fifth is free.

## The claim

*"And yet **not one of them drops to the ground without your Father**."*

The Greek is **aneu tou Patros humon**.

Not *without your Father knowing*. The Greek says only *without your Father*.

Translations supply *knowing* or *caring*. The sentence itself does not.

## The hairs

*"As for you, **even the hairs on your head have all been counted**."*

The Greek is **erithmemenai eisin** — have been numbered. Perfect passive.

Not counted at a moment. Counted, and the count stands.

## The conclusion

*"So **do not be afraid**."*

The third time in four verses.

## The comparison

*"You are worth more than **a whole sky full of sparrows**."*

The Greek is **pollon strouthion diapherete** — you are of more value than many sparrows.

## The argument's shape

Not that they will be protected. The paragraph opens by acknowledging that they can be killed.

The argument is about accounting. If the coinage covers sparrows, it covers them.

## What follows

*"Therefore **whoever confesses Me before men, him I will also confess before My Father**."*

*"Do not think that I came to bring peace on earth. I did not come to bring peace but a sword."*

*"He who loses his life for My sake will find it."*

## The last verse of the chapter

*"And whoever gives one of these little ones only **a cup of cold water** in the name of a disciple,
assuredly, I say to you, he shall by no means lose his reward."*

The smallest gift, and the same accounting.

## What Luke does with the sparrows

*"**Are not five sparrows sold for two copper coins? And not one of them is forgotten before God**."*

The Greek is **epilelesmenon** — forgotten.

Same argument, different verb.

> **The clause.** *Not one of them drops to the ground without your Father.* Translations supply
> *knowing*; the Greek does not have it.
