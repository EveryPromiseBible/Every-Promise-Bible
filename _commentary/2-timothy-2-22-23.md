Two verbs in opposite directions, and the second one is longer.

## Who is being written to

Timothy, at Ephesus, from a Roman prison. Paul's last surviving letter — chapter 4 has *the
time of my departure is at hand*.

Timothy is not a young man by now, but he had been very young when Paul picked him up at
Lystra, and Paul had written earlier: *let no one despise your youth*.

## What comes just before

*In a great house there are not only vessels of gold and silver, but also of wood and clay,
some for honour and some for dishonour. **Therefore if anyone cleanses himself** from the
latter, he will be a vessel for honour, sanctified and useful for the Master.*

## The first instruction

*Run hard away from **the cravings that come with being young**.*

The Greek is **pheuge** — flee. Present imperative: keep running.

**Neoterikas epithumias** — youthful desires. Not necessarily sexual; the word covers
impulsiveness, ambition, the appetite for a quarrel.

The context here suggests arguments as much as anything, given what verse 23 is about.

## Where the same verb goes

*Flee sexual immorality.* See the note on 1 Corinthians 6:15–17.

*Flee from idolatry.*

*Flee these things* — of the love of money, in the first letter to Timothy.

Paul uses the running-away verb four times, and never recommends standing and arguing with any
of them.

Joseph is the model, and he left his coat in the woman's hand. See the note on
Genesis 39:7–12.

## The second instruction

*And **run just as hard after what is right, after faith, after love, after peace**.*

The Greek is **dioke** — pursue, chase. The verb used for persecuting; it is what Saul used to
do.

Four things, listed. Righteousness, faith, love, peace.

Fleeing alone leaves a vacuum. Paul never gives the first verb without the second.

## The company

*And run **in the company of everyone who calls on the Lord out of a clean heart**.*

The Greek is **meta ton epikaloumenon** — with those calling on.

The pursuit is not solitary. Paul adds a clause about who else is running.

## The second half

*As for arguments that are **foolish and know nothing, have nothing to do with them**.*

The Greek is **moras kai apaideutous** — stupid and uneducated. And **paraitou** — refuse,
decline, beg off.

## The reason

*You understand perfectly well that **all they ever give birth to is a fight**.*

The Greek is **gennosin machas** — they beget battles.

Paul does not say the arguments are wrong. He says they are fertile in one direction only.

## What follows

*And a servant of the Lord **must not quarrel but be gentle to all, able to teach, patient, in
humility correcting those who are in opposition**, if God perhaps will grant them repentance,
so that they may know the truth.*

The Greek for *gentle* is **epion** — the word for a nurse with a child. Paul uses it of
himself in Thessalonians: *we were gentle among you, just as a nursing mother cherishes her
own children*.

## The letter's other instruction about words

*Be diligent to present yourself approved to God, a worker who does not need to be ashamed,
**rightly dividing the word of truth**. But **shun profane and idle babblings**, for they will
increase to more ungodliness.*

Same pattern: do this, avoid that.

> **The two directions.** Away from one thing and after four others, and the second list is
> longer. Paul never gives the fleeing without saying what to run toward.
