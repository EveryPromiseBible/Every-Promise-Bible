Philip asks for one thing that would settle everything, and the answer is that he
has been looking at it.

## The request

*Lord, show us the Father, and that will be enough for us.*

It is not a foolish thing to want. Moses asked to see God's glory. Philip is
asking for the same and saying it would be sufficient.

The pathos is in *that will be enough* — a man who thinks one more thing would
do it.

## The answer

*Have I been with you all this time, Philip, and you still do not know Me?
Anyone who has seen Me has seen the Father.*

Three years of walking, eating and watching. Jesus is not surprised at the
question so much as at who is asking it.

And the claim is unqualified: seeing Him **is** seeing the Father. Not a
resemblance, not a good representation.

## The two grounds He offers

*The words I speak to you I do not speak on My own; it is the Father, living in
Me, who is doing His work.*

Then: *believe Me when I tell you… or else believe because of the works
themselves.*

He gives them two ways in — take His word for it, or reason back from what they
have watched Him do. He does not insist on the first.

## What this settles

Everything a person guesses about God's character has to be checked against this
face. If Jesus is what God looks like, then the disposition on display in the
Gospels — toward lepers, tax collectors, the bent-over woman, the thief on the
cross — is the Father's disposition.

There is nothing behind Jesus that is different from Jesus.

> **Where the chapter opens.** *Do not let your hearts be troubled… I am going to
> prepare a place for you.* Philip's question comes minutes later, on the same
> night, from a man who has just been told the way and asked where it goes. The
> whole conversation is Jesus explaining Himself to people about to lose Him.
