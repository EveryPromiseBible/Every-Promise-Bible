The centre of the book, and it is the only part that looks up.

## What the book is

Five poems over the ruins of Jerusalem, written by an eyewitness to the siege. The city
is burned, the temple is gone, the walls are down, and the poems describe famine so
severe that mothers ate their children.

Chapters 1, 2, 4 and 5 are unrelieved.

## Where this sits

Chapter 3 is the middle poem, and it is the longest — sixty-six verses, an acrostic with
three lines to each Hebrew letter.

The turn comes at verse 21, and this passage is on the other side of it.

## What comes just before

*This I recall to my mind, therefore I have hope: **through the LORD's mercies we are not
consumed, because His compassions fail not. They are new every morning; great is Your
faithfulness**.*

Written by a man standing in the ash of his own city.

## The first line

*For the Lord **will not cast off forever**.*

The Hebrew is **zanach** — to reject, to cast away.

He does not deny the casting off. He puts a limit on it.

## The concession

*Though **He brings grief**.*

Stated plainly, in the same sentence. The poet does not attribute the disaster to
accident or to Babylon alone.

## The turn

*He will **gather you back in compassion**.*

The Hebrew is **racham** — the verb from the word for a womb. Deep, physical mercy.

## The measure

*For His **unfailing love is past all counting**.*

The Hebrew is **chesed** — covenant loyalty — and *the multitude of His mercies*.

## The two negatives

*He **does not wound from His heart**, nor **bring grief to the children of men because He
wills it**.*

The Hebrew of the first is *He does not afflict **from His heart***.

Two statements about motive, in a book whose subject is a judgment nobody disputes was
deserved.

The poet has already said God did it. Here he says where it did not come from.

## The word for "children of men"

**Bene ish** — sons of man.

Not *sons of Israel*. He widens it to the human race.

## What the poem says a few verses on

*Who is he who speaks and it comes to pass, when the Lord has not commanded it? Is it not
from the mouth of the Most High that woe and well-being proceed?*

And then: *why should a living man complain, a man for the punishment of his sins? Let us
search out and examine our ways, and turn back to the LORD.*

The poet holds both — that God did it, and that it is not what He wants.

## What Jeremiah had spent his life doing

Warning the city for forty years, being put in a cistern for it, and being told at the
start that nobody would listen.

He is the man who wept over it and the man who said it was coming.

## The other line from this chapter

*It is good that one should hope and wait quietly for the salvation of the LORD.*

> **Where the sentence stands.** In the middle of five poems about a burned city, by
> somebody who saw it — and what he says is not that it did not happen, but that it did
> not come from the heart.
