One verse, and the whole argument turns on a single word in a quotation.

## What has just happened

The writer has quoted Jeremiah 31 in full — the longest Old Testament passage cited anywhere in
the New Testament.

*Behold, the days are coming… when I will make **a new covenant** with the house of Israel.* See the
note on Jeremiah 31:31–34.

## The observation

*By calling this one **new**, He has made the first one **old**.*

The Greek is **en to legein kainen pepalaioken ten proten**.

**Kainos** — new in kind, not merely recent.

**Palaioo** — to make old, to render obsolete. Perfect tense: it stands done.

## The argument

Not that the old covenant failed morally.

That the announcement of a replacement is itself the verdict. If a new one is promised, the first
was not final — and the promise is in the old covenant's own Scriptures.

Jeremiah made the case six hundred years before the letter was written.

## The two adjectives

*And whatever is **wearing out and growing old**.*

The Greek is **palaioumenon kai geraskon**.

**Gerasko** — to grow elderly. The word for a person ageing.

The second verb personifies it.

## The verdict

*Is **on the point of vanishing for good**.*

The Greek is **engus aphanismou** — near to disappearance.

**Aphanismos** — vanishing, obliteration. It occurs once in the New Testament.

## The tense

Present. *Is growing old*, *is near*.

Not gone. The letter is written while the temple is still standing and the sacrifices are still
being offered.

Within a few years of this being written, the temple was destroyed and the sacrificial system
stopped permanently.

## What the letter is arguing

*But now He has obtained a more excellent ministry, inasmuch as **He is also Mediator of a better
covenant**, which was established on better promises.* See the note on
Hebrews 8:6–7.

*For if that first covenant had been faultless, then no place would have been sought for a
second.*

## Where the fault was

*Because **finding fault with them**, He says: "Behold, the days are coming…"*

The Greek is **memphomenos autois** — finding fault with them.

With them, not with it. The covenant is described as faultless in itself; the parties were the
problem.

## What the readers were tempted to do

Go back.

The letter is written to people under pressure to return to a system with a temple, a priesthood
and visible sacrifices, and it argues that the shadow cannot compete with the substance.

*Let us therefore come boldly to the throne of grace.* See the note on
Hebrews 4:14–16.

## Where the argument arrives

*By one offering **He has perfected forever those who are being sanctified**.* See the note on
Hebrews 10:14.

*Now where there is remission of these, **there is no longer an offering for sin**.* See the note on
Hebrews 10:18.

> **The argument.** Not that the old covenant failed — that the promise of a new one, made inside
> its own Scriptures, was already the verdict.
