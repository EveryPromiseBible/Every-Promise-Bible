key: Lamentations 1:12
title: "Is it nothing to you, all you who pass by"
updated: 2026-08-09
---
The most quoted line in the book, and it is addressed to people walking past.

## Who is speaking

Jerusalem, personified as a woman, in the middle of the first poem.

*Is it nothing to you, **all you who pass by**?*

The Hebrew is **kol overei derekh** — all who pass along the way. Not enemies. Traffic.

The complaint is not about cruelty. It is about being walked past.

## The request

*Look and see if there is **any sorrow like my sorrow**.*

**Im-yesh makh'ov kemakh'ovi** — if there is any pain like my pain.

**Makh'ov** is the word Isaiah uses of the Servant: *a man of **sorrows**, and acquainted with
grief.*

## Where the passers-by turn up again

*And those who **passed by** blasphemed Him, wagging their heads and saying, "You who destroy the
temple and build it in three days, save Yourself!"*

**Matthew** 27, and the Greek is **hoi paraporeuomenoi** — the ones going past. The same category
of person, at the same city, standing in front of a different sorrow and doing the same thing.

The verse has been read of **Christ** at least since the earliest Latin liturgies, and it is sung
in the Reproaches on Good Friday and in the *Messiah*. The reason is not allegory. It is that
**Matthew** put the passers-by there.

## Who laid it on

*The sorrow **He has laid on me**, the sorrow the LORD poured out on the day of His fierce anger.*

The poet does not blame Babylon. He names the source, and he is not disputing that it was
deserved — *the LORD is righteous, yet I rebelled against His word* comes six verses later.

*Yet it pleased the LORD to bruise Him; He has put Him to grief… **the LORD has laid on Him the
iniquity of us all**.*

Isaiah's verb is **hiphgia** — caused it to meet Him. In Lamentations 1 the sorrow is laid on
somebody who admits the charge. In Isaiah 53 it is laid on somebody who does not have one.

## What the city wanted

To be looked at.

*"**Behold the Man!**"*

*"And I, if I am **lifted up** from the earth, will draw all peoples to Myself."*

**John** 12, and the Greek verb is **hypsotho** — raised up, which He then explains: *this He said,
signifying by what death He would die.* The answer to *look and see* turns out to be a Person
raised where nobody can walk past without seeing.

> **The complaint.** Not that anybody hurt her — that they did not stop. And the one place in
> Scripture the same phrase reappears, Matthew has the passers-by at Golgotha, wagging their
> heads.
