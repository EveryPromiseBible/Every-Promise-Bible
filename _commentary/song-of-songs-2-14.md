One verse, and the whole of it is a request to be let in.

## Where she is

*My dove **in the clefts of the rock**, in **the hidden places of the cliff**.*

The Hebrew is **bechagvei hasela** — in the crevices of the rock. And **beseter hammadregah**, in
the secret place of the steep path.

Rock doves nest in cliff faces, in cracks a hand cannot reach.

He is describing somebody who has gone somewhere inaccessible.

## What has just happened

Two verses earlier the finest lines in the book: *rise up, my love, my fair one, and come away.
For lo, the winter is past, the rain is over and gone.*

An invitation to come out.

And this is the same speech, addressed to somebody who has not come out yet.

## The two requests

*Let me **see your face**, let me **hear your voice**.*

The Hebrew is **mar'ayik** and **qolek** — your appearance and your voice.

Face and voice, in that order, and then the reasons come back in reverse order.

## The two reasons

*For **your voice is sweet** and **your face is lovely**.*

The Hebrew is **arev** — pleasant, sweet. And **naveh** — comely, becoming.

The chiasm is deliberate: see your face, hear your voice, your voice is sweet, your face is
lovely.

## Why the request is remarkable

He is not asking her to change anything.

The reason given for wanting to see her face is that her face is lovely, and he says so to
somebody hiding in a cliff.

She has already described herself in chapter 1: *I am dark, but lovely… do not look upon me,
because I am dark, because the sun has tanned me*.

The hiding and the self-assessment are connected, and the passage does not argue with either.
It just asks.

## What he calls her

*My dove.*

He uses it repeatedly. Doves were the offering of the poor — the pair Joseph and Mary brought at
the presentation, because they could not afford a lamb. See the note on
Luke 2:33–35.

## The clefts

The image turns up elsewhere.

Moses, asking to see God's glory: *I will put you **in the cleft of the rock**, and will cover
you with My hand while I pass by*.

The Hebrew word there is different, but the picture is the same — a person in a crack in a
cliff, being addressed.

Whether the Song intends the echo is unprovable. It is the same landscape.

## What follows immediately

*Catch the foxes for us, the little foxes that spoil the vineyards.* See the note on
Song of Songs 2:15.

The tender request is followed by pest control, and the poem does not explain the transition.

## The verse before her answer

*My beloved is mine, and I am his. He feeds his flock among the lilies.*

The whole exchange resolves into possession, both ways.

## Where the same want turns up

*Those are the worshipers **the Father is looking for**.* See the note on
John 4:20–24.

The seeking, in that sentence, is His.

> **What he asks for.** Not that she come out and be seen — he asks to see her, in the place she
> is hiding, and gives as his reason that her face is lovely.
