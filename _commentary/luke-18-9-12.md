---
key: Luke 18:9–12
title: Two men went up to pray
updated: 2026-08-07
---

Luke names the audience before the story, and everything in the Pharisee's prayer is true.

## The target

*For the benefit of some who were **confident of their own goodness** and **looked down on everybody
else**.*

The Greek is **tous pepoithotas eph' heautois hoti eisin dikaioi** — those persuaded about
themselves that they were righteous.

And **exouthenountas tous loipous** — despising the rest.

## The two halves

Confidence in oneself, and contempt for others.

Luke presents them as one disposition with two faces.

## The setting

*"Two men **went up to the temple** to pray."*

The Greek is **anebēsan** — went up. The temple mount is uphill from anywhere in Jerusalem.

Both men are religiously observant enough to be there at the hour of prayer.

## The two

*"One was **a Pharisee** and the other **a tax collector**."*

The best and the worst, by the reckoning of the audience.

A Pharisee was a serious man. A **telōnēs** collected for Rome and was barred from giving evidence
in court.

## The posture

*"The Pharisee **stood up and stood by himself** and prayed."*

The Greek is **statheis pros heauton** — having stood, toward himself.

The phrase is ambiguous and both readings have been argued: he stood apart, or he prayed to
himself.

## The opening

*"**God, I thank You**."*

It is a thanksgiving, correctly addressed. Nothing formally wrong with it.

## The list

*"That **I am not like other people** — the thieves, the crooks, the adulterers."*

Three categories, and then a fourth.

## The fourth

*"And I am certainly not like **this tax collector over here**."*

The Greek is **ē kai hōs houtos ho telōnēs**.

The man is in the room. The prayer points at him.

## The practices

*"**I fast twice a week. I give a tenth of everything I take in**."*

Both go beyond requirement.

The law commanded one fast a year, on the Day of Atonement. Twice a week is voluntary.

Tithing applied to produce. Tithing everything acquired is more than the law asks.

## What is not false

Nothing.

He is not a thief, an adulterer, or a tax collector. He does fast and tithe. The prayer contains no
lie.

## What is wrong with it

The pronoun.

Five first-person references in two sentences, and the only thing God is thanked for is the
speaker's difference from other people.

## The other man

*"But the tax collector, standing afar off, would not so much as raise his eyes to heaven, but beat
his breast, saying, '**God, be merciful to me a sinner**.'"* See the note on Luke 18:13–14.

The Greek is **hilasthēti moi** — be propitiated toward me.

Not the ordinary word for mercy. A sacrificial word: let the atonement cover me.

## Where he was standing

Afar off, in the temple, where the atonement sacrifice was being made.

## The verdict

*"I tell you, **this man went down to his house justified rather than the other**."*

The Greek is **dedikaiōmenos** — perfect passive participle.

## The closing line

*"For everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and **he who humbles himself will be exalted**."*

The same sentence closes a different parable earlier in Luke, about seats at a wedding.

## What comes next in the chapter

Infants brought to Him, and *whoever does not receive the kingdom of God as a little child will by no
means enter it*.

And then a ruler asking *what shall I do to inherit eternal life?* See the note on Luke 18:18–23.

The rich ruler's answer to the commandments is *all these things I have kept from my youth*, which
is the Pharisee's prayer in a different form.

## The word the tax collector uses

*"God, **be merciful to me** a sinner!"*

The Greek is not the ordinary word for mercy. It is **hilastheti** — *be propitiated toward me*, a
passive imperative from the same root as the mercy seat.

He is not asking to be let off. He is asking that the thing which stands between them be dealt
with, and the verb assumes a sacrifice.

*Whom God set forth as **a propitiation by His blood**, through faith… that He might be just and the
justifier of the one who has faith in **Jesus**.*

**Paul**'s noun in **Romans** 3 is **hilasterion** — the same root, and the Greek Old Testament's word
for the lid of the ark, sprinkled once a year with blood.

The man in the temple asks for it. **Romans** 3 says where it was provided.

## What the verdict word is

*"**I tell you, this man went down to his house justified rather than the other.**"*

**Dedikaiomenos** — a perfect passive participle. Justified, by somebody else, and it stays done.

It is the verb **Paul** builds Romans and Galatians on, and **Luke** has **Jesus** using it of a man
who has just said seven words and hit himself in the chest.

*Being **justified freely by His grace** through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus.*

*But to him who does not work but believes on Him who **justifies the ungodly**, his faith is
accounted for righteousness.*

**Romans** 4's phrase is the parable in three words. Not the man who stopped being ungodly — **the
ungodly**.

## What is not disputed

Every claim in the Pharisee's prayer. He is not a thief, he does fast twice a week, he does tithe
everything.

*But what things were gain to me, these **I have counted loss for Christ**… and count them as rubbish,
that I may gain Christ and be found in Him, **not having my own righteousness**.*

**Paul** had the Pharisee's list — he itemises it in **Philippians** 3 — and describes what he did
with it.

> **The audit.** Every claim in the prayer is true. He is not a thief, he does fast, he does tithe.
> The parable does not dispute a word of it.
