Luke was there for this one — the *we* in the passage is his — and he tells it
with a doctor's eye and a slight glint of humour.

## The setting

*On the first day of the week, when the disciples came together to break bread.*

One of the earliest indications of Christians meeting on Sunday, and of what they
met for.

*There were many lamps burning in the upper room.* Luke mentions it because it
matters: many oil lamps in a crowded upper room, at night, burning the air. A
young man in a window is looking for cooler air.

## Paul talking

*He was leaving the next day, and he kept on talking until midnight.*

Luke does not defend him. The word he uses for the sinking sleep is a medical
term — being borne down, overcome by degrees. Eutychus does not doze off
carelessly; he is gradually overwhelmed.

## The fall

*He fell from the third floor, and **they picked him up dead**.*

Luke, a physician, states it flatly. Not *as though dead*. Dead.

## What Paul does

*Paul went down, bent over him, and took him in his arms.*

The posture is Elijah's over the widow's son, and Elisha's over the Shunammite's
boy — stretching himself on the child. Paul repeats an old gesture.

*"Do not be alarmed. His life is in him."*

## What happens next

*Then he went back up, broke bread and ate, and talked with them a long while,
until daybreak, and then he left.*

He resumes. The meeting continues, the bread is broken, and the preaching runs
till dawn.

The boy is mentioned once more, in the last line: *they brought the young man
home alive, and were greatly comforted.*

## His name

**Eutychus** — from *eu*, well, and *tuche*, fortune. It means **good fortune**,
**fortunate**, **lucky**.

It was a common slave name in the Greek world; owners gave it the way a person might
name a child Prosper. Luke, who names very few of the people in these travel
sections, names this one.

A boy called Good Fortune falls asleep, drops three floors, and is picked up dead.
Then he is carried home alive, and the name holds after all.

## The three floors

Luke marks the height twice — the lamps in the upper room, and *he fell from the third
floor*. He wants the reader to know how far it was.

## Sleep

The Greek moves through two words. First **katapheromenos** — being borne down,
sinking by degrees, the medical term for going under. Then **hupnos**, deep sleep.

Luke is a doctor describing a descent in stages, and then a fall.

## Why they were there

*"**Now on the first day of the week, when the disciples came together to break bread.**"*

The earliest reference in Scripture to Christians meeting on Sunday, and the stated purpose is a
meal.

*For as often as you eat this bread and drink this cup, **you proclaim the Lord's death till He
comes**.*

## What Paul does over the body

*But **Paul** went down, fell on him, and embracing him said, "**Do not trouble yourselves, for his
life is in him.**"*

The posture is Elijah's over the widow's son and Elisha's over the Shunammite's — stretched out on
the body.

*And they brought the young man in alive, **and they were not a little comforted**.*

**Luke** ends the paragraph with the understatement and does not preach it. The point of the chapter
is elsewhere.

## Where the chapter goes

*"**But none of these things move me; nor do I count my life dear to myself, so that I may finish my
race with joy, and the ministry which I received from the Lord Jesus, to testify to the gospel of
the grace of God.**"*

**Paul**'s farewell to the Ephesian elders, twenty verses later, and it is his own summary of what he
was doing all night: *the gospel of the grace of God*.

*"**And now, brethren, I commend you to God and to the word of His grace, which is able to build you
up and give you an inheritance among all those who are sanctified.**"*

> **What Luke leaves out.** Any comment. No sermon on the meaning of it, no
> rebuke of the boy, no reflection on the length of the sermon. A death, a
> raising, and then breakfast — reported by a man who was in the room.
