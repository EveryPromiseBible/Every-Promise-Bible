The rule for touching the dead, and it is the reason for the ash of the red heifer.

## What comes before

The red heifer, killed outside the camp, burnt entire with cedar, hyssop and scarlet, and the
ashes gathered and stored *for the water of purification*. See the note on
Numbers 19:1–10.

The ash existed for one purpose, and this passage is it.

## The rule

*"Whoever **touches a dead body** is **unclean for seven days**."*

Not sinful. Unclean, and the code is careful about the difference.

Death was the strongest source of ritual impurity in the system. A corpse, a grave, a bone, or a
tent where somebody had died.

## Why it mattered so much

Because it made ordinary life difficult. Somebody had to bury the dead, and somebody had to sit
with them.

The high priest and a Nazirite were forbidden to go near a corpse at all — even a parent's.
Which is what Samson's Nazirite vow was, and he ate honey out of a lion's carcass. See the note
on Judges 14:12–14.

## The remedy

*"On the **third day and the seventh** he is to be cleansed with the water, and then he is
clean."*

Two applications, and the days are specified.

The water is the ash of the heifer mixed with running water, sprinkled with a bunch of hyssop.

## The refusal

*"**If he refuses**, uncleanness clings to him."*

The Hebrew is *if he does not purify himself*.

The remedy is available and can be declined. Nothing in the passage forces anybody.

## The consequence

*"And he **defiles the dwelling of the LORD**; that person must be **cut off from Israel**."*

The Hebrew is **karat** — cut off, severed.

A man in that condition, walking around, is a problem for the whole camp — not for himself
alone.

## The reason

*"For **the water of cleansing was not sprinkled on him**."*

The passage states the cause in the last clause. Not the touching. The not being sprinkled.

The uncleanness was ordinary and expected. The failure was in not using what had been provided.

## The oddity of the whole rite

Everybody involved in making the ash becomes unclean — the priest who supervises, the man who
burns it, the man who gathers it.

The ash that cleanses others defiles everyone who handles it. The rabbis listed it among the
statutes that cannot be reasoned out.

## What Hebrews does with it

*For if the blood of bulls and goats, and **the ashes of a heifer, sprinkling the unclean,
sanctifies for the purifying of the flesh**, how much more shall the blood of Christ… **cleanse
your conscience from dead works** to serve the living God?*

The letter names this rite specifically, and puts *dead works* against a rule about dead
bodies.

The old rite reached the flesh. The claim is that this one reaches the conscience.

## The hyssop

*Purge me with hyssop, and I shall be clean; wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow.*

David, in Psalm 51, using the vocabulary of this rite about something the rite did not cover.

## Where the phrase goes

*Let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith, **having our hearts sprinkled
from an evil conscience**, and our bodies washed with pure water.* See the note on
Hebrews 10:22.

Sprinkled and washed, in one sentence, and both perfect passives.

> **What the last clause names as the failure.** Not the contact with death, which was ordinary
> and unavoidable. *The water of cleansing was not sprinkled on him* — the remedy was there and
> was not used.
