The same question as at the start of the chapter, asked again with one word changed.

## The two questions

Verse 1: *shall we continue in sin **that grace may abound**?* See the note on
Romans 6:1–2.

Verse 15: *shall we sin **because we are not under law but under grace**?*

The first is about the purpose. The second is about the permission.

Paul answers both, and the answers are different.

## Where the second one comes from

The verse immediately before it: *for **sin shall not have dominion over you**, for you are
not under law but under grace*.

He makes a promise, and then asks the question the promise invites.

## The answer

*Certainly not.*

**Me genoito** — the strongest negative in his vocabulary, and the tenth of the fourteen
times he uses it.

## The principle

*Do you not know that when you **hand yourselves over to someone as obedient slaves, you are
the slaves of the one you obey**?*

The Greek is **paristanete** — to present, to place at someone's disposal. The same verb he
has just used in verse 13: *present yourselves to God*.

The argument is about what obedience does to a person, not about what is permitted.

## The two options

*Either **sin, with death at the end of it**, or **obedience, with righteousness at the end
of it**.*

The Greek is *sin unto death, or obedience unto righteousness*.

There are two, and the question of which master is not on the table — only which one is
being obeyed.

## What is not offered

Freedom in the abstract. Nobody in the passage is unowned.

Paul's whole argument for six chapters has been that a person is in Adam or in Christ, under
law or under grace, a slave of sin or a slave of righteousness.

## The apology

*I speak in human terms because of the weakness of your flesh.*

Verse 19, and Paul flags the metaphor as imperfect. See the note on Romans 6:17–19.

He is uncomfortable calling it slavery, and uses it anyway because nothing else conveys that
the ownership is total.

## The asymmetry

The two slaveries are not equivalent, and the chapter says so at the end: *the wages of sin
is death, but **the gift of God is eternal life***.

One pays wages. The other gives a gift. Paul breaks his own parallel deliberately in the
last verse.

## What has already been established

*Knowing this, that our old man was crucified with Him… for **he who has died has been freed
from sin**.* See the note on Romans 6:6–7.

The freedom is stated as past before the question about permission is asked.

Paul is not warning them that sinning will re-enslave them. He is describing what obedience
reveals about who is being served.

## The tense of it

The first half of the chapter is indicative — this is what happened to you.

The second half is imperative — therefore present yourselves.

Verse 11 is the hinge: *reckon yourselves to be dead indeed to sin*. The command is to count
the fact as a fact.

## Where it ends

*But now **having been set free from sin, and having become slaves of God**, you have your
fruit to holiness, and the end, everlasting life.*

Two perfect participles. Both already done to them.

> **What the question is really about.** Not whether it is allowed. Paul answers a question
> about permission with a question about ownership — you belong to whichever one you are
> obeying.
