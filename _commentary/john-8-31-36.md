The most quoted line in this passage — *the truth will set you free* — is
attached to a condition on one side and an argument on the other, and both get
cut off when it is quoted alone.

## The condition

*If you hold to My word, then you really are My disciples. And you will know the
truth…*

The verb means to remain, to stay put, to keep living somewhere. Freedom is
promised to people who stay in what He said, not to people who encounter it once.

## The claim that gives the game away

*We are Abraham's descendants, and we have never been anyone's slaves.*

They were speaking in a country under Roman occupation, as descendants of people
who had been slaves in Egypt, and after Babylon. The sentence is historically
false three times over, and they say it without blinking.

That is the whole diagnosis. A person can be quite certain they are free while
standing inside the thing that holds them, and the certainty is part of the
condition.

## What Jesus says the slavery is

*Everyone who commits sin is a slave of sin.* Not "does wrong things
occasionally" — the construction is about a settled practice.

Then the family image: *a slave does not stay in the house forever, but a son
belongs there forever*. Slaves in a household could be sold; sons could not be
un-sonned. He is contrasting two kinds of belonging, and only one of them is
permanent.

> **"Free indeed."** The word is *ontos* — really, actually, in fact. Jesus adds
> it because He has just been told by the people in front of Him that they were
> free already. The adverb is aimed at that claim: not free in the way you think
> you are. Free in fact.
