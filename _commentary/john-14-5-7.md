The most quoted sentence in the chapter is an answer to a man admitting he is lost.

## The question

*Thomas said to Him, "**Lord, we do not know where You are going. How can we know the
way?**"*

He is the one who says it out loud. The others were presumably thinking it — Jesus has just
said *where I go you know, and the way you know*, and Thomas contradicts Him flatly.

## Who Thomas is

The same man who had said, a chapter earlier, when Jesus announced He was going back to
Judea where they had tried to stone Him: *let us also go, that we may die with Him*.

Brave and blunt, in that order.

And later: *unless I see in His hands the print of the nails… I will not believe*. And then
*my Lord and my God!* — the highest confession anybody makes in the Gospel.

Every time he appears he says the thing nobody else will.

## The answer

*"**I am the way, and the truth, and the life**."*

The Greek is **ego eimi** — the seventh and last of the great *I am* sayings with a
predicate, in a Gospel that has been building them: the bread, the light, the door, the good
shepherd, the resurrection, the vine.

## Three nouns

Not three qualities. Three definite articles: *the* way, *the* truth, *the* life.

Thomas asked one question, about the way. He gets three answers, and the second and third
explain the first.

## The name for the first Christians

*The Way.* Before anybody said *Christian*, that is what the movement was called.

Saul went to Damascus *so that if he found any who were of the Way, whether men or women, he
might bring them bound to Jerusalem*.

And Paul before Felix: *according to the Way which they call a sect, so I worship the God of
my fathers*.

They took the name from this sentence.

## The exclusive clause

*"**No one comes to the Father except through Me**."*

The Greek is **oudeis** — nobody.

Said in an upper room to eleven friends, not shouted at a crowd. It is a statement about
access, and the same discourse has just opened with *in My Father's house are many
mansions… I go to prepare a place for you*.

## The destination

The Father. Every sentence in the paragraph is oriented that way.

Thomas asked about a road. The answer names the address.

## The consequence

*"**If you had known Me, you would have known My Father as well**."*

The Greek is a contrary-to-fact condition — you have not, and so you did not.

## The turn

*"**From now on you do know Him, and you have seen Him**."*

The Greek is **ap' arti** — from this moment.

Something changes as He says it. The sentence before is about their ignorance; the sentence
after says the ignorance is over.

## What Philip does with it

*"Lord, **show us the Father**, and it is sufficient for us."*

And the answer: *have I been with you so long, and yet you have not known Me, Philip? **He
who has seen Me has seen the Father**.* See the note on John 14:8–11.

Two disciples in four verses, both asking for something they are looking at.

## What the chapter has already promised

*"Let not your heart be troubled; you believe in God, believe also in Me."*

And what it promises next: *I will pray the Father, and He will give you another Helper*.
See the note on John 14:15–17.

> **Who the sentence was for.** A man who had just said out loud that he did not know where
> they were going. The most exclusive claim in the Gospel is an answer to somebody admitting
> he was lost.
