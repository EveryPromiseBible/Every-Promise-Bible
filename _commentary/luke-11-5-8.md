The parable is not about a reluctant God. It is about a man who is not asking for himself.

## Where it sits

Immediately after the Lord's Prayer. The disciples asked how to pray; He gave them the prayer and
then this. See the note on Luke 11:1–4.

## The setup

*"Suppose one of you has a friend, and you go to him **in the middle of the night**."*

## The request

*"Friend, **lend me three loaves of bread**."*

Three small flat loaves. A single meal for one man.

## The reason

*"**A friend of mine has arrived at my house off a journey**, and **I have nothing to set in front of
him**."*

## Who the bread is for

Not the man knocking.

He is not hungry. He is hosting, and hospitality in that culture was not optional — a guest arriving
unfed was a disgrace on the whole village.

## The refusal

*"**Do not bother me. The door is already bolted, and my children are in bed with me. I cannot get up
and give you anything**."*

A one-room peasant house. The family sleeps on a raised platform together. The door is barred with a
wooden beam through iron rings.

Getting up wakes everybody.

## What the excuse is not

Not unkindness. It is a straightforward description of the cost.

## The turn

*"Even if he will not get up and help him **for friendship's sake**, **he will get up because of the
man's sheer persistence**."*

The Greek is **anaideian** — from **a-**, not, and **aidōs**, shame. Shamelessness.

## The disputed word

**Anaideia** appears once in the New Testament, and the arguments over whose shamelessness it is
have run for centuries.

Read of the asker: he keeps knocking past the point of embarrassment.

Read of the sleeper: he gets up to avoid the shame of being the man who would not open.

## Either way

The bread comes.

## The amount

*"And give him **as much as he needs**."*

The Greek is **hosōn chrēzei**.

He asked for three loaves. He is given as many as the situation requires.

## The logic of the parable

Not comparison. Contrast.

If a tired neighbour behind a bolted door eventually hands over the bread, the argument runs, then
what is the Father like?

## What follows immediately

*"So I say to you, **ask, and it will be given to you; seek, and you will find; knock, and it will be
opened**."* See the note on Luke 11:9–13.

The Greek verbs are present imperatives: keep asking, keep seeking, keep knocking.

## And then

*"If a son asks for bread from any father among you, will he give him a stone?… **How much more will
your heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit** to those who ask Him!"* See the note on Luke 11:9–13.

Matthew has *good things*. Luke has *the Holy Spirit*.

## The other parable Luke pairs with it

*"There was in a certain city a judge who **did not fear God nor regard man**."* And a widow who wore
him down. See the note on Luke 18:1–5.

Same structure: an unwilling figure, a persistent asker, and the point made by contrast.

Luke says why he told it: *that men always ought to pray and not lose heart*.

## Where the bread came from in the end

The man at the door has nothing to set in front of a guest. That is the whole engine of the parable.

*"**I am the bread of life. He who comes to Me shall never hunger.**"*

*"Give us this day **our daily bread**."*

The prayer **Jesus** has just taught, four verses earlier, asks for exactly what the man in the
parable is short of — and then the parable is told to explain the asking.

## The argument, and where it lands

*"**If you then, being evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your
heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit to those who ask Him!**"*

**Luke** ends the sequence three verses later, and where **Matthew** has *good things*, **Luke** names
one.

The parable's man gets bread by being a nuisance. The conclusion drawn is that God is not the
neighbour in the story — the comparison runs from the reluctant to the willing, and the gap is the
argument.

*Let us therefore **come boldly to the throne of grace**, that we may obtain mercy and find grace to
help in time of need.*

**Hebrews** 4's word is **parresia**, and the reason it gives is in the verse before: *we do not have
a High Priest who cannot sympathise with our weaknesses.*

## Who else was asked for bread at midnight

*Now it came to pass, as He sat at the table with them, that **He took bread, blessed and broke it,
and gave it to them**. Then their eyes were opened and they knew Him.*

**Luke** 24, at the end of the same gospel, in a house at the end of a day. The man who told this
parable is the one who ends up handing the bread over.

> **Whose need it is.** The man at the door is not hungry. He is out of bread for somebody who has
> just arrived at his house, and that is the whole reason he keeps knocking.
