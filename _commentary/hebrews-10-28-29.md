The writer defines the sin he has just warned about, and the definition is entirely about
how the sacrifice is treated.

## The comparison

*Anyone who **threw out the law of Moses died with no mercy shown him**, on the word of
**two or three witnesses**.*

Deuteronomy's standard for a capital case. No conviction on one voice — the same rule
that governed the cities of refuge. See the note on Numbers 35:16–21.

## The argument's shape

*How much **heavier a punishment** do you think a man will deserve.*

The writer uses this form repeatedly — the lesser to the greater. If that was true under
the old arrangement, how much more under this one.

## The three descriptions

Every one is about the Son and His blood:

**Walked all over the Son of God.** The Greek is **katapateo** — to trample underfoot. It
is the word used of salt that has lost its savour, and of pearls before swine.

**Treated the blood of the covenant as something common.** The Greek is **koinon** —
common, ordinary, unclean. It is the word Peter uses on the rooftop: *I have never eaten
anything common or unclean.*

To call the blood common is to rank it with the blood of bulls and goats, which the
writer has just said could never take sins away. See the note on Hebrews 10:3–4.

**Insulted the Spirit of grace.** The Greek is **enubrizo** — to treat with contempt, to
outrage. It appears once in the New Testament.

## The clause in the middle

*The blood **that made him holy**.*

The Greek is *by which he was sanctified*.

The writer says it about the person being warned. Whatever else the sentence does, it
does not describe someone the blood never touched.

## What is not on the list

Failure, weakness, relapse, or a particular sin. Nothing in the three descriptions is
about behaviour falling short.

All three are about the standing of the sacrifice.

## What the letter is arguing against

Going back to the temple. The offerings were still being made when this was written, a
few days' walk away.

A man returning to that altar is saying the one on Golgotha was not sufficient — which is
what *treating the blood as common* amounts to.

## What follows

*For we know Him who said, "**Vengeance is Mine, I will repay**," says the Lord. And
again, "The LORD will judge His people."*

*It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God.*

## And then

*But **recall the former days** in which, after you were illuminated, you endured a great
struggle with sufferings.*

The writer turns immediately to what they had already come through, and ends the chapter:

*But **we are not of those who draw back** to perdition, **but of those who believe** to
the saving of the soul.*

He puts himself and them in the second category.

## The pattern

Twice in the letter: a hard warning, followed at once by a statement that he does not
think it describes them. See the notes on Hebrews 6:4–6 and Hebrews 6:9–10.

> **What the three verbs have in common.** Trampling, calling common, insulting — every
> one is something done to the sacrifice rather than something done wrong by the person.
