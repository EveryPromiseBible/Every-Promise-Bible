He finishes, dresses, sits down, and asks whether anybody followed.

## The three actions

*When He had finished washing their feet, **He put His clothing back on and sat down again**.*

The Greek is **elaben ta himatia autou kai anepesen** — took His garments and reclined.

He had *laid aside His garments* to start. See the note on John 13:4–5.

The verbs are the ones John uses of Him elsewhere: *I lay down My life… **and I have power to take
it again***.

## The question

*"**Do you understand what I have just done for you?**"*

The Greek is **ginoskete** — do you know, do you perceive.

He had told Peter *you do not understand now what I am doing, but afterward you will*. This is
not the afterward; it is the first attempt.

## The titles

*"You call Me **Teacher and Lord**, and **you are right to, because that is what I am**."*

The Greek is **kalos legete, eimi gar** — you say well, for I am.

He does not deflect the titles. He confirms them, and then reasons from them.

## The argument

*"So **if I, your Lord and your Teacher, have washed your feet, then you also ought to wash one
another's feet**."*

The Greek is **opheilete** — you owe it, you are obliged.

The higher position is the premise. Because He is Lord and did it, they are obliged.

## The word

*"**I have given you an example**: do as I have done for you."*

The Greek is **hupodeigma** — a pattern, a specimen, something set out to be copied.

Not the word for a moral illustration. The word for a template.

## The two proverbs

*"**A servant is not greater than his master**, and **a messenger is not greater than the one who sent
him**."*

The Greek is **apostolos** — one sent. Its only appearance in John's Gospel, and it is used in its
plain sense.

## The blessing

*"Now that you know these things, **you are blessed if you do them**."*

The Greek is **makarioi este ean poiete auta** — blessed are you if you do them.

The knowing is assumed. The condition is on the doing.

It is one of only two beatitudes in John. The other is to Thomas: *blessed are those who have not
seen and yet have believed*.

## What He does immediately after

*"**Most assuredly, I say to you, one of you will betray Me**."*

The blessing, and then the betrayal, in consecutive verses.

Judas's feet had been in the basin.

## What He gives Judas

*"It is he to whom I shall give a piece of bread when I have dipped it."*

The Greek is **psomion** — a morsel.

Handing someone a dipped piece was a host's gesture of honour to a favoured guest.

## What follows the departure

*"**A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another; as I have loved you**."*

*"By this all will know that you are My disciples, if you have love for one another."* See the note
on John 13:31–35.

The instruction about feet becomes the new commandment two paragraphs later.

## What Paul does with the same logic

*Let this mind be in you which was also in Christ Jesus, who… **made Himself of no reputation,
taking the form of a bondservant**.* See the note on Philippians 2:5–8.

The Greek is **doulos** — slave. The one who washed feet.

> **The premise.** *If I, your Lord and your Teacher.* The obligation is built on the higher
> position, not on humility as a general virtue.
