They come down off the mountain into the worst scene in the chapter.

## The timing

*The **next day**, when they had come down from the hill.*

The hill is the transfiguration. See the note on Luke 9:28–36.

Moses, Elijah, the face changed, the voice from the cloud — and then this, the following
morning, at the bottom.

Raphael's last painting puts both scenes on one canvas for exactly this reason.

## The father

*A man in the crowd called out, "**Teacher, please, look at my son. He is my only child**."*

The Greek is **monogenes** — only-begotten. Luke uses the word three times, all of them of an
only child: the widow's son at Nain, Jairus' daughter, and this boy.

## The description

*"A spirit takes hold of him and he **suddenly screams**, and it **throws him into
convulsions until he is foaming**, and it **wears him down and will hardly let him go**."*

Mark's version adds that it has thrown him into fire and water, and that it has been
happening *from childhood*.

The Greek for *wears him down* is **suntribon** — bruising, crushing, shattering.

## What he had already tried

*"I begged Your disciples to drive it out, and **they could not**."*

Nine of them were at the bottom of the hill. Luke reports the failure without comment.

Mark adds that the scribes were arguing with them about it when Jesus arrived.

## The response

*"**What an unbelieving, twisted generation this is. How long must I be with you and put up
with this?**"*

The Greek is **diestrammene** — twisted, distorted, bent out of shape.

One of very few times He is recorded saying something like this. It is not aimed at the
father.

## The instruction

*"**Bring your son here to Me**."*

Four words after the exclamation, and the whole thing turns on them.

## What happened on the way

*Even as the boy was coming, the **demon threw him to the ground and shook him violently**.*

The last attack happens in transit, in front of everybody.

## The cure

*But Jesus **spoke sharply to the unclean spirit, healed the boy**.*

The Greek is **epetimesen** — rebuked. One word to the spirit, and Luke does not record what
was said.

## The last clause

*And **gave him back to his father**.*

The Greek is **apedoken** — gave back, restored, returned.

Luke uses the same construction at Nain: *and He presented him to his mother*.

The healing is not the end of the sentence. The handing over is.

## What the crowd did

*Everyone there was **astonished at the greatness of God**.*

The Greek is **megaleioteti** — the majesty.

## What Mark records the father saying

*"If You can do anything, have compassion on us and help us."*

*"**If you can believe**, all things are possible to him who believes."*

*"**Lord, I believe; help my unbelief!**"*

The most honest sentence anybody says in the Gospels, and the boy is healed immediately
after it.

## The private question

The disciples ask afterwards why they could not do it.

Matthew: *because of your unbelief… if you have faith as a mustard seed, you will say to
this mountain, "Move from here to there," and it will move.*

Mark: *this kind can come out by nothing but prayer and fasting.*

## What He says while they are still marvelling

*"Let these words sink down into your ears, for **the Son of Man is about to be betrayed
into the hands of men**."*

Luke puts it in the same breath as the crowd's astonishment. The sentence begins in verse 43
and finishes in 44.

*But they did not understand this saying… and they were afraid to ask Him.*

> **Where the account ends.** Not with the boy standing up. *And gave him back to his
> father* — Luke does the same at Nain, and both times the last clause is a handing over.
