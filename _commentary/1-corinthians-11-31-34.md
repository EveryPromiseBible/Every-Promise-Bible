*If we judged ourselves, we would not be judged. But when we are judged, we are
chastened by the Lord.* Three judgment words in two lines — and in the Greek they
are not all the same word.

## The first one is a different verb

*If we **judged** ourselves…* — **diakrino**. To distinguish, to separate, to
tell one thing apart from another.

It is the same verb Paul used two verses earlier in *not **discerning** the
Lord's body*, and the same one Jesus used in Mark 11 for the man who *does not
doubt* — is not divided — in his heart.

So the first clause is not about interrogating yourself for sin. It is the verb
of telling things apart, applied to yourself at that table: judging yourself
healed because of a body broken for you, and forgiven because of blood poured out
for you.

## The second and third are another verb

*…we would not be **judged**. But when we are **judged**, we are chastened by the
Lord.* — **krino**, both times.

Two different Greek verbs, rendered by one English word, in a single sentence.
The distinction is invisible in translation and it is the whole hinge of the
passage.

## And Paul says what the second word means

He does not leave it to be guessed. He defines it in the same breath:

*We are **chastened** by the Lord, **so that we would not be condemned along with
the world**.*

The chastening and the condemning are set against each other, not stacked
together. Whatever *krino* is doing here, Paul's own clause rules out its being
sentence and condemnation — that is what the world is under, and he says so
explicitly.

## "Chastened"

The Greek is **paideuo**, from **pais**, a child. It is the vocabulary of raising
a son — training, instruction, correction inside a family.

Hebrews 12 uses the same word at length: *the Lord disciplines the one He loves…
no discipline seems pleasant at the time, but afterward it yields the peaceable
fruit of righteousness.* Correction, and a stated harvest. See the note on
Hebrews 12:5–11.

## And condemnation is off the table already

*…so that we would not be condemned along with the world.*

John 3 settles who is where: *whoever believes in Him is not condemned… he who
does not believe is condemned already.* The condemnation is the world's
condition, and it is described as a thing already in place.

Paul writes to keep the Corinthians clear of that, not to put them under it. See
the note on John 3:14–18.

## The practical instruction

*When you come together to eat, **wait for one another**. If anyone is hungry, let
him eat at home.*

After all of it — the warnings, the weakness, the sleeping — the remedy is five
words long, and it is about manners at a shared table.

Nobody is told to stay away.

> **The last line.** *And the rest I will set in order when I come.* Paul closes
> the subject the way a man closes a letter, not the way a judge closes a case.
