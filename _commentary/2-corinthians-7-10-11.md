Paul distinguishes two griefs that look identical from outside.

## What this is about

A letter Paul had sent that made them sorry — *for even if I made you sorry with my letter,
I do not regret it, though I did regret it.*

He had written something hard, worried about it, and then Titus brought back the news of how
it landed. See the note on 2 Corinthians 8:16–17.

## The first kind

*The sorrow that is **shaped by God**.*

The Greek is **kata theon lupe** — sorrow according to God.

Not sorrow about God, and not sorrow He inflicts. Sorrow shaped along His lines.

## What it does

*Turns a person around, **toward being saved**, and **leaves nothing to regret**.*

The Greek is **metanoian** — a change of mind. The word usually rendered *repentance*, and
it is a word about the mind turning rather than the feelings hurting.

**Ametameleton** — not to be regretted. The one sorrow nobody looks back on and wishes they
had skipped.

## The second kind

*But **the world's kind of sorrow produces death**.*

The Greek is **tou kosmou lupe** — the sorrow of the world.

Paul gives it one clause and no description. The result is the whole definition.

## The two men on the same night

Peter *went out and wept bitterly*, and was on a beach three days later.

Judas *was remorseful* — and the Greek there is **metamelomai**, a different word, the
regret one — *and he went and hanged himself*.

Both were sorry, and the same evening.

## What the good sorrow produced

*Just look at what this very thing has produced in you.*

Seven items, and Paul lists them fast:

*Such **earnestness**, such **eagerness to clear yourselves**, such **anger**, such
**fear**, such **longing**, such **passion**, such **readiness to see justice done**.*

The Greek opens each with **alla** — *but also, but also* — piling them up.

Every one is energy. Nothing on the list is passive, and nothing on it is misery.

## The verdict

*At every point you have **shown yourselves innocent** in this matter.*

The Greek is **hagnous** — pure, clear. The same root as the purifying in 1 John 3:3. See
the note on 1 John 3:2–3.

He is not still holding it against them. The chapter has already said *I have great boldness
of speech toward you, I have great pride in you*.

## The test in the passage

The difference is not the intensity of the feeling. Both sorrows hurt.

It is what is at the end of each: one arrives at a change of mind and salvation, and the
other arrives at death.

## What Paul says right before

*For when we came to Macedonia, our bodies had no rest, but we were troubled on every side.
Outside were conflicts, inside were fears. **Nevertheless God, who comforts the downcast,
comforted us by the coming of Titus**.*

Paul admits fear in the same chapter where he explains sorrow.

## What comes after

*Therefore we have been comforted in your comfort. And **we rejoiced exceedingly more for
the joy of Titus**, because his spirit has been refreshed by you all.*

The chapter ends: *I have confidence in you in everything*.

> **The difference between them.** Not how badly it hurts. Where it arrives — a turned mind,
> or death — and Paul says the first one is never regretted afterwards.
