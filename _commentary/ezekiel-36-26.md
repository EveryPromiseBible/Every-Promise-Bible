One verse, four clauses, and every verb is *I will*.

## Who is being addressed

Exiles in Babylon, whose city and temple have been destroyed and who know exactly why.

The chapter is blunt about it: *so I poured out My fury on them for the blood they had shed on
the land, and for their idols with which they had defiled it*.

## Why He says He is doing it

Not because they deserve it, and the chapter says so twice:

*"I do not do this for your sake, O house of Israel, but **for My holy name's sake**, which you
have profaned among the nations."*

And later: *"Not for your sake do I do this," says the Lord GOD, "let it be known to you. **Be
ashamed and confounded for your own ways**."*

The motive is stated, and it is His reputation among the watching nations.

## The first clause

*"**I will give you a new heart**."*

The Hebrew is **lev chadash**.

**Lev** — heart, and in Hebrew it is the seat of thought and will as much as feeling. Closer to
*mind* than the English word.

## The second

*"**I will put a new spirit within you**."*

**Ruach** — spirit, breath, wind.

Two things given, and both are internal.

## The third

*"**I will take out of you the heart of stone**."*

The Hebrew is **lev ha'even**.

Stone does not respond. It does not soften, absorb or change temperature quickly. It is inert.

And the law had been written on it. Two tablets, cut from rock, and the pun is not accidental —
Paul makes it explicit.

## The fourth

*"And **I will give you a heart of flesh, warm and living**."*

The Hebrew is **lev basar**.

Flesh is usually the negative term in Scripture. Here it is the improvement, because the
contrast is not flesh against spirit but flesh against stone.

Soft, warm, and able to feel.

## What follows

*"**I will put My Spirit within you and cause you to walk in My statutes**."*

The obedience is described as an outcome of the transplant rather than a condition for it.

## What David asked for

*Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a steadfast spirit within me.* See the note on
Psalms 51:10–12.

A king asks for it as a favour. A prophet is told it is coming as a policy.

## What Jeremiah has

*"I will put My law in their minds, and write it on their hearts; and I will be their God, and
they shall be My people."*

*"For I will forgive their iniquity, and their sin I will remember no more."*

Hebrews quotes that passage twice, at length. See the note on
Hebrews 8:10–12.

## What Paul does with the stone

*You are our letter, written on our hearts… **not on tablets of stone but on tablets that are
human hearts**.* See the note on 2 Corinthians 3:1–3.

He takes Ezekiel's contrast and applies it to a congregation, calling them the letter.

## Where the chapter ends

*"The desolate land shall be tilled instead of lying desolate in the sight of all who pass by.
So they will say, '**This land that was desolate has become like the garden of Eden**.'"*

And *I will increase them with men like a flock*.

> **Which way the contrast runs.** Not flesh against spirit. Flesh against *stone* — and the
> word for the improvement is the one usually used of the problem.
