A short domestic scene dropped into the middle of a long list of boundary lines,
and it is there because of what a young woman says.

## What she had been given

Land in the Negev — the dry south. It is a real inheritance and it is genuinely
hers, and without water it is close to useless.

The chapter has just described her father taking Hebron off three giants, so
nobody in this family is short of nerve. Achsah is her father's daughter.

## What she asks for

*Give me a blessing. You've given me land in the dry Negev — now give me springs
of water too.*

She names the gap precisely. Not more land, and not a complaint about what she
received. The specific thing that would make what she already has work.

And the detail the narrator keeps: *as she climbed down from her donkey*. She
dismounts to ask — the posture of someone making a formal request, and the sort
of small physical detail the Bible records when someone was actually there.

## What she gets

*So Caleb gave her the upper springs and the lower springs.*

Both. She asked for springs and he gave her the whole watershed, top and bottom.
No hesitation is recorded, and no negotiation.

## What she asked, and who she asked

*"**Give me a blessing.** Since you have given me land in the South, give me also **springs of
water**."*

She had been given the Negev — dry country. A field without water is a field you cannot use.

She does not complain about the gift and does not ask for a different one. She asks her father
for the thing that would make it work.

## What she got

*So he gave her **the upper springs and the lower springs**.*

She asked for springs. He gave her two sets.

Paul: *He who did not spare His own Son, but delivered Him up for us all, **how shall He not
with Him also freely give us all things?*** And Jesus, of the same Father:

*"If you then, being evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, **how much more will
your Father who is in heaven give good things to those who ask Him!**"*

## Where she asked from

Not as a stranger. As a daughter, of a father who had just handed her a field.

> **Why the story is in the book at all.** Judges tells it a second time, almost
> word for word — which means it was remembered and repeated. In a chapter that
> is otherwise surveyors' work, someone thought this was worth keeping: a woman
> who looked at a good gift, saw what it lacked, and asked her father for the
> rest.
