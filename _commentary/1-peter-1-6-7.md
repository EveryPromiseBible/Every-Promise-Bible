The reason for the joy is named, and then the trials are named, and Peter does not soften either.

## What has you glad

*That is what has you so glad.*

The Greek is **en ho agalliasthe** — in which you greatly rejoice.

**Agalliao** — to exult, to leap for joy. A strong word.

The antecedent is the previous paragraph: the living hope, the inheritance kept in heaven, and the
garrison round it. See the note on 1 Peter 1:3.

## The concession

*Even now, when **for a little while**, if it has to be this way.*

The Greek is **oligon arti** — a little now.

**Ei deon estin** — if it is necessary.

Peter does not claim it is short in itself. He calls it short against what he has just described.

## The pressure

*All sorts of hard things are pressing down on you and **you feel the weight**.*

The Greek is **lupethentes en poikilois peirasmois** — having been grieved by various trials.

**Poikilos** — many-coloured, varied. The same word he uses two chapters later of God's grace:
*the manifold grace of God*.

Varied trials, and varied grace. Peter uses one adjective for both.

## What is not said

That the grief is imaginary, or that it should not be felt.

**Lupethentes** — having been made sorrowful. He grants it.

## The gold

*Gold goes into the fire **to prove it is the real thing**.*

The Greek is **dia puros dokimazomenou** — tested through fire.

**Dokimazo** — to assay, to test metal for purity.

The refiner heats it until the impurities rise and can be lifted off, and what is left is what the
metal actually was.

## The concession about gold

*And **gold still wears out in the end**.*

The Greek is **tou apollumenou** — which perishes.

The most durable substance the ancient world knew, and Peter notes in passing that it does not
last.

## The comparison

*Your faith, tested, is **worth more than gold**.*

The Greek is **polutimoteron chrusiou** — more precious than gold.

## The conclusion

*So **it gets the fire too**.*

Which is the whole argument. The fire is not evidence of neglect. It is what is done to things
worth testing.

## What comes out

*And when Jesus finally **steps into plain sight**, what comes out the other side is **praise, and
honor, and glory**.*

The Greek is **en apokalupsei Iesou Christou** — at the revelation of Jesus Christ.

## Who receives the three

The Greek does not specify.

It can read as praise to God for the tested faith, or as praise given to the believer. Peter
leaves it open, and both readings sit oddly and deliberately.

## What Peter had been told

*"Simon, Simon! Indeed, **Satan has asked for you, that he may sift you as wheat**. But I have prayed
for you, that your faith should not fail."* See the note on Luke 22:31–34.

The man writing about tested faith had been told his own would be sifted, and it was.

## What follows

*Whom having not seen you love. Though now you do not see Him, yet believing, **you rejoice with joy
inexpressible and full of glory**.* See the note on 1 Peter 1:8–9.

## What James says with the same vocabulary

*My brethren, count it all joy when you fall into **various trials**, knowing that **the testing of
your faith produces patience**.* See the note on James 1:2–4.

The same two words — **poikilois peirasmois** and **dokimion**.

> **The argument.** Gold is put in the fire because it is worth assaying, and Peter adds that gold
> perishes anyway. The fire is not a sign of neglect.
