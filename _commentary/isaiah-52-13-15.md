The fourth Servant Song begins here, three verses before the chapter break, and
it opens with the ending.

## The order

*He will be raised up and lifted high and greatly exalted.*

Three verbs of exaltation, stacked, **before** a word about suffering. Isaiah
gives away the outcome first and then tells you what it cost.

The same three-fold lifting language is used elsewhere in Isaiah of God Himself.

## The disfigurement

*His appearance was disfigured beyond that of any man, his form beyond human
likeness.*

The Hebrew is stronger than most translations manage — a marring so severe that
what people were looking at no longer registered as a man.

Isaiah wrote this centuries before crucifixion existed as a Roman practice. What
he describes is a body damaged past recognition, in front of appalled onlookers.

## "So he will startle many nations"

The verb has been debated for a long time. It can mean to sprinkle — the word
used of a priest sprinkling blood — or to startle, to make them recoil.

The parallel in the sentence favours the second: *just as many were appalled at
him… so he will startle many nations.* One reaction answering another. But the
sprinkling sense sits underneath, and readers have heard both.

## Kings with nothing to say

*Kings will shut their mouths because of him. For what they were never told, they
will see, and what they never heard, they will understand.*

The silence is not respect. It is people with authority having no reply.

And the reason given is that something arrives outside the report — not passed
down, not heard about. Seen.

> **Where it goes next.** *Who has believed our message?* — the opening line of
> chapter 53. The song moves straight from kings shutting their mouths to the
> complaint that nobody believed it. Both are true of the same figure, and Paul
> quotes the second line in Romans 10.
