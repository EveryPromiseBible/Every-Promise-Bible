A short psalm about a household, and the images are all domestic.

## What it is

One of the Songs of Ascents — the fifteen psalms sung by pilgrims going up to Jerusalem for
the feasts.

They are short, portable, and mostly about ordinary life: sleep, family, going up steps.

## What comes before

*Blessed is every one who fears the LORD, who walks in His ways. **When you eat the labour of
your hands, you shall be happy, and it shall be well with you**.* See the note on
Psalms 128:1–2.

Eating what you worked for. In an agrarian economy under repeated invasion, that was not a
given — Deuteronomy lists the opposite as a curse: *you shall build a house and not dwell in
it; you shall plant a vineyard and not gather its grapes*.

## The wife

*Your wife shall be like **a fruitful vine in the very heart of your house**.*

The Hebrew is **gephen poriyah** — a fruitful vine. And **beyarketei beitekha**, in the inner
parts of your house.

A vine trained against the inside wall of a courtyard, which is where they were grown.

## The children

*Your children like **olive plants all around your table**.*

The Hebrew is **shetilei zeitim** — olive shoots, saplings.

Olives are the slowest of the crops. A newly planted tree takes many years before it produces
anything, and the family plants it anyway, expecting to be around.

And *all around your table* — the psalm's picture of a blessed man is a crowded meal.

## The summary

*Behold, thus shall the man be blessed who fears the LORD.*

## The blessing

*The LORD **bless you out of Zion**, and may you **see the good of Jerusalem all the days of
your life**.*

The blessing on the household is routed through the city. A pilgrim song, sung on the way up,
and the personal blessing comes from the place they are walking to.

## The grandchildren

*Yes, may you **see your children's children**.*

The Hebrew is **banim levaneikha** — sons to your sons.

The longest view in the psalm, and the least controllable thing in it. A man can work his
field. He cannot arrange to live long enough to meet his grandchildren.

## The last line

*Peace be upon Israel.*

The Hebrew is **shalom al-Yisrael**.

The psalm zooms out at the last word: one man, one household, one table, one city, and then
the nation.

## What is not in it

Nothing about wealth, position, victory, or land beyond what he works.

The picture of a blessed life is: bread you earned, a wife, children round the table,
grandchildren, and peace over the country.

## Where the same images go

*I am the vine, you are the branches.* See the note on John 15:5–8.

And the olive: Paul's argument in Romans 11 about branches grafted into a cultivated olive
tree.

And Israel as a vine, which usually goes badly: *I had planted you a noble vine… how then have
you turned before Me into the degenerate plant of an alien vine?*

## The psalm before it

*Unless the LORD builds the house, they labour in vain who build it… **it is vain for you to
rise up early, to sit up late, to eat the bread of sorrows; for so He gives His beloved
sleep**.*

And then children, in the same psalm, called a heritage and a reward.

Two consecutive psalms about a house, and both put the outcome outside the householder's
effort.

> **The slowest thing in the picture.** Olive saplings. A man plants them knowing he will wait
> years — and the psalm's last personal wish is to meet his grandchildren.
