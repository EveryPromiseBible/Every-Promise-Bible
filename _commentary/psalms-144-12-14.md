A list of what a settled country looks like, written by a man asking for one.

## What comes first

The psalm opens as a war song: *blessed be the LORD my Rock, who trains my hands for war, and my
fingers for battle*.

And the plea: *bow down Your heavens, O LORD, and come down; touch the mountains, and they shall
smoke. Flash forth lightning and scatter them.*

*Rescue me and deliver me… **from the hand of foreigners, whose mouth speaks lying words***.

Then, without transition, this.

## The sons

*Then our sons will be like **sturdy plants, grown tall in their youth**.*

The Hebrew is **kinti'im megudalim** — like plants grown large.

Full height, early. Nothing stunted.

## The daughters

*Our daughters like **carved corner pillars, shaped for a palace**.*

The Hebrew is **kezaviyyot mechuttavot** — like corner pillars, cut and fashioned.

A corner pillar carries two walls. It is the load-bearing member at the joint, and the one that
gets the carving.

The image is of strength and craftsmanship together, and it is the daughters who get it.

## The barns

*Our barns will be **full, overflowing with every kind of store**.*

The Hebrew is *furnishing all manner of store*.

## The flocks

*Our flocks will multiply **by thousands, by tens of thousands** in the fields.*

## The cattle

*Our cattle will be **heavy with young**.*

The Hebrew is difficult and is read either as *bearing well* or *laden*.

## The three negatives

*No **breach in the walls**, no **going into exile**, no **cry of grief in our streets**.*

The Hebrew is **perets** — a breach.

The same noun as Perez-uzzah, the breaking-out. See the note on 2 Samuel 6:6–8.

Three things absent: a broken wall, a deportation, and a wail in the street.

That is the whole of it. The list of blessings is children, food, livestock — and then three
disasters that have not happened.

## What the psalm knows

Everything in the list is ordinary.

Nothing in it is spectacular. Tall sons, capable daughters, full barns, pregnant cattle, and no
sound of grief outside.

It is a list written by somebody who knows what the alternative looks like — and the psalm's
first half is about being under attack.

## The last verse

*Happy are the people who are in such a state; **happy are the people whose God is the LORD**.*

The Hebrew is **ashrei** — the word Psalm 1 opens with.

Said twice, and the second says where the first came from.

Some read the two lines as a correction: happy is the nation that has all this — and happier
still the one that has Him.

## What the psalm quotes

*LORD, **what is man, that You take knowledge of him**? Or the son of man, that You are mindful
of him? Man is like a breath; his days are like a passing shadow.*

Psalm 8 asks it in wonder. This one asks it in the middle of a war.

## Where the same picture goes

*Everyone shall sit under his vine and under his fig tree, and **no one shall make them
afraid**.*

Micah's version, and it ends the same way — on the absence of fear rather than the presence of
anything.

## What is being asked for

Sons like grown plants, daughters like corner pillars in a palace, full barns, thousands of
sheep, strong oxen — *no breaking in or going out; no outcry in our streets*.

An entirely ordinary list. Nothing in it is religious.

*"Therefore do not worry, saying, 'What shall we eat?' or 'What shall we drink?'… For your
heavenly Father knows that you need all these things. But seek first the kingdom of God and
His righteousness, and **all these things shall be added to you**."*

**Jesus** does not dismiss the list. He reorders it.

## The one image not taken from a farm

*That our daughters may be **as pillars, sculptured in palace style**.*

The one image in the paragraph taken from architecture rather than farming — and it is the
corner pillar, the stone that carries the weight of two walls.

*In whom the whole building, being fitted together, grows into **a holy temple in the Lord**…
**Jesus Christ Himself being the chief cornerstone**.*

## The last verse of the psalm

*Happy are the people **whose God is the LORD**.*

The list ends and the psalm answers itself. Everything asked for in three verses is set beside
one clause, and the clause is not about any of it.

*I have learned, in whatever state I am, **to be content**… I can do all things through Christ
who strengthens me.*

> **What the list ends with.** Three things that have not happened. No breach, no exile, no
> crying in the streets — and a man under attack is the one writing it down.
