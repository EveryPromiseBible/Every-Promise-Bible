A queen says no, and a vacancy opens that will save a nation.

## The seventh day

*On the seventh day, when the king's heart was merry with wine.*

The writer tells us the state he was in before he tells us what he ordered. Six
days of drinking are behind the command.

## What he wanted

*To bring Queen Vashti before him wearing her royal crown. He wanted to **display
her beauty** to the people and the officials.*

The same verb as the six months of showing off the kingdom's wealth. She is the
last item in the display — the treasury, the army, the palace, and then his wife.

## Seven names

*Mehuman, Biztha, Harbona, Bigtha, Abagtha, Zethar, and Carkas.*

The writer lists all seven eunuchs, and no reader has ever needed to know their
names. He does the same with the seven princes a few verses later.

The book keeps its eye on detail like this, on court procedure and paperwork and
who was standing where. That is the level at which the whole rescue is going to
happen.

## "Would not come"

Two words in the Hebrew, and the empire tilts on them.

Nothing in the text explains her. No reason is given, no speech recorded. She
simply does not come.

## What the refusal produced

*The king was furious, and the anger smoldered in him.*

Out of that anger comes the decree, out of the decree comes the vacancy, and out
of the vacancy comes a Jewish orphan on the throne of Persia four years before
Haman writes his letters.

Nobody in chapter 1 is trying to save anybody. A queen refuses a drunk man's
summons and a place is made ready that will be needed later.

> **What the book is doing here.** God is not named and nothing miraculous
> happens. A woman's pride, a king's temper and a courtier's advice do all the
> work — and every one of them is moving a piece into position. He is ruling
> through it while none of them know His name.
