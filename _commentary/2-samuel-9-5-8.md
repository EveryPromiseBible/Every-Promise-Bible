A king sends for the last surviving heir of the family he replaced, and the man
arrives certain he is going to die.

## Who he was

Jonathan's son, Saul's grandson. Crippled in both feet since he was five, when
his nurse dropped him fleeing the news that his father and grandfather had been
killed.

By the political logic of that world he was a dead man — a rival claimant living
on borrowed time. That is why he had been living in **Lo Debar**, a name that
means roughly *no pasture*, or *nothing there*.

## What he says about himself

*Who is your servant, that you would even notice a dead dog like me?*

He is trembling, face to the ground. He does not ask for anything. He does not
argue that he is harmless or that he deserves better.

He calls himself a dead dog.

## What David gives him

Three things, and none of them are requested:

- **don't be afraid**
- **every piece of land that belonged to your grandfather Saul**
- **you yourself will always eat at my table**

The land is restored, and the seat is permanent. The chapter ends by saying he
ate at the king's table like one of the king's sons.

## Why it happened

*I am going to show you kindness **for the sake of your father Jonathan**.*

The reason has nothing to do with Mephibosheth. It is a covenant made years
earlier between David and Jonathan — see 1 Samuel 18:1–4 — and the kindness runs
to him on the strength of somebody else's name.

He is not being rewarded. He is being kept.

## The reason given

*"Is there still anyone left of the house of Saul, that I may show him kindness **for
Jonathan's sake**?"*

Not for his own sake. Not because of anything about him.

The Hebrew for *kindness* is **chesed** — covenant loyalty, the word used of God all through
Scripture.

*And **be kind to one another**, tenderhearted, **forgiving one another, even as God in Christ
forgave you**.*

**For Christ's sake** is the same construction, and it is the ground everybody who has ever
been shown kindness by God has stood on.

## How he describes himself

*"**What is your servant, that you should look upon such a dead dog as I?**"*

He does not think he has a claim, and he is right. He is the grandson of the king who hunted
David, from a house under sentence, hiding in a place called Lo Debar — *no pasture*.

## What he is given

*"**You shall eat bread at my table continually.**"*

Said four times in the chapter. Not a pension, not a pardon, not a cottage on the estate — a
permanent seat at the king's own table.

And: *all the land of Saul your grandfather*.

## What he is called at the end

*So Mephibosheth ate at David's table **like one of the king's sons**.*

*Behold what manner of love the Father has bestowed on us, **that we should be called children
of God**!*

*Therefore you are no longer a slave but a son, and if a son, then an heir of God through
Christ.*

## The last verse

*And **he was lame in both feet**.*

The chapter ends on it. Nothing about the seat at the table fixed the feet, and nothing about
the feet affected the seat.

Under the table, nobody could see them.

> **The last verse of the chapter.** *And he was lame in both feet.* Second Samuel
> mentions it three times, and the final time is the very last line — after the
> land, the table, and the sons. The condition never changed. His place at the
> table was not conditional on it.
