Three verses of instruction between the darkness and the last plague, and the
instruction is about asking the neighbours for silver.

## The word that is usually mistranslated

Older versions have Israel *borrow* from the Egyptians, which made generations of
readers uneasy — it sounds like taking things under false pretences.

The Hebrew verb is simply *ask*. There is no promise of return in it. What
follows is a request made openly, and granted.

## Why it is not theft

Four hundred years of unpaid labour stand behind it. The law God gives later
requires that a freed slave not be sent away empty-handed but supplied
generously from the flock and the threshing floor — and that is exactly the shape
of what happens here.

Israel leaves as freed labourers being paid off, not as looters. Psalm 105 says
it plainly: *He brought His people out with silver and gold.*

## "The LORD had already softened the Egyptians"

The favour is not attributed to Israel's charm or to Egyptian generosity. The
text says God had gone ahead of the request and changed how these people saw
their slaves.

And then a line about Moses himself: *a great man in that land, respected by
Pharaoh's officials and by the people alike*. The man who had fled Egypt as a
wanted murderer is, by the end, the most respected person in it.

> **What the silver was for.** Some of it ends up in the tabernacle a few chapters
> later — gold, silver, bronze, blue and purple yarn, brought as freewill
> offerings. And some of it ends up in the golden calf. The same wealth, out of
> the same pockets, going two directions.
