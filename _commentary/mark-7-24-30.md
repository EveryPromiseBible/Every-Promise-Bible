Mark's version of the Syrophoenician woman, and he keeps details Matthew leaves
out.

## Why He was there

*He went into a house and **did not want anyone to know He was there, but there was
no hiding Him**.*

Only Mark records this. He had crossed the border into Gentile territory to get away
from the crowds, and it did not work.

## Who she was

*A **Greek, born in Syrian Phoenicia**.*

Mark identifies her by culture and birthplace. Matthew calls her a Canaanite — the
old covenant word, the nation Israel was told to drive out.

Between them: a pagan by religion, a Greek by culture, a Canaanite by ancestry. Every
category that excludes her.

## What she does

*She **came and fell at His feet**… and **pleaded** with Him.*

The Greek for *pleaded* is imperfect — she kept on asking.

## The saying

*"Let the children at the table be **fed first**. It is not right to take **the
children's bread** and throw it to the little dogs."*

Mark alone keeps **first**. It is a word about order, not exclusion — and she hears it.

**Little dogs** — the Greek **kunarion** is a diminutive: the household pets, not the
scavengers in the street. It is a word from inside a house.

## Her answer

*"**Yes, Lord** — and even **the little dogs under the table** eat the scraps the
children drop."*

She concedes everything. She does not argue with the order, the priority, or the
term.

And she picks up the household picture and finishes it: *under the table.* If the
dogs are indoors, then she is talking about a place at the family's feet, and food
that is already falling.

## What He says

*"**For an answer like that**, you may go home."*

The Greek is *because of this word* — because of what you said.

Mark's version credits the reply. Matthew's credits the faith: *O woman, great is your
faith.*

## The healing

*"**The spirit has left your daughter.**" And she went back and found her child lying
on the bed, and the spirit was gone.*

Past tense before she moves. It was done while she was still standing there, and the
child was miles away.

## What comes before this in Mark

The whole first half of chapter 7 is the argument about clean and unclean — washing
hands, traditions, and Jesus' verdict: *there is nothing that enters a man from
outside which can defile him.*

Mark adds his own note: *thus He declared all foods clean.*

And then, in the very next paragraph, He crosses into unclean territory and heals an
unclean woman's daughter of an unclean spirit.

Mark puts the sermon and the demonstration side by side.

> **What He does next.** Goes further into Gentile country, to the Decapolis, and
> heals a deaf man with an impediment — *Ephphatha, be opened.* Then feeds four
> thousand people there. The bread the children could not finish is being handed out
> across the border within two chapters.
