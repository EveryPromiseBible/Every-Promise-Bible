John 3:16 almost never gets quoted with the sentence before it, and that sentence
is the reason it says what it says.

## The comparison Jesus chose

*Just as Moses lifted up the snake in the wilderness, so the Son of Man must be
lifted up.*

The story is in Numbers 21. The people are dying of snakebite, and God's remedy
is not to remove the snakes. It is a bronze snake on a pole — an image of the
very thing killing them, raised up where everyone can see it. Anyone who looked
at it lived.

They did not have to understand it, deserve it, or do anything but look.

That is the picture Jesus reaches for to describe His own death, and the verse
everybody knows follows immediately from it.

## "Lifted up"

The Greek **hypsoo** means to raise up, and also to exalt. John uses it three
times in this Gospel of the crucifixion, and each time both senses are live at
once: lifted onto a cross, and lifted to glory. The word will not let you keep
the two apart.

---

## What the famous verse actually claims

*For God loved the world so much that He gave His only Son.*

The love comes first and the giving follows from it. That order matters, because
the sentence is often heard the other way round — as though the cross persuaded a
reluctant God to love. John says the love was the reason for the gift.

And the scope is *the world* — the same word John uses elsewhere for the world
that does not know Him. Not the deserving part of it.

## The verses nobody quotes

*God did not send His Son into the world to condemn the world, but so that the
world might be rescued through Him.*

That is verse 17, and it exists because verse 16 raises the question. Jesus
states the purpose negatively and positively in the same breath: not to condemn;
to rescue.

Then verse 18 draws the line: whoever believes is not condemned; whoever does not
*stands condemned already*. Not *will be* — already. The condemnation is the
situation being rescued from, not a punishment for refusing the rescue.

> **On the snake, once more.** Israel kept that bronze snake for centuries and
> eventually started burning incense to it, until Hezekiah broke it up. Even the
> right symbol becomes a problem when people look at the object instead of what
> it points to.
