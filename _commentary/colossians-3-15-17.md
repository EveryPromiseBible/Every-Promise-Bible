*Let the peace of God have the deciding voice in your hearts.* The English has
to spread out to say what the Greek says in one word, and that word is where
this verse gets interesting.

## The word is from the stadium

**Brabeuo.** A *brabeus* was the judge at the games — the official who watched
the race and said who had won, whose call settled the matter. The verb means to
act as that official: to arbitrate, to decide, to rule on.

So Paul is not saying *feel peaceful*. He is saying: give peace the umpire's
whistle. Let it be the thing that makes the call inside you when two courses of
action are competing.

That is a far more practical instruction than it usually gets treated as. An
umpire is not an atmosphere. An umpire is a decision-maker.

## What is being umpired

The context is a chapter about how believers live together — putting off anger
and malice, putting on compassion, kindness, humility, patience, bearing with
one another and forgiving. Then this.

*For it was into that peace you were called, in one body.* The peace is not
first a private calm; it is the peace they were called into *together*. So a
good deal of what this umpire is ruling on is how a person deals with other
people, and the verse right after it is about the word of Christ living in them
richly enough to teach and correct one another.

---

## Reading it for guidance

This verse is used constantly as a way of finding God's will, and that use is
not foreign to it. Paul does put peace in the position of the one who decides.

Two things keep it honest. First, it says *the peace of God*, not the absence of
nerves — the same peace Jesus described as not the kind the world hands out.
Someone can feel nervous and still be at peace, and can feel enthusiastic and
have no peace at all. Second, it sits in a sentence about the body, and Paul
immediately surrounds it with the word of Christ dwelling richly and with people
teaching one another. Peace umpires; it does not umpire alone.

## And be thankful people

Paul attaches this to the end of the sentence, and it is easy to read as a
pleasantry. In the paragraph it is structural: he says it here, again two lines
later with the singing, and a third time at the close — *giving thanks through
Him to God the Father*.

Three times in three verses. Whatever else the umpired life looks like, Paul
seems to think it is audibly grateful.

> **On "whatever you do, in word or in work."** The paragraph ends by collapsing
> the distinction between religious activity and ordinary work — everything, in
> the name of the Lord Jesus. That is where a chapter about how to live together
> ends up: not with a special category of holy tasks, but with the whole of a
> life brought under one name.
