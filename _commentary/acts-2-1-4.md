Three images in four verses, and every one of them had been used before.

## The wind

*A sound from heaven like a great rushing wind, and it filled the whole house.*

Luke is careful: a **sound like** wind. Nothing is described as blowing.

In both Hebrew and Greek the word for spirit is also the word for wind and
breath. Ezekiel's valley of dry bones is raised when the breath comes; Jesus
told Nicodemus the wind blows where it wishes.

## The fire

*What looked like tongues of fire, separating and coming to rest on each one of
them.*

Fire had marked God's presence before — the bush, the pillar, the mountain, the
altar on Carmel. Always one fire, in one place, that the people stood back from.

Here it divides, and a piece of it settles on every individual in the room.
Nobody is standing at a distance.

## The languages

*Began to speak in other languages, as the Spirit gave them the words.*

The crowd outside is the point: fifteen nations are listed in the next paragraph,
and each hears its own tongue.

Babel scattered people by confusing their language. This gathers people by
overcoming it. Luke does not draw the comparison; he just lists the nations.

## Where the sermon goes

Peter stands up and explains it from Joel — *in the last days I will pour out my
Spirit on all people* — and adds the detail from Psalm 16 about the Holy One not
seeing decay.

*This Jesus God has raised up, and we are all witnesses of it… He has poured out
what you are seeing and hearing.* The wind and the fire are presented as evidence
of an ascension. See the note on Acts 2:32–33.

> **The day it happened.** Pentecost was the feast of firstfruits of the wheat
> harvest, fifty days after Passover — and by tradition, the day the law was
> given at Sinai. Three thousand died the day the law came down at Sinai. Three
> thousand were added the day the Spirit came down here.
