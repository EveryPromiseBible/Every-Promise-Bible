The third of three parables told to answer one complaint.

## Why it is being told

*Then all the tax collectors and the sinners drew near to Him to hear Him. And the
Pharisees and scribes complained, saying, "**This Man receives sinners and eats with
them**."*

Luke 15 is one answer in three parts: a lost sheep, a lost coin, and this.

The first two end with the finder rejoicing. The third is longer and has a second half
about somebody who does not.

## The request

*"Father, **give me my share of the property now**."*

In that culture the estate passed at death. Asking for it early was as good as saying the
father was dead to him.

There is no record in the parable of the father arguing.

## What he did

*So the father **divided what he had between them**.*

The Greek is **bion** — his living, his means of life. The father divides his own
livelihood.

The elder brother's share goes to him at the same moment, which matters for the second
half of the story.

## The distance

*Not many days later the younger son **gathered up everything that was his, left home**,
and traveled off **to a distant country**.*

The Greek for *gathered up* is a word for converting property into cash. He liquidated.

## The spending

*And there he **threw the whole of it away on wild and reckless living**.*

The Greek is **asotos** — from **a-sozo**, unsaved, without deliverance. It carries the
sense of a life with no way of being kept.

Luke does not itemise. The elder brother does that later, and Luke is careful to attribute
the details to him rather than to the narrator.

## The timing

*When it was all gone, **a hard famine settled over that country**.*

The money ran out first and then the famine came. Either alone he might have survived.

## The job

*So he **hired himself out** to one of the local men, who sent him **into the fields to
feed pigs**.*

The Greek is **ekollethe** — he glued himself to, attached himself. It is the language of
a man begging for work.

A Jew feeding pigs is at the bottom of every category at once — foreign country, unclean
animals, hired rather than owned, and hungry.

## The hunger

*He got so hungry that he **would gladly have eaten the pods the pigs were eating**.*

The pods are carob — animal fodder.

## The last clause

*And **nobody gave him anything at all**.*

That sentence ends the section, and it is the bottom of the story.

## What happens next

*But when he came to himself* — and the speech he rehearses, and the father who sees him
*while he was still a great way off* and runs. See the note on Luke 15:17–20.

The Greek for the father's reaction is **esplanchnisthe** — the gut-word, the same one
used of Jesus looking at the crowds. See the note on Matthew 9:35–38.

## What he rehearses and what he gets to say

He plans: *make me like one of your hired servants.*

He gets as far as *I am no longer worthy to be called your son* and the father cuts him
off with instructions to the servants.

The line about hired servants is never delivered.

> **Where the story starts.** With a father who divides his living because he was asked
> to, and lets a son walk out with it. Nothing in the first half is done to stop him.
