The writer explains the difference between the two diets, and the test he gives is not
about knowledge.

## The condition

*Anyone who is **still living on milk cannot handle what is said about righteousness**.*

The Greek is **apeiros logou dikaiosunes** — inexperienced in the word of righteousness.

**Apeiros** means untried, unskilled — the opposite of practised.

Not that they have not been told. That they have not handled it.

## The name

*He is **a baby**.*

The Greek is **nepios** — infant, literally *non-speaking*.

The same word Paul uses of the heir who is no different from a slave. See the note on
Galatians 4:1–3.

## The other diet

*Food you have to chew belongs to **grown men and women**.*

The Greek is **teleion** — the mature, the complete. From **telos**, the end a thing is
aiming at.

## The definition

*People who have **worked their senses hard enough, and long enough**, to **tell what is
good from what is evil**.*

The Greek is **gegumnasmena** — trained, exercised. From the root of *gymnasium*.

And **hexin** — a settled habit, a state produced by repetition.

## What maturity is not, in this sentence

Information. The writer has just said he has *a great deal to say* and cannot say it. See
the note on Hebrews 5:11–12.

The gap is not on the input side.

## What it is

Discernment produced by use. The Greek is literally *having the faculties trained by
practice for the distinguishing of good and evil*.

A trained sense is not a fact learned. It is what a person develops by handling something
repeatedly until they can tell the difference without stopping to think.

## The reproach

*By this time **you ought to be the ones teaching**.*

Verse 12, and the diagnosis is that they had gone backwards: *you have come to have need
of milk*.

## What the writer does about it

*Therefore, **leaving the discussion of the elementary principles of Christ, let us go on to
perfection**.*

The Greek for *perfection* is **teleioteta** — the noun of the word he has just used for
the grown.

He refuses to go back over the foundations, lists six of them so they know what he is
leaving, and moves on.

## What the solid food turns out to be

Melchizedek. He had named it four times and broken off each time.

He picks it up in chapter 7 and spends a whole chapter there: the priest with no
genealogy, the oath in Psalm 110, and the change of law that follows a change of
priesthood. See the notes on Psalms 110:4 and Hebrews 7:20–22.

## The order

Milk, solid food, and then two chapters of the hardest argument in the letter.

The writer does not simplify. He tells them what they are missing and then delivers it
anyway.

> **The test he gives.** Not what a person knows. Whether their senses have been worked
> hard enough and long enough to tell one thing from another without being told.
