The hardest paragraph in the letter, and the first thing to see is who it is written to
and what they were about to do.

## Who the letter is for

Hebrew believers under pressure to go back to the temple. The whole letter is one long
argument that there is nothing to go back to — a better priest, a better covenant, a
better sacrifice, and all of it *once for all*.

The temple was still standing. The offerings were still being made daily.

## The word

*It is **impossible**.*

The Greek is **adunaton** — without power, incapable. The writer uses it four times in
this letter:

- *It is **impossible** for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins.*
- *Without faith it is **impossible** to please Him.*
- *It is **impossible** for God to lie.* See the note on Hebrews 6:18.
- And this one.

## The five descriptions

*Have once been **given light**, have **tasted the gift** that came down from heaven, have
been **given a share in the Holy Spirit**, have **tasted how good the word of God is** and
**the powers of the age that is coming**.*

Five, and two of them are the verb **tasted** — **geuomai**.

## What is being described

Exposure to the whole thing. Light, the heavenly gift, the Spirit, the word, and the
powers of the coming age — these people had seen all of it at close range.

## What they then do

*And then **turn their backs on it all**.*

The Greek is **parapesontas** — to fall away alongside, to defect.

The specific defection in view is the one the whole letter is written against: going back
to the sacrifices.

## What is impossible

*It is **impossible to make them new again so that they change their minds**.*

The Greek is *to renew them again to repentance* — **metanoia**, a change of mind.

## The reason given

*They are **crucifying the Son of God over again, on their own account**, and **putting Him
on show for people to jeer at**.*

The Greek is **anastauroo** (crucify again) and **paradeigmatizo** (expose to public
shame).

That is what returning to the altar amounted to in the writer's argument. If the
sacrifice was not enough, then the cross has to be repeated — and the letter has just
spent five chapters saying it cannot be and does not need to be.

There is no second cross to go to. That is the impossibility.

## What the writer says next

He does not leave it there. Three verses on:

*But dear friends, even when we talk like this, **we are convinced of better things where
you are concerned** — the things that come with being saved.* See the note on
Hebrews 6:9–10.

He states the warning and then immediately says he does not think it applies to them.

## Where the chapter lands

On an oath sworn to Abraham, and two unchangeable things, and *a hope both sure and
steadfast, which enters the Presence behind the veil.* See the notes on
Hebrews 6:13–14, Hebrews 6:16–17 and Hebrews 6:18.

The chapter that contains the sharpest warning ends on the strongest guarantee in the
letter.

> **What the passage is arguing.** Not that a person can be lost and re-lost, but that
> there is only one sacrifice and it has already happened. A man walking back to an
> altar is walking toward something the letter says was finished.
