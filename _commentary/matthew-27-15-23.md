A governor tries to release a prisoner and asks the wrong crowd.

## The custom

*Now it was the governor's custom at the festival to **release one prisoner, whichever one the crowd
asked for**.*

The Greek is **eiothei apoluein**.

Attested nowhere outside the Gospels, but a plausible gesture for a Roman prefect at Passover — the
feast that commemorated a liberation.

## The other prisoner

*At that time there was a **well-known prisoner** in custody named **Barabbas**.*

The Greek is **desmion episemon** — a notorious prisoner.

Mark says he was *chained with his fellow rebels, who had committed murder in the rebellion*. John
calls him **lestes** — a brigand.

## The name

**Bar-abba** — son of the father.

Some early manuscripts of Matthew give his full name as **Jesus Barabbas**, which would make the
question *Jesus Barabbas, or Jesus called Christ?*

Origen knew the reading and was uncomfortable with it.

## The question

*"**Which one do you want me to release to you — Barabbas, or Jesus who is called the Messiah?**"*

Pilate frames it as a choice between an insurrectionist and a man he considers harmless.

## What he knew

*He knew perfectly well that **they had handed Jesus over out of envy**.*

The Greek is **dia phthonon** — because of envy.

The governor has correctly diagnosed the case and proceeds anyway.

## The interruption

*And while he was sitting there **on the judgment seat**, his wife sent a message.*

The Greek is **epi tou bematos** — on the tribunal.

He is mid-trial, in the chair, and a note arrives.

## The message

*"**Have nothing to do with that innocent man. I have been troubled all night by a dream about
Him**."*

The Greek is **to dikaio ekeino** — that righteous man.

Matthew alone records it. The only person in the trial who says Jesus is innocent without
qualification is a Roman woman who never appears again.

## The lobbying

*But **the chief priests and the elders worked through the crowd**, and talked them into asking for
Barabbas.*

The Greek is **epeisan tous ochlous** — persuaded the crowds.

The crowd is worked. Matthew does not present the verdict as spontaneous.

## The vote

*"Barabbas."*

## The second question

*"Then **what am I to do with Jesus who is called the Messiah?**"*

The Greek is **ti oun poieso Iesoun** — what then shall I do with Jesus?

He asks a mob to make a judicial decision he has already reached himself.

## The answer

*They all said, "**Crucify Him!**"*

## The third question

*"**Why?**" he said. "**What wrong has He done?**"*

The Greek is **ti gar kakon epoiesen**.

A Roman judge asking a crowd to name the charge, and getting no answer.

## The volume

*But they only **shouted louder**: "Crucify Him!"*

The Greek is **perissos ekrazon** — they cried out the more.

The escalation is in volume, not argument.

## What Pilate does

*When Pilate saw that he could not prevail at all… **he took water and washed his hands** before the
multitude.*

*"I am innocent of the blood of this just Man."*

The third person in the passage to call Him innocent, after his wife and his own reasoning.

## What actually happened

A guilty man walked out, and an innocent one took the cross that had been prepared.

The narrative does not comment. Matthew records the exchange and moves on.

> **What Pilate knew.** *He knew perfectly well that they had handed Jesus over out of envy.* He
> diagnosed the case correctly, asked the crowd to name a charge, got none, and proceeded.
