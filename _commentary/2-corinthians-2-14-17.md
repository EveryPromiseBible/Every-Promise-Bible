Paul reaches for a Roman street scene his readers had all watched.

## The image

*Who **always leads us along in His victory parade** in Christ.*

The Greek is **thriambeuo** — to lead in a triumph. The Roman triumph was a specific
event: a victorious general processing through the city, with his army behind him and
the captives walking in the column.

Priests swung censers the whole way, so the route was full of smoke and scent.

## Where Paul puts himself

In the procession, behind the general.

He calls himself **doulos** — a slave of Christ — at the top of most of his letters, and
the picture here is consistent with it.

## "Always"

The Greek is *in every place*, *at all times*.

He writes it three verses after saying he had no rest in his spirit because he could not
find Titus, and one chapter before describing the trouble in Asia where he despaired of
life. See the note on 2 Corinthians 1:8–11.

## The fragrance

*And through us **spreads the fragrance of knowing Him everywhere we go**.*

The Greek is **osme** — the smell itself, the thing carried on the air.

You do not see a triumph before you smell it.

## What we are

*For **to God we are the fragrance of Christ**.*

The audience named is God. The scent goes up to Him first, before it does anything to
anybody in the street.

The Greek here changes to **euodia** — the same word the Greek Old Testament uses for
the *sweet aroma* of an accepted sacrifice, over Noah's altar and throughout Leviticus.
See the note on Genesis 8:20–22.

## Both crowds

*Both among **those who are being saved** and among **those who are perishing**.*

The same smell reaches both. Paul does not describe two different fragrances.

## The two effects

*To the one **a smell of death that ends in death**, to the other **a breath of life that
opens into life**.*

The Greek is *death unto death* and *life unto life*.

In a triumph the same incense meant two different things depending on which part of the
column you were in — the returning soldiers, or the prisoners walking to execution.

## The question

*And **who is equal to any of this?**"*

Paul stops mid-argument and asks it. He answers it two chapters later: *our sufficiency
is from God.*

## What he is not

*We are **not like so many, hawking the word of God for profit**.*

The Greek is **kapeleuo** — to be a retailer, a huckster; it carried the sense of a
trader who waters the wine.

**So many** — Paul says there were plenty of them.

## What he is

*We speak **in Christ, with sincerity**, as men **sent from God** and **standing in the
sight of God**.*

Four descriptions, and two of them are about who is watching.

> **Where the scent comes from.** Not from the man carrying it. In a triumph the smoke
> belongs to the procession, and the people lining the street smell the victory of
> somebody who was not them.
