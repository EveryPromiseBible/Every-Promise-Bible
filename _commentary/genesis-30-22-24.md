Three verses at the end of a long domestic war, and they are the turning point of Genesis.

## What has been happening

Two sisters married to the same man, and a competition running for chapters.

Leah unloved and fertile; Rachel loved and barren. Two maids brought in as proxies. Eleven
sons and a daughter, named as moves in an argument — *the LORD has looked on my affliction*,
*now this time my husband will become attached to me*, *with great wrestlings I have wrestled
with my sister*.

Rachel had said to Jacob: *give me children, or else I die*. And he had answered angrily:
*am I in the place of God, who has withheld from you the fruit of the womb?*

## The verb

*Then **God remembered Rachel**.*

The Hebrew is **zakar**.

It never means God had forgotten. It is the word for God acting on a commitment — *God
remembered Noah* while the water was still up, *God remembered His covenant with Abraham* in
Egypt, *God remembered Hannah*.

Every time it appears, something changes immediately afterwards.

## The two clauses

*He **listened to her** and **opened her womb**.*

The Hebrew is *God listened to her*, which means she had been asking. None of the praying is
recorded.

And *opened her womb* — the same phrase used of Leah at the start of the rivalry: *when the
LORD saw that Leah was unloved, He opened her womb*.

He opened both, at different times, for different reasons.

## The first name

*She said, "**God has taken away my disgrace**."*

The Hebrew is **asaph** — gathered up, removed. And **cherpah**, reproach.

Barrenness was a public shame, and she names the removal of it before she names the child.

## The second name

*She named him **Joseph**, saying, "**May the LORD add to me another son**."*

The Hebrew is **yoseph** — may He add. From **yasaph**.

Two verbs are audible in the name: **asaph**, taken away, and **yasaph**, may He add.

She names her son for both — what has been removed and what she is still asking for.

## The request

The first thing she does with a son is ask for another.

## What she got

Benjamin, on the road to Bethlehem, and she died having him. She named him **Ben-Oni**, son
of my sorrow, and Jacob renamed him **Benjamin**, son of my right hand. See the note on
Genesis 35:16–20.

The second son was granted and it cost her life.

## What happens next in the chapter

Jacob immediately tells Laban he wants to go home: *send me away, that I may go to my own
place and to my country*.

Joseph's birth is what starts the return to Canaan. The whole speckled-and-spotted business
with the flocks follows in the same breath.

## What the boy becomes

The coat, the dreams, the pit, Potiphar's house, the prison, the two dreams in Pharaoh's
court, and a granary that keeps the family alive. See the note on Genesis 42:6–17.

Everything from Genesis 37 to the end of the book comes out of these three verses.

## The line that runs through

*But God was with him.* Genesis says it in the prison and in Potiphar's house.

And at the end: *you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good*. See the note on
Genesis 50:15–21.

## The other barren women

Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, Hannah, and then an old priest's wife in the hill country of Judea.

*Blessed be the Lord God of Israel, for **He has visited and redeemed His people***. See the
note on Luke 1:68–79.

Zacharias says it over a son given to a woman past the age, in the same chapter as the
announcement to a girl in Nazareth. The line of the promise keeps running through wombs that
had been closed, so that nobody could mistake the child for an achievement.

## What she names him

**Yoseph** — *may He add*.

She has just been given one son and the name is a request for another. The next one costs her
her life on the road to Bethlehem, and she names him **Ben-oni**, son of my sorrow — and his
father renames him **Benjamin**, son of the right hand. See the note on Genesis 35:16–20.

> **What she named him.** Not for the child she had. *May He add* — the first thing she does
> after years of waiting is ask for the next one.
