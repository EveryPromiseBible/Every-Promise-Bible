Luke was a physician, and his account of Gethsemane includes a detail no other
Gospel records.

## "As He always did"

*Then He went out, **as He always did**, to the Mount of Olives.*

Luke notes the habit. It is also how Judas knew where to find Him — John says *Judas
also knew the place, for Jesus often met there with His disciples.*

He goes to the place His betrayer can find Him.

## The instruction, twice

*"**Pray that you do not walk into the testing.**"*

Said at the start and again at the end of the passage, bracketing the whole scene.

The Greek is **peirasmos** — testing, trial, temptation. It is the same word in the
Lord's Prayer: *do not lead us into temptation.*

## A stone's throw

*He **drew away from them, about as far as a stone could be thrown**.*

Luke measures it. Close enough to be seen; far enough to be alone.

## The prayer

*"Father, **if You are willing, take this cup away from Me. And yet — not what I
want. What You want.**"*

Both halves are real. He asks for it to be removed, and He submits.

The request is not withdrawn — He asks, and then places the asking under something
else.

## The cup

The image runs through the prophets — the cup of God's wrath, passed round and drunk
to the dregs. Psalm 75: *there is a cup in the hand of the LORD… He pours it out, and
the wicked of the earth drink it dry.* See the note on Psalms 75:6–8.

He is looking at that.

## The angel

*Then **an angel from heaven appeared to Him, strengthening Him**.*

Only Luke records this. The cup is not taken away and help is sent.

The Greek is to strengthen, to put strength into.

## What Luke records next

*And **in His anguish He prayed more earnestly still**, and **His sweat became like
great drops of blood** falling to the ground.*

The Greek for *anguish* is **agonia** — the origin of the English word, and it
carried the sense of a contest, a struggle.

And the sweat. The condition is called **hematidrosis** — under extreme stress the
small vessels around the sweat glands can rupture, and blood mixes with sweat. It is
rare, documented, and associated with acute terror or grief.

Luke, of the four writers, is the one who mentions it.

Note also what the strengthening produced: *he prayed **more earnestly**.* The angel
did not settle Him into calm. He went back in harder.

## Where the drops fall

*Falling **to the ground**.*

Genesis 3: *cursed is the ground because of you… in toil you will eat of it all the
days of your life. It will bring you thorns and thistles. By the sweat of your brow
you will eat your bread.*

Sweat and cursed ground, and a man in a garden. See the note on Genesis 3:17–19.

## The disciples

*Found them **asleep, worn out with grief**.*

Luke alone supplies the reason. Matthew and Mark just say they were sleeping; Luke
says why, and it is not indifference.

> **What was in a garden the first time.** A man, a tree, and a choice, and the
> sentence *not what You want, but what I want.* This one is a garden, a man on his
> knees, and the sentence reversed.
