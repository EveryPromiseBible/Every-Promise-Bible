A king finishes a public prayer with an admission.

## The situation

Three nations are marching on Judah. Jehoshaphat *set himself to seek the LORD, and
proclaimed a fast throughout all Judah*, and the whole country came to Jerusalem.

He prays standing in the temple court in front of them.

## The history he recites

*"Here are the men of **Ammon and Moab and Mount Seir**. **You would not let Israel invade
them** when they came out of Egypt, so **Israel turned away from them and did not destroy
them**."*

Ammon and Moab are Lot's descendants; Mount Seir is Esau's country.

Deuteronomy 2 records the instruction: *do not provoke them… I have given Mount Seir to
Esau as his possession.* See the note on Deuteronomy 2:1–7.

Israel had gone round them because they were told to.

## The complaint

*"**Look how they repay us** — coming to drive us out of the land You gave us to
inherit."*

He is not arguing that Judah deserves rescue. He is pointing out that the nations they
spared are the ones invading.

## The question

*"O our God, **will You not judge them?**"*

## The three admissions

*"**We have no power** against this great multitude that is coming against us."*

*"**We do not know what to do**."*

*"**But our eyes are upon You**."*

The Hebrew for *no power* is *no strength*.

Three clauses, and the first two are concessions. He does not claim capability and does
not claim a plan.

## What a king is not supposed to say

Jehoshaphat is standing in front of the army, the elders, the women and the children,
saying he does not know what to do.

The next verse notes who was in the room: *all Judah, **with their little ones, their wives,
and their children**, stood before the LORD.*

## The turn

The whole prayer pivots on the last clause. The Hebrew is *upon You are our eyes*.

Nothing is offered. The prayer ends on a direction of gaze.

## The answer

*Then, in the middle of the assembly, **the Spirit of the LORD came on Jahaziel**.*

A Levite of the sons of Asaph — a musician's family — speaks. See the note on
2 Chronicles 20:13–15.

*"**Do not be afraid or dismayed** because of this great multitude, for **the battle is not
yours, but God's**."*

## The instruction

*"**You will not need to fight in this battle. Position yourselves, stand still and see the
salvation of the LORD** with you."*

The same sentence Moses gave at the sea: *stand still, and see the salvation of the LORD.*
See the note on Exodus 14:10–14.

## What they did

Appointed singers to go out in front of the army, praising *the beauty of holiness* and
singing *praise the LORD, for His mercy endures forever* — the refrain of Psalm 136. See the
note on Psalms 136:1–3.

*And when they began to sing and to praise, **the LORD set ambushes** against the people…
and they were defeated.*

Judah arrives to find the field already settled, and spends three days collecting the
spoil.

## What he admits out loud

*"**We have no power** against this great multitude that is coming against us; **nor do we
know what to do**; **but our eyes are upon You**."*

No strength, no plan, and no idea. A head of state says all three out loud, in public, in
front of the whole nation including the children.

## What he does not do

Offer anything. There is no vow in the prayer, no promise of reform, no bargain, and no appeal
to Judah's record.

He recites what God did, states the problem, and stops.

*Let us therefore **come boldly to the throne of grace**, that we may obtain mercy and find
grace to help in time of need.*

The Greek is **parrhesia** — the right to speak plainly, which is what a citizen has in an
assembly and a beggar does not.

## Where the eyes go

*"**But our eyes are upon You.**"*

*Let us run with endurance the race that is set before us, **looking unto Jesus, the author
and finisher of our faith**.*

The Greek is **aphorontes** — looking away from everything else to one place. It is the same
motion Jehoshaphat describes, and the writer of **Hebrews** gives it an object.

## Who is standing there

*Now all Judah, with their **little ones, their wives, and their children**, stood before the
LORD.*

The Chronicler names the children twice. The prayer that admits total helplessness is prayed
in front of them on purpose.

> **How the prayer ends.** Not with a strategy or a claim. Two admissions — no power, and
> no idea — and then a sentence about where they are looking.
