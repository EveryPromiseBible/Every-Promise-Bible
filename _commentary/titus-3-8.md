title: "A saying you can lean your whole weight on"

One verse that does two jobs: it certifies what has just been said, and it says what follows from
it.

## The formula

*"**That is a saying you can lean your whole weight on.**"*

The Greek is **pistos ho logos** — faithful is the word.

**Paul** uses the phrase five times, all in the letters to **Timothy** and **Titus**, and every time
it marks a sentence he expects to be quoted:

*This is a faithful saying and worthy of all acceptance, that **Christ Jesus came into the world to
save sinners, of whom I am chief**.*

*For if we died with Him, we shall also live with Him… **if we are faithless, He remains faithful;
He cannot deny Himself**.*

Here it certifies the four verses before it: *not on the strength of anything we did… justified by
His grace.* See the note on Titus 3:4–7.

## What it is applied to

Grace, not conduct. The **faithful saying** in this chapter is the sentence with no imperative in
it.

## The instruction that follows

*"**So that the people who have put their trust in God will give their minds to leading the way in
good work.**"*

**Hina phrontizosin kalon ergon proistasthai hoi pepisteukotes Theo.**

**Proistasthai** — to stand before, to take the lead in, to be at the front of.

## The order

The good works come after the justification and are described as its purpose, not its price.

*For by grace you have been saved through faith… not of works, lest anyone should boast. **For we are
His workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works**, which God prepared beforehand that we
should walk in them.*

**Ephesians** 2 has the same three moves in the same order: not of works, then *for good works*, and
then the works themselves prepared in advance.

**Paul** never separates the two and never reverses them. Three verses earlier he has excluded
*works done in righteousness*; here he tells people to lead in them.

## Who has put their trust

*"**The people who have put their trust in God.**"*

**Hoi pepisteukotes Theo** — a perfect participle. Those who have believed, and still do.

The good works are asked of a group already defined by something else.

## Excellent and profitable

*"**These things are good and useful for people.**"*

**Kala kai ophelima tois anthropois** — good and beneficial to men.

**Paul**'s reason for good works in this letter is horizontal: they help people. He does not offer
them as a way of securing anything, and the very next verse tells Titus to avoid *silly arguments,
and family trees, and squabbles and fights about the law* because they are **anopheleis** — the same
word, negated. Useless.

> **What gets the seal.** *Faithful is the word* — and the thing Paul attaches it to is the sentence
> with no command in it. The good works arrive in the following clause, as the purpose of what was
> done, and never as its price.
