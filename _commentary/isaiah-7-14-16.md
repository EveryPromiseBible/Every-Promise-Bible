A sign given to a frightened king, and a name that carries more than the moment
it was spoken into.

## The situation

Ahaz, king of Judah, is facing two enemy kings and is about to buy Assyrian
protection instead of trusting God. Isaiah offers him a sign — *ask for anything,
as deep as the grave or as high as heaven* — and Ahaz refuses, in a show of false
piety.

So the sign is given anyway, over his refusal. *Therefore the Lord Himself will
give you a sign.*

## The word

The Hebrew is **almah** — a young woman of marriageable age. It is used a handful
of times in the Old Testament and never of a married woman.

Two centuries before Christ, Jewish scholars translating into Greek chose
**parthenos**, which means virgin specifically. That is the reading Matthew
quotes, and it was made by translators with no Christian argument to advance.

## The name

*She will call his name Immanuel* — with us, God.

Matthew quotes the verse and then translates the name for his readers, which is
how he tells you what he thinks it means.

And the Gospel closes on the same note: *I am with you always, to the very end of
the age.* The name at the beginning and the promise at the end are the same
claim.

## The near horizon

*Before the boy knows enough to reject the wrong and choose the right, the land
whose two kings you dread will be left empty.*

The sign had an immediate function — a timescale for Ahaz, measured in a child's
early years. Prophecy in Isaiah often works on two horizons at once, and the near
one does not cancel the far one.

> **What Ahaz did with it.** He went to Assyria anyway, sent the temple silver as
> tribute, and got the protection he wanted along with everything that came
> after. The sign of Immanuel was given to a man who had already decided not to
> need it.
