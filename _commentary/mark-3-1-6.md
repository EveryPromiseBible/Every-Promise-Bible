The only place in the Gospels where Mark tells us Jesus was angry, and he tells us
what it was aimed at.

## The setup

*They were **watching Him closely** to see whether He would heal the man on the
Sabbath, **so that they would have something to accuse Him of**.*

The Greek for *watching closely* is to observe with sinister intent — to lie in
wait.

Nobody in the room is asking about the man's hand.

## What Jesus does with the staging

*"**Come and stand here where everyone can see you.**"*

He puts him in the middle. Whatever happens next is going to happen in public, and
Jesus arranges it that way deliberately.

He could have healed him quietly after the service. He calls him out to the front
first.

## The question

*"**Is it right on the Sabbath to do good, or to do harm? To save a life, or to
destroy one?**"*

Two pairs, and the second is sharper than it looks. While He is asking about
destroying a life on the Sabbath, they are in the process of planning His death — as
the last verse shows.

*But they would not say a word.*

The Greek is that they were silent. There is no answer to the question and they know
it.

## The anger

*He looked around at them, **angry and grieved at how hard their hearts had gone**.*

Two emotions in one clause. The Greek for *angry* is **orge**; the participle for
*grieved* means being pained together with, deeply distressed.

Matthew and Luke both record this scene and neither mentions the anger. Only Mark
does.

## What it was directed at

Not the man. Not sinners. Not the crowd.

*The hardness of their hearts* — the Greek is **porosis**, a medical word for a
callus, or bone forming over a break. Tissue that has gone hard and lost feeling.

He is angry at the calcification, and the same sentence says He is grieved by it.
Anger and grief over the same object, at the same time.

## The healing

*"**Stretch out your hand.**" He stretched it out, and the hand was made completely
whole.*

Nothing was done to him. No touch is recorded, no word over the hand, no prayer.

He is told to do the one thing a withered hand cannot do, and does it.

## What the healing produced

*And the Pharisees went straight out and **began plotting with Herod's men how they
might destroy Him**.*

The Pharisees and the Herodians had nothing in common and detested each other. A
restored hand united them.

Mark records the first plot against His life immediately after this.

> **Where Jesus' anger is not recorded.** With the woman caught in adultery. With
> Zacchaeus, or Matthew at the tax booth, or the woman at the well, or the thief on
> the cross. The Gospels show Him angry at hardness and at a temple market, and
> nowhere else.
