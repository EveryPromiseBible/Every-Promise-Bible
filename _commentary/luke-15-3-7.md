The first of the three stories in this chapter, told to people who were
complaining about the company Jesus kept.

## Why He is telling it

The chapter opens with tax collectors and sinners crowding in to hear Him, and
the Pharisees muttering: *this man welcomes sinners and eats with them.*

All three parables answer that one sentence. The complaint is the reason the
chapter exists.

## "Keep going until he finds it"

The searching has no stated limit. Not for a while, not until dark — until.

And the ninety-nine are left *out in the open country*. Jesus does not pause to
justify that. The shepherd's arithmetic is not the point of the story; the
finding is.

## What he does with it

*He lifts it up onto his shoulders, and the whole way home he is rejoicing.*

The sheep does nothing. It does not find its way back or follow him home — it is
carried the entire distance, on the shoulders of the one who came out for it.

And the mood is set on the road, not at the door. He is rejoicing before anyone
else knows.

## The party

*Celebrate with me — I have found my sheep.*

Every one of the three stories ends with neighbours being called together. The
joy is public each time, and the celebration is never at the recovered thing's
expense.

## The comparison at the end

*More joy over one sinner whose mind is turned around than over ninety-nine
upright people who have no need of it.*

The last phrase carries an edge. Jesus is describing people who see no need of
repentance, and He is looking at some while He says it.

> **What the three stories add up to.** A sheep that wandered off, a coin that
> could not have moved itself, and a son who walked out deliberately. Different
> ways of being lost — and in every case somebody goes looking, and there is a
> party afterwards. See the notes on Luke 15:8–10, 15:17–20 and 15:21–24.
