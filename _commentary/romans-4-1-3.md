Paul argues his case from the one man his readers could not dismiss.

## Why Abraham

Because he is the fixed point.

Everything a first-century Jewish reader believed about standing with God ran through him. He
is the father of the nation, the one circumcision was given to, and the pattern case in every
rabbinic discussion of merit.

If Paul can settle it on Abraham, the argument is over.

## The question

*What shall we say, then, about **Abraham, our father according to the flesh**?*

*What did he find?*

The Greek is **heurekenai** — to have found, discovered, obtained.

## The hypothetical

*If Abraham was **put right with God by what he accomplished**, he has **something to be proud
of**.*

The Greek is **kauchema** — a boast, a ground for boasting.

Paul concedes it freely. A man who earns something is entitled to say so.

## The limit

*But **not in front of God**.*

The Greek is **all' ou pros theon**.

The concession dies in the second half of the sentence. Whatever Abraham could claim before
men, there is one audience where the claim does not stand.

## The verdict

*Listen to what the Scripture says: "**Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him as
righteousness**."*

Genesis 15:6. See the note on Genesis 15:6.

Paul quotes it as a settled text and builds the chapter on it. James quotes the same verse to
make a different point, and both are working from one sentence in Genesis.

## The timing argument

Paul's next move is chronological, and it is devastating in its simplicity.

*How then was it accounted? While he was circumcised, or uncircumcised? **Not while
circumcised, but while uncircumcised**.*

Genesis 15 is the accounting. Genesis 17 is the circumcision. Fourteen years apart, by the
narrative's own dating.

And the law is four hundred and thirty years later still.

## The wage

*Now to him who works, the wages are not counted as grace but as **debt**.*

The Greek is **opheilema** — what is owed.

*But to him who does not work but believes on Him who **justifies the ungodly**, his believing is
accounted for righteousness.* See the note on Romans 4:4–5.

**Justifies the ungodly** — the phrase that would have been the hardest for Paul's readers, and
he states it without softening.

## The other witness

*Just as David also describes the blessedness of the man to whom God imputes righteousness
apart from works: "**Blessed are those whose lawless deeds are forgiven**."* See the note on
Romans 4:6–8.

Two witnesses, the patriarch and the king, and Deuteronomy requires two.

## What Abraham had actually done by Genesis 15

Left home, which is to his credit.

And lied about his wife in Egypt to save himself, which is not. See the note on
Genesis 12:10–20.

He does it a second time, years later, at Gerar. See the note on
Genesis 20:1–2.

The man Genesis credits with righteousness is the man whose record includes both.

## The word

**Elogisthe** — counted, reckoned, put to his account.

An accounting term. Paul uses it eleven times in this chapter.

Nothing is described as being produced in Abraham. Something is entered in a ledger.

## Where the chapter ends

*It was not written for his sake alone that it was imputed to him, but **also for us**.* See
the note on Romans 4:23–25.

> **What Paul concedes and where it dies.** A man who earns something may boast — and then the
> same sentence adds *but not before God*.
