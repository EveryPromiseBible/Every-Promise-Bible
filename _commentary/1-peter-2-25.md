One sentence, and it is the last line of the passage about the cross.

## What comes immediately before

*Who Himself bore our sins in His own body on the tree… **by whose stripes you were
healed**.* See the note on 1 Peter 2:24.

Then this verse, with no break. The sheep sentence is the conclusion of the stripes
sentence.

## The wandering

*You had **wandered off, the way sheep do**.*

Peter is quoting Isaiah. *All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned, every one, to
his own way.* See the note on Isaiah 53:4–6.

The Greek is **planomenoi** — straying, from the root of *planet*, the wandering stars.

Sheep do not run away. They graze in a direction, one mouthful at a time, and look up
somewhere else.

## The tense

*Now you **have come back**.*

The Greek is **epestraphete** — an aorist passive. *You were turned back*.

Peter does not describe a search that succeeded. He describes people who were turned.

## The title

*To **your Shepherd**.*

The Greek is **poimena**, and Peter is the man to whom this word was said three times over
breakfast: *feed My lambs*, *tend My sheep*, *feed My sheep*. See the note on John 21:4–8.

He knew what it was to wander, and to be turned back on a beach.

## The second title

*The One who **keeps watch over your souls**.*

The Greek is **episkopon** — overseer, the word behind *bishop*.

Shepherd and Overseer, side by side, of one flock.

## Where Peter uses it again

*Shepherd the flock of God which is among you, serving as **overseers**… and when **the
Chief Shepherd** appears, you will receive the crown of glory that does not fade away.*

Same two words, in his instructions to elders three chapters later. He is telling them what
they are a copy of.

## The three shepherd sentences

*I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd **gives His life** for the sheep.* See the note
on John 10:11–13.

*The great Shepherd of the sheep*, in Hebrews, *brought up from the dead*.

*The Chief Shepherd*, in Peter, *when He appears*.

Good, at the cross. Great, at the resurrection. Chief, at the return.

## The psalm underneath

*The LORD is my shepherd; **I shall not want**.*

And the line in the middle of it: *He restores my soul* — the Hebrew is *He brings my soul
back*, the same idea as Peter's returning.

## What the sentence does not say

Nothing about how far, how long, or what was done out there.

Isaiah's line is *every one to his own way*, and Peter's answer to all of it is one verb in
the passive.

> **Where the passage lands.** Not on the wandering, and not on a search. *You were turned
> back* — and the one they were turned to is named twice, as the one who feeds and the one
> who watches.
