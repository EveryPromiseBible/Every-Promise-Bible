An angel arrives in an unimportant town and says something that frightens a young
woman — not because it is a threat, but because of what it implies.

## What Gabriel actually says

*Rejoice, you who have been so greatly favoured! The Lord is with you.*

The Greek behind *greatly favoured* is one word, **kecharitomene** — a perfect
passive participle built on *charis*, grace. Something like: you who have been
graced, and stand in that state.

It is passive. Nothing is said about what Mary has done. The word describes
something done **to** her, already complete.

## Her reaction

*The words unsettled her all the way through, and she turned them over, trying to
understand what kind of greeting this could possibly be.*

Luke says the greeting disturbed her, not the angel. She is troubled by being
addressed that way — and then she does what Luke will say she does twice more in
two chapters: she thinks about it rather than answering quickly.

## Where the story is set

*A town in Galilee called Nazareth* — a village of a few hundred people, never
mentioned once in the Old Testament. Luke has just come from the temple in
Jerusalem, where the other annunciation in this chapter happened, to a priest, at
the altar, at the hour of incense.

The contrast is deliberate and Luke does not comment on it. He simply reports
where the second angel went.

> **What follows.** Mary's question is not *why me* but *how* — a practical
> objection, and Gabriel answers it. Then: *let it be to me according to your
> word.* Luke gives her more speaking lines in this chapter than anyone else, and
> the longest of them is a song about God putting the powerful down and lifting
> the low.
