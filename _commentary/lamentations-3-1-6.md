The middle poem opens in the first person, and the noun it opens with is chosen.

## The first word

*I am **the man** who has walked through the dark.*

The Hebrew is **ani haggever** — I am the man.

Not **adam**, humanity. Not **ish**, a male person. **Gever** — from **gavar**, to be strong. The
word for a man at full strength, the one you would expect to withstand something.

The poem's first claim is that this happened to the strong one.

## What it says God did

*Who has felt the weight of His hand come down… He **led me out where there was no light**…
against me He **turned His hand again and again**… my skin, my flesh, He has worn away; **my bones
He has broken**… He **built a wall around me**… He set me down **in the deep places, in the
darkness where the long-dead lie**.*

Every verb has God as its subject. There is no enemy in the paragraph.

The Hebrew for the last line is **bemachashakkim kemetei olam** — in dark places like those long
dead.

## Where the darkness turns up

*Now from the sixth hour until the ninth hour **there was darkness over all the land**.*

*"**My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?**"*

**Matthew** records darkness at noon and a sentence in which God is the one being addressed and
the one held responsible. Lamentations 3 is written by a man saying that about a city that
deserved it.

## The bones

*My bones He has broken.*

*But when they came to **Jesus** and saw that He was already dead, they did not break His legs…
For these things were done that the Scripture should be fulfilled, "**Not one of His bones shall
be broken**."*

**John** 19 quotes the Passover regulation. The **gever** of Lamentations 3 has his bones broken;
the one the soldiers came to did not, and **John** says the exemption was written into the law
about a lamb.

## Where the poem goes

Fifteen verses of this, and then verse 21: *this I recall to my mind, therefore I have hope.*

*Because of the LORD's great love we are not swept away.* See the note on
Lamentations 3:22–24.

The turn does not deny a line of what precedes it. The man who says *great is Your faithfulness*
is the same man who has just said God broke his bones, and both sentences are in the same poem
by design.

> **The noun.** *Gever* — the strong man, the one built to take it. The middle poem of the book
> opens by saying that this is who it happened to.
