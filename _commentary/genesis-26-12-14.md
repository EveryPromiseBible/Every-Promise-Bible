Three verses, and the timing is everything.

## When he planted

The chapter opens with a famine — *besides the one in Abraham's time* — and God
telling Isaac not to go down to Egypt but to stay put.

So the sowing happens in a year when the land was not producing, in a country
belonging to somebody else, by a man who had just been told not to go where the
food was.

## The harvest

*Isaac planted crops in that land, and that same year he reaped a hundredfold,
because the LORD blessed him.*

A hundredfold was extraordinary in ordinary conditions — Jesus uses it as the top
end of the good-soil yield in the parable of the sower. In a famine it is
absurd.

And the writer gives the reason in the same sentence, without elaboration:
*because the LORD blessed him.*

## The word "sowed"

The Hebrew is plain agriculture: he put seed in the ground. There is no ritual
described, no vow, no negotiation.

What preceded it is in the verses just before — God's promise to be with him and
bless him if he stayed. Isaac stayed, and then he planted, which is what a man
does with land he intends to remain on.

## What followed

*His wealth kept increasing until he became very wealthy… so many flocks and
herds and servants that the Philistines grew envious of him.*

The chapter does not stop there, and it is worth reading on: the envy leads to
his wells being stopped up, and to Abimelech asking him to leave. The blessing
brought friction with the neighbours almost immediately.

> **What Isaac does about the wells.** He digs them again, is driven off, digs
> again, is driven off again, and digs a third — and names it Rehoboth, *room*,
> saying the LORD has made room for us. The prosperity in this chapter is not
> frictionless. It is a man who keeps digging.
