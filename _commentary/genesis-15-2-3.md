God has just said something enormous, and Abram answers with the one thing he does not
have.

## What was said

The verse before: *"Do not be afraid, Abram. **I am your shield, your exceedingly great
reward**."* See the note on Genesis 15:1.

## The answer

*But Abram **answered from the heart, laying his ache before God**.*

*"LORD God, **what could You possibly give me? I go on childless**."*

The Hebrew is *what will You give me, seeing I go childless*.

He is not thanking Him. He is asking what the reward is worth without the one thing.

## What is remarkable about it

Nothing in the passage rebukes him.

God has just called Himself a shield and a great reward, and Abram effectively asks what
the point is. Genesis records the complaint and then records the answer.

## The problem stated

*"The one who **stands to inherit everything I own is a servant in my house, Eliezer of
Damascus**."*

Under the customs of the region, a childless man could adopt a trusted servant as heir.
The arrangement was legal and normal, and Abram had evidently made it.

He has done the sensible thing and it is not what he wants.

## The restatement

*"You've **given me no children of my own** — a servant born under my roof will be my
heir."*

He says it twice. The second version is blunter.

## How long he has been waiting

He was seventy-five when he was told *I will make you a great nation*. He is somewhere in
his eighties here, and Isaac arrives when he is a hundred.

## The answer

*"**This one shall not be your heir, but one who will come from your own body shall be your
heir**."*

And then outside, at night: *"Look now toward heaven, and count the stars if you are able to
number them… **so shall your descendants be**."* See the note on Genesis 15:4–5.

## The verse that follows

*And he **believed in the LORD, and He accounted it to him for righteousness**.* See the
note on Genesis 15:6.

Paul builds Romans 4 and Galatians 3 on that sentence — and it is spoken over a man who
has just complained.

The believing in verse 6 comes directly after the complaint in verse 2.

## What happens later in the chapter

The covenant — animals divided, birds of prey driven off, a deep sleep, and *a smoking oven
and a burning torch that passed between those pieces*.

Abram is asleep for it. Only God walks the path between the halves, which in that ritual
meant only one party carried the obligation.

## What he does next

Chapter 16. Sarai suggests Hagar, and he agrees.

The complaint was heard and answered, and he still tried to arrange it himself. See the
note on Genesis 21:8–11.

> **What the passage permits.** The complaint. God makes the largest promise in the chapter
> and Abram asks what it is worth — and four verses later he is counted righteous for
> believing.
