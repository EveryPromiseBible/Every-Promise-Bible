God forbids putting Him to the test everywhere else in scripture. Here He invites
it, and the subject is giving.

## Where the passage begins

Not with money. *Return to Me, and I will return to you.* The tithe question
arrives inside an invitation to come back, and it is God's answer to their own
question — *how are we to return?*

That order matters. This is not a demand for revenue. It is a description of what
turning back looks like for a people who had drifted.

## The invitation

*Bring the whole tithe into the storehouse… **Test Me in this**, says the LORD
Almighty, and see if I will not throw open the floodgates of heaven and pour out
so much blessing that there won't be room enough to store it.*

Three promises follow, and they are specific:

- the floodgates opened — the same word used of the windows of heaven at the flood
- **the devourer rebuked** — the locust stopped before it reaches the crop
- the vine not dropping its fruit early

Two of the three are about **protection** rather than increase. What is already
coming is guarded so it arrives.

---

## The tithe is older than the law

This is the point that decides how a Christian reads it. Abraham gave a tenth to
Melchizedek in Genesis 14 — four hundred and thirty years before Sinai, with no
commandment anywhere requiring it. Jacob promised a tenth at Bethel before there
was a law to obey.

So the tenth did not begin as a Mosaic regulation and does not stand or fall
with one. It began as something a man did in response to a priest who blessed
him, which is the order it still runs in: the blessing first, the tenth after.

And Hebrews says the priest receiving it now is the living one. See the note on
Hebrews 7:8.

## What this passage is not for

It is not a lever for putting a believer in fear. Christ took the curse of the
law at the cross, and a Christian giving under threat has misread which covenant
they are standing in.

Read it the way it is written and the fear drops out anyway. *Test Me* is an
invitation from someone confident of the outcome, addressed to people who had
stopped expecting anything from Him. The passage is trying to get them to find
out that He gives.

> **"All the nations will call you blessed."** The promise does not stop at full
> barns. It ends with a people so evidently cared for that everyone around
> notices — *yours will be a land of delight*. What is being described is not a
> transaction but a reputation.
