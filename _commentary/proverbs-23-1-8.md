Eight verses about dinner, and the two meals bracket a warning about money.

## The first table

*When you **sit down to dine with a ruler**, consider carefully what is before you.*

The Hebrew is *consider diligently what is before you*.

An invitation to a great man's table is not hospitality. It is a transaction, and the guest is
the one being assessed.

## The knife

*And **put a knife to your throat** if you are a person of large appetite.*

The Hebrew is **baal nephesh** — a master of appetite, an owner of a big soul.

Deliberately shocking language, and the point is self-restraint at somebody else's expense.

## The reason

*Do not crave his delicacies, for **that food is deceptive**.*

The Hebrew is **lechem kezavim** — bread of lies.

The food is real. What it costs is not on the menu.

## The pivot

*Do not **wear yourself out to get rich**; be wise enough to **let it go**.*

The Hebrew is **al-tiga** — do not labour, do not toil to exhaustion.

And *cease from your own understanding*.

## The image

*Fix your eyes on wealth, and **it is gone**, for it **sprouts wings and flies off like an eagle
to the sky**.*

The Hebrew is *will you set your eyes on that which is not?*

Riches are described as a thing that is not there, and then as a bird.

The eagle is the fastest thing the writer could name.

## The second table

*Do not eat the bread of **a stingy man**, or crave his delicacies.*

The Hebrew is **ra ayin** — one who has an evil eye.

That is the idiom for a miser. And it is the phrase behind the eye saying: *when your eye is
clear and single, your whole body is full of light*. See the note on
Luke 11:33–36.

The opposite of a *single* eye, in Hebrew, is a grudging one.

## The diagnosis

*For he is the kind who is **always counting the cost**.*

The Hebrew is *as he thinks in his heart, so is he*.

That clause has been lifted out of context for a century and a half and made to mean something
about positive thinking. In its place it is about a host doing arithmetic while you eat.

*"Eat and drink," he says to you, **but his heart is not with you**.*

## The outcome

*You will **vomit up the little you have eaten** and **waste your kind words on him**.*

The Hebrew is *your pleasant words*.

Two losses: the food and the conversation.

## What the two tables have in common

Both are free meals, and both cost something.

At the ruler's table the danger is your own appetite. At the miser's the danger is his
resentment.

The proverb sets them either side of the passage about chasing wealth, and the arrangement is
the argument.

## Where the chapter goes next

*Do not overwork to be rich… will you set your eyes on that which is not?*

And later: *do not remove the ancient landmark, nor enter the fields of the fatherless; **for
their Redeemer is mighty; He will plead their cause against you***.

## What Paul says about the same appetite

*But those who desire to be rich fall into temptation and a snare… **for the love of money is a
root of all kinds of evil**.*

And the alternative: *now godliness with contentment is great gain*.

> **Where the money passage sits.** Between two free dinners. One tests your appetite and the
> other tests the host's, and the proverb puts *do not wear yourself out to get rich* in the
> middle.
