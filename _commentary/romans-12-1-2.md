The turn in the letter, and it is one word wide.

## The therefore

*Therefore, brothers and sisters.*

Eleven chapters come first.

Sin, judgment, justification by believing, Abraham, peace with God, union with Christ, the law, the
Spirit, no condemnation, the eternal purpose, Israel, and the doxology at the end of chapter 11:
*oh, the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and knowledge of God!*

Then the first imperative of the letter.

Paul never puts the instruction first. The proportion here is eleven chapters to four.

## The ground

*By **the mercies of God** I appeal to you.*

The Greek is **dia ton oiktirmon tou theou** — through the compassions of God, plural.

Not *for the sake of*. *By* — he is appealing on the strength of them.

That is the leverage he chooses, having spent eleven chapters describing them.

## The request

*Bring your bodies to Him as **a living sacrifice**.*

The Greek is **parastesai** — to present, place at somebody's disposal. The same verb he used
of the members in Romans 6. See the note on Romans 6:12–14.

**Bodies** — not hearts, not souls. The part that goes places and does things.

## The oxymoron

*A **living** sacrifice.*

Every sacrifice in Leviticus was killed. That is what made it one.

Paul puts the two words together deliberately, and the difficulty in the phrase is the point: a
sacrifice that keeps getting up off the altar.

## The description

*Set apart for Him and **pleasing in His sight**.*

The Greek is **hagian** and **euareston** — holy, and well-pleasing.

The sacrificial vocabulary continues. Leviticus called the smoke *a sweet aroma to the LORD*.
See the note on Leviticus 1:3–9.

## The word

*This is worship offered with your **whole reasoning self**.*

The Greek is **logiken latreian** — and it is genuinely hard to translate.

**Logikos** — rational, reasonable, or *of the word*. **Latreia** — priestly service.

The older versions have *reasonable service*; others have *spiritual worship*. Both are
defensible.

Whatever else it means, it locates worship in the ordinary use of a mind and a body rather than
in a building.

## The mould

*Do not let the present age **press you into its own mold**.*

The Greek is **suschematizesthe** — from **schema**, the outward shape something is pressed
into.

Peter uses the same verb: *do not let your old cravings press you back into the shape you had*.
See the note on 1 Peter 1:14–16.

## The alternative

*Be **transformed** instead.*

The Greek is **metamorphousthe** — the word behind *metamorphosis*.

It is the verb used of the transfiguration, and of the beholding in 2 Corinthians: *we all…
beholding as in a mirror the glory of the Lord, **are being transformed** into the same image*.
See the note on 2 Corinthians 3:16–18.

Both are present passives. Stop being pressed; go on being transformed.

## Where

*From the inside, by **the making new of your mind**.*

The Greek is **anakainosei tou noos** — the renewing of the mind.

The mould works from outside. The transformation is named as starting somewhere else.

## The outcome

*So that you can **discern what the will of God is** — what is **good, and pleasing to Him, and
whole**.*

The Greek is **dokimazein** — to test and approve, the word for assaying metal.

Not obtain it. Recognise it.

The will of God is described here as something a renewed mind becomes able to identify, and
three adjectives are attached: good, acceptable, and **teleion** — complete, finished, entire.

> **What he appeals by.** Not duty and not fear. *By the mercies of God* — which he has spent
> eleven chapters establishing before asking for anything at all.
