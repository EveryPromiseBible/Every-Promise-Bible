Four verses of case law, and the arithmetic in them is deliberate.

## Where this sits

The Book of the Covenant — the chapters immediately after the ten commandments, which apply
them to cases.

*You shall not steal* is one sentence. This is what the courts did with it.

## The rates

*If a man steals an **ox** or a **sheep** and **kills or sells it**, he must repay **five
oxen** for the ox and **four sheep** for the sheep.*

Two different multiples for two different animals, and the higher one is for the working
animal.

An ox ploughs. Steal a man's ox and you have taken his next harvest as well as the beast.

## The lower rate

*If the stolen animal is **found alive in his hands**, whether ox, donkey, or sheep, he
**repays double**.*

Five or four if it has been killed or sold; two if it is still standing in his yard.

The scale tracks how far the theft has gone, not how bad the intention was.

## The night clause

*If a thief is **caught breaking in at night** and is struck so that he dies, **there is no
bloodguilt**.*

The Hebrew is *no blood for him*.

## The daylight clause

*But if it happens in **daylight, there is bloodguilt**.*

The distinction is about what the householder can know.

In the dark he cannot see whether the intruder is armed or how many there are; in daylight
he can, and killing him becomes homicide.

The law protects the defender at night and the thief by day.

## What if he cannot pay

*A thief must make **full restitution**; if he has nothing, **he is to be sold** to pay for
his theft.*

Not imprisonment. Labour, until the debt is worked off — and the Hebrew slave laws limit
that to six years, with release in the seventh.

Nothing in the Torah's penal code is a jail sentence. The penalties are restitution, labour,
or death.

## Who gets paid

The victim. Every shekel of it.

There is no fine to the state anywhere in the passage. The whole apparatus exists to put the
injured party back where he was, and further.

## The other rate

Leviticus sets a different figure for the man who confesses before he is caught: the value
plus a fifth. See the note on Leviticus 6:1–7.

Fourfold or fivefold when the court finds you. A hundred and twenty per cent when you come
forward.

## Where the fourfold turns up

David, hearing Nathan's story about the rich man and the lamb: *he shall **restore fourfold**
for the lamb, because he did this thing and had no pity.*

He is quoting this statute — and then Nathan says *you are the man*.

And Zacchaeus, at his own table: *if I have taken anything from anyone by false accusation,
I restore fourfold*. See the note on Luke 19:1–10.

Neither man was compelled. Both named the number themselves.

## What the chapter goes on to

Grazing another man's field, a fire that spreads to a neighbour's stack, goods left with a
friend, a borrowed animal that dies.

And then, without a break: *you shall neither mistreat a stranger nor oppress him, for you
were strangers in the land of Egypt*, and *you shall not afflict any widow or fatherless
child*.

The property law and the compassion law are in the same chapter with no seam between them.

> **What the multiples measure.** Not malice. An ox that has been killed costs five and one
> still standing in the yard costs two — the scale follows how much of the man's living went
> with it.
