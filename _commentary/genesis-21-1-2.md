Twenty-five years after the promise, two verses record its arrival, and they are
almost entirely made of repetition.

## The repeated phrase

*The LORD came to Sarah, just as He had said He would. He did for her exactly
what He had promised… right on time, at the very season God had spoken of.*

Three times in two verses: as He said, as He promised, at the appointed time. The
Hebrew is even more insistent than the English.

The writer is not being redundant. He is making the only point that matters about
a promise delayed by a quarter of a century — it did not arrive early, and it
did not fail.

## What had happened in between

The intervening chapters are not a story of patient waiting. Abraham laughed at
the promise. Sarah laughed at it. They tried to arrange it themselves through
Hagar, with consequences that run for the rest of Genesis. Twice Abraham passed
his wife off as his sister to save himself.

None of that is edited out, and none of it changes the timetable.

## The name

Isaac means *he laughs*. The child is named after the reaction the promise
provoked, and Sarah says so herself in the verses following: *God has brought me
laughter, and everyone who hears about this will laugh with me.*

The joke told at God's expense becomes the boy's name.

> **The line the Illumination adds.** *Nothing was too late. Nothing was
> forgotten.* It is a translator's summary rather than a separate clause in the
> Hebrew, and it is a fair rendering of what the triple repetition is doing. The
> passage exists to say that the delay was not neglect.
