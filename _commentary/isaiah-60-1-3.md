Two imperatives, and then the reason for both.

## The order of the sentence

*Arise. Shine. **Your light has come**, and the glory of the LORD rises upon
you.*

The commands come first and the reason second, and the reason is not an
instruction to generate anything. The light *has come*. The glory *rises upon*
you — the verb is the one used of the sun coming up over a horizon.

Nobody is being told to produce light. They are being told to get up, because
something has risen on them.

## The darkness is not denied

*Look, darkness covers the earth, thick darkness over the peoples.*

The second word is heavier than the first — a deep, enveloping dark. Isaiah puts
it right between the command to shine and the promise of nations coming, and does
not soften it.

The contrast is the point: *but the LORD rises upon you*. Both things are true at
once, and the light is not described as ending the darkness elsewhere. It is
described as landing here.

## Who ends up looking

*Nations will come to your light, and kings to the brightness of your dawn.*

The light attracts. That is the whole mechanism given — no campaign, no
persuasion. Something is lit in a dark landscape, and people walk toward it.

> **Where it lands in the New Testament.** Jesus tells a hillside crowd *you are
> the light of the world* and *a town built on a hill cannot be hidden* — the
> same picture of light in a dark place drawing eyes to it. Both passages put the
> attention that follows on someone else: nations coming to *your* light, and
> people giving glory to *your Father*. See the note on Matthew 5:13–16.

---

## What arrives once the light is up

The chapter does not stop at the dawn. The next two verses tell the city to look
around at who is walking toward it — sons from far away, daughters carried close
and safe, and then the wealth of the sea and the riches of nations streaming in.

The order is worth holding on to. The light comes first and it is not the city's
own; then people come to it; and only then does anything arrive that could be
counted. Isaiah never presents the traffic as the point — it is the consequence
of something having risen. See the note on Isaiah 60:4–5.

And the darkness is not said to lift everywhere. *Darkness covers the earth,
thick darkness over the peoples* stays true in the same breath as *the LORD rises
upon you*. Whatever this chapter is promising, it is not a world with the lights
back on. It is one lit place in a dark landscape, and a road toward it.
