Paul names a situation in one sentence and then turns, immediately, to the
congregation's reaction to it.

## What is being reported

*Immorality of a kind **not found even among the nations around you** — that a
man is living with his father's wife.*

Roman law itself forbade this; Cicero calls a comparable case an almost unheard-of
crime. Paul's measure is not the synagogue but the pagan city, and Corinth was not
a strict place.

*His father's wife*, not *his mother* — the phrase indicates a stepmother, and
Leviticus 18 names it directly.

## The turn

*And **you** are swollen with pride over it.*

The Greek verb is the one Paul has been using through the whole letter for the
Corinthians' inflation — puffed up, blown out like a bellows. He has used it in
chapter 4 twice already.

Two verses in, and the subject has changed from the man to the church.

## What should have been there instead

*Should you not rather have been **broken with grief**?*

The word is the one used of mourning the dead. Paul does not ask for outrage,
disgust, or a statement. He asks for a funeral.

And notice the order in his sentence: *so that the man… would be taken out from
among you.* The mourning comes first, and the action follows from it. A church
that had grieved would have known what to do.

## Why they were proud

The letter's whole argument gives the answer. Corinth prized wisdom, freedom,
and being beyond the reach of small rules. A man doing this and remaining in
fellowship was, in that atmosphere, a demonstration of how free they were.

> **What Paul does not do.** He does not write to the man. The letter is addressed
> to the church, the pride is the church's, and the correction lands there. See the
> notes on 1 Corinthians 5:3–5 and 1 Corinthians 5:9–11.
