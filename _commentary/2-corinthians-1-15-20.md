This is the verse this app opens on, and it is usually quoted from halfway
through. The half that gets left off is the reason Paul wrote it.

## He is defending a change of travel plans

That is genuinely what starts it. Paul said he would visit, then did not, and
somebody in Corinth used it to suggest he was unreliable — a man whose yes meant
nothing.

His answer runs: *do I make my plans the way anyone makes them, so that with me
Yes, yes and No, no come out of the same mouth in the same breath?* Then, instead
of defending his diary, he moves straight to the character of the message he
preached. My travel arrangements are not the point, he says. What we brought you
was never *Yes and No at once*.

Everything after this — the most quoted sentence in the letter — is Paul arguing
from God's consistency, not his own.

## "However many promises God has made"

The phrase is deliberately open-ended. Not *some*. Not *the important ones*.
However many there are, they all find their Yes in one place: **in Him.**

Which locates them. The promises are not free-floating guarantees attached to
circumstances, and they are not conditional on the reader's steadiness — the
whole argument began with Paul's own unsteadiness. They are held in Christ, and
that is why they can be described with a word as flat as *always*.

---

## Where the Amen ends up

Here is the part almost always dropped:

> *and in Him the Amen, spoken through us to the glory of God.*

Amen is a Hebrew word meaning firm, settled, so be it. It is what a congregation
says back.

Paul's picture has both halves: God says Yes in Christ, and the Amen comes back
*through us*. The promises are answered by God, and then answered again by the
people who trust them. Nobody has to generate the Yes. What is left for a person
to do is agree with it.

That is a very different act from working to secure the promise, and it is why
the sentence ends where it does — *to the glory of God*. The praise goes to the
one who said Yes, not to the one who managed to believe it.

## Why it matters that this began with a broken plan

Paul's point lands harder because of where it started. A man whose word had just
been questioned uses the moment to say: whatever you think of my reliability,
God's Yes does not run on the same terms.

> **On "a second helping of grace."** Paul's phrase for the visit he had planned.
> He thought of turning up in person as another portion of grace for them. It is
> a small thing, but it is the vocabulary of a man who thought of grace as
> something you get more of, not something you use up.
