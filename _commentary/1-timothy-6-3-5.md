Paul describes a kind of teacher, and the diagnosis is medical.

## The test

*If anyone teaches something other than this, and **will not come over to sound words, the
words of our Lord Jesus Christ**.*

The Greek for *sound* is **hugiainousi** — healthy, sound in body. The root of *hygiene*.

Paul uses it repeatedly in the pastoral letters: sound words, sound doctrine, sound in the
faith. The metaphor throughout is health.

## The second half of the test

*And to **the teaching that goes with a life turned toward God**.*

The Greek is **kat' eusebeian** — according to godliness.

Doctrine and life, and the teaching in question fails both.

## The character

*He is **swollen with his own importance** and **understands nothing at all**.*

The Greek is **tetuphotai** — from **tuphos**, smoke. To be puffed up, wrapped in fog.

Both in one clause: inflated, and knowing nothing.

## The diagnosis

*What he has is **a sickness: a craving for disputes and for battles over words**.*

The Greek is **noson** — a disease — and the participle means *morbidly craving*.

Paul reverses his own metaphor. The teaching is not *healthy*; the teacher is *sick*, and
the sickness is an appetite for argument.

**Battles over words** — **logomachias**, word-fights. A term Paul appears to have coined.

## What it produces

Five things, listed:

*Out of that come **envy, quarreling, insults, ugly suspicions**, and **the endless rubbing of
one man against another**.*

The last is **diaparatribai** — constant friction, wearing away by rubbing.

Every item is relational. None of them is a doctrinal error.

The fruit of the teaching is what identifies it.

## The people

*Among people **whose minds have been ruined** and **who have been robbed of the truth**.*

The Greek for *robbed* is **apesteremenon** — defrauded, deprived.

## The motive

*People who **imagine that godliness is a way of getting on in the world**.*

The Greek is *supposing that godliness is a means of gain* — **porismon**, a source of
income.

## The instruction

*Keep away from such people.*

Two words. Paul does not tell Timothy to argue with them.

## The counter-statement

*Now **godliness with contentment is great gain**.*

He takes the word they were using — **porismos**, gain — and reassigns it.

Their version is godliness as a way of getting; his is godliness plus contentment as the
getting.

## What follows

*For we brought nothing into this world, and it is certain we can carry nothing out. And
having food and clothing, with these we shall be content.*

And then the warning about those determined to be rich, and the misquoted verse about the
love of money. See the note on 1 Timothy 6:9–10.

## Where the chapter ends

*Command those who are rich in this present age **not to be haughty, nor to trust in
uncertain riches but in the living God, who gives us richly all things to enjoy**.* See the
note on 1 Timothy 6:17–19.

Paul warns about wanting money and then tells the wealthy to enjoy what they have and give
it away.

> **How the false teacher is identified.** Not by a doctrinal test in the first instance.
> By an appetite — a craving for disputes — and by five things that come out of it, all of
> which happen between people.
