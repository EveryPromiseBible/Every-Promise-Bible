*The servant took ten of his master's camels and set out, with all kinds of good
things from his master in his hands.*

The number is not incidental. Genesis counts things.

## Ten, and what it carries

Ten camels, each loaded, standing for the whole of what Abraham had to send. The
servant is not carrying a sample. He is carrying his master's substance out on
an errand, and every good thing in that caravan came from the man who sent him.

Abraham is the man who had already given a tenth to Melchizedek and refused to
take a thread from the king of Sodom. The wealth on those camels is what a man
has when he has handled his blessing that way — held loosely, given first,
received back with interest.

## What the wealth was for

Not display. Every item on those animals was going to be given away — to
Rebekah, to her mother, to her brother, before the servant would even eat.

That is the shape of the whole chapter. The blessing goes out ahead of the
servant, arrives before he explains himself, and secures the bride for the son.

## The picture the chapter draws

Christian readers have long seen the outline here and it is hard to miss: a
father sends a trusted servant to find a bride for his son, and the servant goes
loaded with the father's own riches to give away on the way.

The camels are how the errand is funded. Ten of them, fully laden, is Genesis
saying the sending was not done by halves.

> **What Rebekah does with them.** She waters all ten — somewhere between fifty
> and a hundred litres, hauled up by hand for animals belonging to a stranger.
> The chapter establishes her character by what she was willing to do for a
> caravan she had nothing to gain from, and then puts her at the head of the
> promise.
