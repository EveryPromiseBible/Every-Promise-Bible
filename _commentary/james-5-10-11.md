The only mention of Job in the New Testament, and it tells you what the whole book
was for.

## Who James points at

*Think about **the prophets**… take them as your pattern: what they had to take,
and how long they took it.*

He does not offer them as examples of success. He offers what they had to take,
and the length of time they took it for.

## "The ones who hold out"

The Greek is **hupomeno** — to remain under. Not to grit the teeth; to stay put
under a weight rather than get out from under it.

*The ones who hold out under it are the ones we call fortunate.*

## The verdict on Job

*You have heard how Job held out, and you have seen **what the Lord finally made
of it**.*

James assumes his readers know the story. What he tells them is where to look in
it.

Not at the boils. Not at the ashes, or the arguments, or the thirty-five chapters
of speeches. At **the end** — the Greek is *the end of the Lord*, the outcome the
Lord brought it to.

## What the outcome shows

*— that **He feels for people deeply, and He is kind all the way through**.*

Two words in the Greek, and the first is one James appears to have coined:
**polusplagchnos**, literally *many-boweled*, from the word for the inward parts,
where compassion was felt. Very compassionate. It occurs nowhere else in Greek
literature before him.

The second is **oiktirmon** — merciful, full of pity.

That is the New Testament's one-sentence summary of the book of Job, and it is not
about patience, endurance, or the mystery of suffering. It is a statement about
what God is like, drawn from how the story ended.

## What James is telling his readers to conclude

If you want to know what the Lord is, read to the end of that book. He is deeply
compassionate and kind all the way through — and the whole forty-two chapters are
offered as evidence for it.

Whatever the middle of it looked like, the end of it is what tells you His
character.

> **The famous phrase.** *The patience of Job* comes from the older English of this
> verse. The Greek word is not patience but staying under — and James's point is
> not really about Job at all. It is about what the Lord was, the whole time, and
> what He turned out to be at the end.
