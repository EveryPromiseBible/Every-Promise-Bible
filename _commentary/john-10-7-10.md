Jesus calls Himself two things in this chapter — the gate and the shepherd — and
the first one is the stranger claim.

## "I am the gate"

Sheepfolds in the hill country were stone enclosures with a single gap in the
wall. There was no door. At night the shepherd lay down across the opening and
slept there.

The shepherd *was* the gate. Nothing came in or went out except over him.

## What the gate is for

*Whoever comes in through Me will be saved, and will go in and go out and find
pasture.*

Two directions. A gate that only let you in would be a cage; this one is for
coming and going. *Going in and out* is a Hebrew idiom for living freely and
safely — the phrase used of Israel's leaders moving among the people.

Safety and freedom in one sentence, and the pasture is outside.

## The thief's three verbs

*The thief comes for one reason only — to steal, to kill, to destroy.*

Three, and they escalate. Take what you have, end what you are, ruin what is
left. Jesus gives no other motive: *for one reason only.*

## The contrast

*I have come so that they may have life, and have it overflowing.*

The Greek is *abundantly* — beyond the necessary measure, over and above. Not
merely enough life to go on with.

Set beside three verbs of destruction, the answer is one verb of giving, with an
adverb attached that means more than required.

> **Who the thieves were.** The chapter follows the healing of a blind man whom
> the religious authorities threw out of the synagogue for saying who healed him.
> Jesus' words about hirelings who abandon the sheep and thieves who come to
> destroy are said with those men in the room.
