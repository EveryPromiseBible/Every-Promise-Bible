The last verse of the psalm, and the verb in it is not a gentle one.

## "Pursue"

*Surely goodness and mercy will **pursue** me all the days of my life.*

The Hebrew is **radaph** — to chase, to hunt, to run after. It is the word used
for an army in pursuit, and for Pharaoh's chariots after Israel.

Most translations soften it to *follow*, which sounds like something trailing
along behind. The verb is a chase.

Two things are hunting him down, and they are goodness and mercy.

## The two names

**Tov** — goodness. **Chesed** — the covenant word, steadfast love, loyal
kindness. It is the word repeated twenty-six times in Psalm 136, once per verse:
*His mercy endures forever.*

## "All the days"

Every one. Not the good ones, not the ones he handled well.

The valley is in verse 4 and the enemies are in verse 5, and verse 6 says *all the
days of my life* without excluding either.

## What a shepherd's psalm ends with

The psalm has moved from pasture to water to valley to a table with enemies
watching, and then to a house.

*And I will **dwell in the house of the LORD forever**.*

The Hebrew is to sit, to remain, to settle — the word for taking up residence, not
visiting.

David wrote this. He was a man who spent his life in tents and caves and on the
run, and who was never allowed to build the house he wanted to build.

## The whole psalm in its place

Set between the cross and the coming King:

- **Psalm 22** — forsaken, pierced, *He has done it*
- **Psalm 23** — the shepherd, the valley, the table, and the house
- **Psalm 24** — *lift up your heads, O gates, that the King of glory may come in*

This is the middle one. It is the psalm for now: the walking, the days, the
pursuit. See the notes on Psalms 22:1–5 and Psalms 24:7–8.

> **The first verse and the last.** *The LORD is my shepherd, I shall not want* —
> and *I will dwell in the house of the LORD forever.* It opens with a want that
> does not exist and closes with an address that does not change.
