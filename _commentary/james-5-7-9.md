The instruction is patience, and the example is somebody who cannot make anything happen faster.

## The command

*So **hold on, brothers, until the Lord comes**.*

The Greek is **makrothumesate** — long-tempered, slow to boil.

The word is about people, not circumstances. Its opposite is the short fuse.

James has just finished four verses on rich men whose hired labourers were not paid — *the wages
of the labourers who mowed your fields, which you kept back by fraud, cry out*. The people told
to be long-tempered are the ones who were cheated.

## The example

*Look at **the farmer**.*

Of all occupations, the one with the least control over the timetable.

## Why he waits

*What the ground can give him **is worth a great deal to him**, so he sits and waits for it, **and
goes on waiting**.*

The Greek is **timion karpon** — the precious fruit.

The waiting is explained by the value, not by the farmer's temperament. He waits because it is
worth waiting for.

## The two rains

*Through **the first rains and then the late ones**.*

In Israel the early rain falls in October and November, softening ground hard from summer so it
can be ploughed and sown. The latter rain falls in March and April, filling out the grain before
harvest.

Between them, months in which nothing visible happens and nothing can be done.

## What a farmer cannot do

Speed it up. He can plough, sow, and wait, and the interval belongs to somebody else.

## The application

*Wait like that yourselves. **Put some steel in your hearts**.*

The Greek is **sterixate tas kardias humon** — establish, make firm, brace your hearts.

Luke uses the same verb of Jesus: *He steadfastly set His face to go to Jerusalem*.

## The reason

*The Lord's coming is close now.*

The Greek is **engiken** — perfect tense. Has drawn near and stands near.

## The turn

*Do not grumble about one another, brothers, **or you will be the ones judged for it**.*

The Greek is **me stenazete** — do not groan against one another.

The word is a sound, not an argument. Sighing at each other.

## Why that follows

Pressure applied from outside tends to come out sideways, on the people nearest.

James has spent the letter on this: *who is wise among you? Let him show by good conduct*, and
*where envy and self-seeking exist, confusion and every evil thing are there*. See the note on
James 3:13–16.

## The last line

*There is **the Judge, right outside the door**.*

The Greek is **pro ton thuron hesteken** — stands before the doors.

The nearness that made the waiting bearable in verse 8 is the same nearness that makes the
grumbling dangerous in verse 9.

One fact, and James uses it both ways in two sentences.

## What comes next

*Take the prophets as an example of suffering and patience*, and then Job — *you have heard of
the perseverance of Job and seen the end intended by the Lord*. See the note on
James 5:10–11.

Farmers, prophets, and Job. Three sets of people who waited without a schedule.

> **The example.** A farmer, because there is nothing he can do to bring it forward. And the
> reason given for his waiting is not his character — it is what the ground is worth.
