Nine words about farm animals, quoted twice in the New Testament about people.

## The law

*Do not muzzle an ox while it treads out the grain.*

The Hebrew is **lo-tachsom shor bedisho**.

## The work

Threshing was done on a flat stone floor. The cut grain was spread out and oxen were driven over
it, sometimes dragging a weighted sledge, until the kernels separated from the husks.

Slow work, in the heat, walking in circles.

## Why a muzzle

An unmuzzled ox eats as it goes. The animal is standing on food.

The prohibition costs the farmer grain, which is exactly why it needs to be a law.

## Where it sits

Between a law limiting judicial flogging to forty stripes — *lest your brother be humiliated in
your sight* — and the law of levirate marriage.

Its neighbours are about human dignity, and the section as a whole is a run of unrelated
regulations on treating people and animals decently.

## What the law is about on its face

Not cruelty, exactly. The ox is not harmed by a muzzle.

It is about withholding from something that is working. The animal produces the food and is
denied a mouthful of it.

## The first quotation

*For it is written in the law of Moses, "You shall not muzzle an ox while it treads out the
grain." **Is it oxen God is concerned about?***

Paul, arguing that those who preach should be supported. See the note on
1 Corinthians 9:7–10.

*Or does He say it altogether for our sakes? For our sakes, no doubt, this is written.*

## What Paul is doing

Not denying that God cares about animals — Deuteronomy has laws about a bird's nest and a fallen
donkey, and Jesus says not a sparrow falls unnoticed.

He is asking who the law was written down for. A law about oxen is preserved in a book given to
people, and Paul reads it as principle.

## The second quotation

*For the Scripture says, "You shall not muzzle an ox while it treads out the grain," and, "**The
labourer is worthy of his wages**."*

Paul to Timothy, on elders who labour in word and doctrine. See the note on
1 Timothy 5:17–18.

The second half of that verse is a saying of Jesus, quoted alongside Deuteronomy as *Scripture*.

## What Paul does with the right

*Yet **we have not used this right**; instead we bear all things, so that we may put no hindrance
in the way of the good news.* See the note on 1 Corinthians 9:11–14.

He argues the case at length and then declines to take it.

## The phrase he lands on

*The Lord has ordained that **those who preach the good news should live from the good news**.*

## What the ox does

Walks in circles over food, and is permitted to eat.

Paul's whole argument is that the person doing the work should not be standing on a harvest they
are not allowed to touch.

## Where else the ox turns up

*Where no oxen are, the trough is clean; **but much increase comes by the strength of an ox***. See
the note on Proverbs 14:1–7.

## The other law about the pair

*You shall not plough with an ox and a donkey together*, three verses after this one.

Two animals of different strength and stride, yoked to the same plough. Paul takes that image
too: *do not be unequally yoked together with unbelievers*.

> **The question Paul asks.** *Is it oxen God is concerned about?* — not a denial that He is, but
> a question about who the law was written down for.
