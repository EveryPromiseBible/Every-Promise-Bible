Six lines, and almost every one of them is a transfer: something that was ours,
moved onto him.

## What moves, and where it goes

- **took up** our pain — the Hebrew *nasa*, to lift and carry away
- **carried** our sorrows — *sabal*, to bear a load on the back
- pierced **for** our transgressions
- crushed **for** our iniquities
- the punishment that brought **us** peace was laid **upon him**
- the LORD has laid on him the guilt of **us all**

Nothing in the passage is described as being fixed in place. Everything is moved
from one party to another. That is the grammar of substitution, and it is why
this chapter is quoted more often in the New Testament than almost any other in
the prophets.

## The sentence in the middle

*Yet we thought him stricken by God, struck down by Him, and afflicted.*

This is the confession of the people speaking. They saw a man suffering and drew
the ordinary conclusion — that God must be against him. They were exactly wrong,
and the chapter is written from the far side of finding out.

It is worth remembering the next time suffering is read as a verdict.

## "By his wounds we are healed"

The Hebrew *rapha* is the ordinary word for healing, used of bodies and of
nations and of God healing a people's turning away. It is broad, and Isaiah does
not narrow it here.

What the sentence does specify is the mechanism: the healing is *by his wounds*.
Whatever is being healed, it is being healed at his cost and not at ours — which
is the argument of the whole chapter.

> **The last line.** *We all, like sheep, have wandered off. Each of us has
> turned to his own way.* Note the two halves: a herd going astray together, and
> every single one going a different direction. Both are true at once, and the
> chapter puts the collective and the individual in the same sentence before
> laying all of it on one man.
