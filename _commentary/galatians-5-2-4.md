Few verses have frightened more people than this one, and nearly always for the
opposite of the reason Paul wrote it. Read quickly, "you have fallen away from
grace" sounds like a warning that grace can run out — that a person can sin past
the edge of it and be dropped. Read in the sentence Paul actually built, it says
something closer to the reverse.

## Look at who he is describing

Paul names them in the same breath: *you who are being made right by the law*.
That is the person in view. Not someone who has sinned badly — someone who has
decided to earn their standing with God by keeping rules. The whole letter aims
at that one move.

Nothing here is addressed to the person who has failed. It is addressed to the
person who has started keeping score.

## The two pictures underneath

Two words carry the verse, and both are stranger in Greek than they sound in
English.

- **cut off** — the word means to be left with nothing to do, put out of work,
  made idle. Not cut in half.
- **fallen away** — a word used of a ship blown off its course, and of a flower
  dropping off its stem.

Put together, the picture is not of grace pulling back from a person. It is of a
person stepping off the ground they were standing on. Christ has not run out of
power; He has been *given nothing to do* by someone who decided to handle their
standing themselves.

## What Paul says two lines later

He goes straight on: *we, through the Spirit, wait with longing for the
righteousness we hope for* — and he says **we**. Having just written the hardest
sentence in the letter, he puts himself back in the same boat as the people he
wrote it to.

He is not telling them what they have permanently become. He is telling them
what their position costs them while they hold it, which is a different thing.
That is why the same chapter opens with *Christ has set us free*. A person
cannot stand on two grounds at once, and the ground they were reaching for
cannot hold anyone up.

## Why this matters practically

If grace could be forfeited by sinning, the remedy would be to sin less. Paul's
remedy is the opposite: stop trying to be made right by what you do. The fall he
describes is not downward, into failure. It is sideways, out of the only thing
that was holding anyone.

> A word about *grace* itself. In Greek it is not leniency, and it is not a mood
> God happens to be in. It runs from favour freely given through to the working
> of that favour in a person's life. So "fallen away from grace" is not "fallen
> out of God's good books." It is nearer to having stepped out of the current
> that was doing the work.
