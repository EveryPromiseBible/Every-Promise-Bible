*This trouble of ours is light, and it lasts a moment.* Coming from most people
that sentence would be worthless. Two chapters later Paul lists what he means by
trouble: beatings, imprisonments, shipwreck, a night and a day in open water,
hunger, cold, and being let down a wall in a basket to escape assassination.

He has earned the sentence, which is the only reason it can be read.

## The two pairs

The passage runs on opposites, and they are stacked:

- **outer person** wearing away — **inner person** being made new, *day after day*
- **light** and **a moment** — **weight** and **endless**
- **what can be seen** — **what cannot**

The middle pair is a deliberate joke in Hebrew terms. The word for glory,
*kavod*, comes from a root meaning heavy. Paul sets a light affliction against a
weight of glory: the very thing that feels heaviest is the lighter side of the
scale.

## "Day after day"

The renewal is not described as a single experience but as something happening on
a daily basis, in parallel with the wearing away. Both processes run at once,
and Paul does not pretend the first one stops.

That honesty is what makes the paragraph usable by someone in a hospital chair.
Nothing here says the outer wearing away is imaginary or will reverse.

> **"We keep our eyes not on what can be seen."** The verb is about aiming
> attention deliberately — the word behind our *scope*. It is not denial of the
> visible; Paul has just catalogued it. It is a decision about which set of facts
> gets to set the terms.
