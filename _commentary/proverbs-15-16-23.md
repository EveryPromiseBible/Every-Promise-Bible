The run ends on a line about timing, and everything before it is about what makes
a household bearable.

## The two "betters"

*Better a little with the fear of the LORD than great riches and trouble with it.
Better a plate of greens where love is than a fattened calf and hatred at the
table.*

Both compare a small good thing with a large bad one, and both put the difference
in the atmosphere rather than the menu.

A fattened calf was the best meal available — the thing killed for the prodigal's
homecoming. Proverbs says vegetables beat it if the room is hostile.

## The temper proverbs

*A hot temper stirs up strife. A patient one calms the quarrel down.*

The Hebrew for patient is *long of nostrils* — slow to flare. The idiom is the
same one used of God being slow to anger.

## The last line

*There is joy in giving the right answer, and **a word in season**, how good it
is.*

Two halves. The right answer, and the right moment — and Proverbs treats them as
separate achievements.

A true thing said at the wrong hour is not counted here as a success. The verse
just before it makes the same point from the other side: *the one who
understands walks straight ahead* rather than blurting.

## What it sits with

*Plans fall apart without counsel, but with many advisers they stand.* The
season-word proverb comes directly after it — which suggests the good answer is
often somebody else's, arriving when you needed it.

> **How to use a run like this.** Proverbs 15 is a chapter of contrasts about
> speech: a gentle answer turning away wrath at the top, a soothing tongue as a
> tree of life in the middle, and the word in season at the end. Read the chapter
> whole and the subject is not wisdom in the abstract. It is what comes out of a
> person's mouth in a house.
