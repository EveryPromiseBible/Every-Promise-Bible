Two verses, and the phrase in the middle of them is one nobody would have thought
to put together.

## What the release rests on

*"**Because of the blood of My covenant with you**, I will set your prisoners free."*

Not because of their record, their repentance, or their usefulness. Because of
blood, and because of a covenant.

The Hebrew is *the blood of your covenant* — the blood by which the covenant was
made.

Jesus uses the same phrase over the cup: *this is My blood of the new covenant,
poured out for many.* See the note on Matthew 26:26–30.

## The pit

*"From **the pit that has no water in it**."*

The Hebrew is a cistern — a bottle-shaped hole cut into rock for storing rain, and a
dry one was the standard improvised prison.

Joseph went into one: *the pit was empty; there was no water in it.* So did Jeremiah,
and he *sank in the mire.*

A dry cistern is not a place you climb out of. The sides curve inward and the neck
is above your reach.

## The phrase

*"Come home, **you prisoners who still have hope**."*

Prisoners **of hope**, in the older rendering — the Hebrew is *prisoners of the
hope*.

Being a prisoner and having hope do not normally belong in the same description. A
man in a dry cistern is defined by what he cannot do.

Zechariah names them by the thing they are holding rather than by the hole they are
in.

## The stronghold

*"**Return to your stronghold.**"*

The Hebrew for *stronghold* is a fortified place, a rock hold. The prophet is calling
them out of a pit and into a fortress.

## The double

*"**This very day I declare it. I will pay you back double.**"*

The Hebrew is *even today* — dated, immediate.

Double is the law's word for restitution. A thief who was caught restored twofold —
and here it is God doing the restoring rather than the thief.

Isaiah says the same to Israel: *instead of your shame you shall have double, and
instead of confusion they shall rejoice in their portion; therefore in their land
they shall possess double.*

And Job, at the end: *the LORD gave Job twice as much as he had before.* See the note
on Job 42:12–15.

## What comes just before this

Two verses earlier is one of the best-known lines in the prophets:

*Rejoice greatly, O daughter of Zion! Behold, your King is coming to you… **lowly,
and riding on a donkey**, a colt, the foal of a donkey.*

Matthew quotes it on Palm Sunday. The prisoners-of-hope promise is in the same
paragraph as the donkey.

> **What the prisoners are told to do.** Come home. Return. Both verbs are movement
> toward, and neither is about getting themselves out. The blood does the releasing;
> they do the walking.
