Written from a prison, and the instruction is to be glad.

## The command

*Be **glad in the Lord always**.*

The Greek is **chairete** — rejoice. And **pantote**, always, without a gap.

Paul is in Rome under guard, chained to a soldier, waiting on a verdict that could go
either way. He mentions the chains three times in the letter.

## The repetition

*I will say it **once more**: be glad.*

The Greek is *again I will say, rejoice*.

He has already said it in chapter 3 — *finally, my brethren, rejoice in the Lord* — and he
comes back for it.

Nothing else in the letter is doubled like this.

## Where the joy is located

*In **the Lord**.*

Not in circumstances, and not manufactured out of nothing. The location is named, and it is
the one thing about his situation that had not changed.

The word **chara**, joy, and its verb run through this short letter more than a dozen times.
It is the most cheerful thing Paul wrote and it was written in custody.

## The second instruction

*Let the **gentle give of your spirit** be plain to everyone.*

The Greek is **epieikes** — the hardest word in the letter to translate. Older versions have
*moderation*, *forbearance*, *gentleness*, *yieldedness*.

It means not standing on the letter of your rights. The willingness to take less than you
are owed rather than press the point.

## Who is to see it

*Be plain to **everyone**.*

The Greek is *to all men*. Not to the church.

Whatever this quality is, its audience is outside.

## The sentence in between

*The Lord is near.*

Three words, and they can be read two ways in Greek — near in place, or near in time.

Paul does not disambiguate, and probably means both. The next verse is about praying to
someone close enough to hear; the letter has already said *our citizenship is in heaven,
from which we also eagerly wait for the Saviour*.

## What follows immediately

*Be **anxious for nothing**, but in everything by prayer and supplication, with
thanksgiving, let your requests be made known to God; and the peace of God, which surpasses
all understanding, will guard your hearts and minds through Christ Jesus.* See the note on
Philippians 4:6–7.

The guarding verb is **phrouresei** — a garrison word. Paul, with a soldier attached to his
wrist, says peace will stand sentry.

## What comes just before

Two women. *I implore Euodia and I implore Syntyche to be of the same mind in the Lord.*

The *rejoice always* and the *let your gentleness be known* land immediately after a
falling-out between two named members of the congregation.

That is who *everyone* includes first.

## Where Paul learned it

*I have learned, in whatever state I am, **to be content**… I know how to be abased, and I
know how to abound.* See the note on Philippians 4:11–13.

The Greek for *learned* is **emathon** — he was taught it, over time, by going through both.

> **Where it was written from.** A rented house in Rome with a guard on the door. The
> instruction is *always*, and he says it twice in case the first one was missed.
