He is a guest at somebody's table, watching the other guests seat themselves.

## Where He is

*It happened, as He went into the house of one of the rulers of the Pharisees to eat bread on
the Sabbath, that they watched Him closely.*

He has just healed a man with dropsy in front of them, and asked the question nobody would
answer: *is it lawful to heal on the Sabbath?* See the note on Luke 14:1–6.

Now He is at the meal, and the watching goes the other way.

## What He noticed

*He had been **watching the guests choose their seats**, each one **edging toward the places of
honor**.*

The Greek is **epechon** — holding his attention on.

At a formal meal, guests reclined on couches and the positions were graded. Everybody in the
room could read the seating.

## The parable

*"When you are invited to a wedding feast, **do not go and sit down in the best seat**."*

## The risk

*"Someone **more important than you may have been invited too**, and then the host will have to
come over and say, '**Would you give this man your place?**'"*

The host has to intervene, in public, and correct the seating.

## The humiliation

*"And you will **get up in front of everyone and make the long walk to the lowest seat left in
the room**."*

The Greek is **eschaton topon** — the last place.

Not just demoted. Demoted to whatever is left, which is the bottom, because everybody else has
sat down.

## The alternative

*"When you are invited, **go and take the lowest place**."*

## The upgrade

*"Then your host will come and find you there and say, '**Friend, come up higher — this seat is
for you**.'"*

The Greek is **phile** — friend.

*"And **in front of all the other guests you will be honored**."*

The same audience, both ways. The passage is honest that the public dimension is what the
seating is about.

## Why this is not a technique

Read as advice, it is a strategy — take the low seat in order to be moved up.

The last verse rules that out by generalising it beyond dinner.

## The principle

*"For **everyone who puts himself forward will be brought low**, and **the one who takes the low
place will be lifted up**."*

Both verbs are passive. Somebody else does the raising and the lowering.

Jesus says this three times in Luke — here, at the end of the Pharisee and tax collector, and in
Matthew's woes.

## What He says immediately after

To the host: *"When you give a dinner or a supper, **do not ask your friends, your brothers,
your relatives, nor rich neighbours**, lest they also invite you back and you be repaid."*

*"But when you give a feast, **invite the poor, the maimed, the lame, the blind**. And you will
be blessed, **because they cannot repay you**."*

The seating advice is followed by guest-list advice, and both are about the economy of return.

## What follows that

Somebody at the table says *blessed is he who shall eat bread in the kingdom of God*, and Jesus
tells the parable of the great supper — the invitations, the excuses, and the streets and
lanes. See the note on Luke 14:15–20.

The whole chapter is one dinner party, and every teaching in it comes out of the room He is
sitting in.

## Where the descent goes

*Who, being in the form of God… **made Himself of no reputation**… and became obedient to the
point of death.*

*Therefore God also has highly exalted Him.* See the note on
Philippians 2:9–11.

Same shape, and the same two passives.

> **What the last verse does.** It stops the parable being a seating tactic. Both verbs are
> passive — the lifting and the lowering are somebody else's to do.
