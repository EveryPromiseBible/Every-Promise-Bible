The psalm that opens the whole book is built on a progression most readers walk
straight past.

## Walk, stand, sit

*Won't walk in step with the wicked… won't stop and stand where sinners
loiter… won't settle into the seat of the scornful.*

Three postures, and they descend: moving alongside, then stopping, then sitting
down. The company gets worse as the posture gets more settled — wicked, then
sinners, then scoffers.

It is a description of how a thing that starts as company ends as identity, and
it is arranged so you can see the slope.

## The word for meditating

*Turning it over softly, day and night.* The Hebrew is **hagah** — the same word
in Joshua 1:8, and it is audible: a lion growling, a dove moaning, a person
muttering.

Set against the three verbs above, it is what fills the space they left. The
happy person is not defined by avoidance; something else has their attention.

## The tree

*Rooted beside flowing streams… offers its fruit right on time, whose leaves
never wither.*

Two details are easy to miss. The tree is **planted** — the Hebrew is passive,
transplanted by someone. And the fruit comes *in its season*, which is a real
qualification: not constantly, not on demand.

> **"Whatever that one does comes into bloom."** The line has been used to
> promise success in any undertaking, which the psalm does not support — it is a
> tree image, and trees do not prosper by trying. What the psalm claims is that a
> life rooted in the right place bears what it is meant to bear, on time, and
> does not dry out when the heat comes. Jeremiah 17 uses the same picture and
> spells that out: it does not fear when heat comes, and in drought it does not
> stop bearing.
