Paul concedes the whole argument to the strong and then tells them to give it up.

## What has been settled

*We know that **an idol is nothing in the world**, and that there is no other God but one.* See the
note on 1 Corinthians 8:4–6.

And *food does not commend us to God*.

The theology is agreed. The meat is meat.

## The turn

*Take care, though, that **this liberty of yours does not somehow become a stumbling block**.*

The Greek is **exousia** — right, authority. And **proskomma** — something you trip over.

The right is real. That is the premise, not the concession.

## The scene

*If someone sees you, who have this knowledge, **reclining at a meal in an idol's temple**.*

The Greek is **en eidōleiō katakeimenon**.

Temple dining rooms were the ordinary venue for guild dinners, weddings and civic functions. This
is social life, not worship.

## The effect

*Will not his conscience, **weak as it is, be emboldened** to eat what is offered to idols?*

The Greek is **oikodomēthēsetai** — will be built up.

Paul uses his own favourite word for edification, and uses it ironically. The man is built up into
doing something he has not settled.

## What the weakness is

Not scruple. In this chapter the weak man still half-believes the idol is something.

*Some, with consciousness of the idol, until now eat it as a thing offered to an idol; and their
conscience, being weak, is defiled.*

## The outcome

*And so **through your knowledge the weak brother perishes**.*

The Greek is **apollutai** — is destroyed, is ruined.

## The clause

*The brother **for whom Christ died**.*

The Greek is **di' hon Christos apethanen**.

Paul sets a dinner invitation against the cross and asks which one the man is worth.

## The charge

*When you sin against your brothers this way and **wound their weak conscience**, **you are sinning
against Christ**.*

The Greek is **tuptontes** — striking, beating.

## The escalation

Against the brother, therefore against Christ.

The same logic as *inasmuch as you did it to one of the least of these My brethren, you did it to
Me*. See the note on Matthew 25:34–40.

## The conclusion

*Therefore, **if food makes my brother stumble, I will never eat meat again**.*

The Greek is **ou mē phagō krea eis ton aiōna** — a double negative with *forever*. The strongest
denial the language has.

## What he does not do

He does not change his mind about the meat.

Chapter 10 restates the freedom: *eat whatever is sold in the meat market, asking no questions for
conscience' sake*.

The liberty stands. He declines to use it.

## Where the argument started

*Knowledge puffs up, but **love edifies**.* See the note on 1 Corinthians 8:1–3.

The Greek is **phusioi** — inflates, blows up. And **oikodomei** — builds.

Air against masonry, in the opening line of the chapter.

## What comes next

A whole chapter on rights Paul had and did not use — payment, marriage, support. See the note on
1 Corinthians 9:1–2.

*Nevertheless we have not used this right, but endure all things lest we hinder the gospel of
Christ.*

He argues the case for his rights at length, and then says he never took them.

## Where Romans says it

*It is good neither to eat meat nor drink wine nor do anything by which your brother stumbles.* See
the note on Romans 14:20–21.

*For the kingdom of God is not eating and drinking.*

> **The concession.** He grants the strong every point of their argument — and then tells them the
> brother they would win it against is one Christ died for.
