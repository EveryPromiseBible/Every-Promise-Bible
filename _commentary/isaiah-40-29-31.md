*They will soar on wings like eagles. They will run and not grow weary. They will
walk and not faint.* The list descends, and readers have wondered about that for
centuries.

## Why the sequence goes down

You would expect a crescendo: walk, then run, then fly. Isaiah puts them the
other way.

The likeliest reason is that it ends where life mostly is. Soaring is the
picture; running is the effort; walking is the long ordinary stretch — and *not
fainting* on that stretch is the hardest of the three. The promise is arranged to
finish on the least dramatic thing, because that is where it is needed longest.

## Who it is spoken to

*Even the young grow tired and weary, even strong young men stumble and fall.*

Isaiah names the strongest people available and says they run out. That is the
setup: the promise is not that some people are naturally tireless. It is that
strength comes from somewhere other than youth.

## The word for "wait"

The Hebrew **qavah** carries the sense of a cord being twisted — strands drawn
together under tension. From that comes waiting, expecting, hoping.

It is not passive. It is closer to being wound tight around something. And the
promise attached is not that strength returns but that it is **exchanged** — the
verb means to change one thing for another, as you would change clothes.

> **Where the chapter starts.** *Comfort, comfort my people.* This is the opening
> of the section of Isaiah written to exiles, and the whole chapter builds toward
> these three verses: God measures the oceans in His hand, weighs mountains on
> scales, brings princes to nothing — and then gives strength to the one with
> nothing left. The size of Him is the argument for the promise.
