Paul takes Elijah's seven thousand and turns them into a definition.

## The parallel

*In the same way, **at this present time also**, there is a remnant.*

Elijah's seven thousand were a live example, not an illustration. Paul says the same
thing is running now. See the note on Romans 11:1–4.

## The word

*A **remnant**.*

The Greek is **leimma** — what is left over. The Hebrew equivalent runs through Isaiah:
*a remnant shall return*, which Isaiah made his son's name.

Paul's own point is that a remnant is not a failure. It is what survives, and it is
always there.

## Who chose it

*Chosen **by grace**.*

The Greek is *according to the election of grace*.

Not a remnant who chose better. A remnant selected, and the selecting agent is named.

## The either/or

*And **if it is by grace, then it is no longer on the basis of works**; otherwise **grace
would not be grace at all**.*

*But **if it is on the basis of works, then it is no longer grace**; otherwise **work would
not be work**.*

Paul states it in both directions, symmetrically, in two clauses.

## What he is arguing

Not that works are bad. That the two words mean incompatible things.

**Charis** is a gift; **ergon** is a wage. A gift that has to be earned is a wage under
another name, and a wage that is given free is not a wage.

Mixing them destroys both words, and Paul says so about each in turn.

He puts it the same way in Romans 4: *now to him who works, the wages are not counted as
grace but as debt.* See the note on Romans 4:6–8.

## What Elijah did not know

He said *I am the only one left*, twice.

Seven thousand had not bowed. He had no way of knowing and had not been told until he
asked.

Paul's point is that the reserving was going on invisibly, and that this is what grace
does.

## Where the chapter goes

The olive tree — branches broken off, a wild branch grafted in, and the warning: *do not
boast against the branches… **you do not support the root, but the root supports you**.*

And: *for God has committed them all to disobedience, that **He might have mercy on
all**.*

## How it ends

*Oh, the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable
are His judgments and His ways past finding out!*

*For of Him and through Him and to Him are all things, to whom be glory forever. Amen.*

Three chapters on the hardest question in the letter, and it ends in a doxology rather
than a conclusion.

> **The test in the sentence.** Whether the word still means what it means. Paul does not
> argue that grace is better — he argues that if anything is added, there is no longer
> anything to call grace.
