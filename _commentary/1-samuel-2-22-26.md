Eli confronts his sons, and the chapter ends by cutting to a boy.

## The man

*Eli was **very old** now. And he **kept hearing** about everything his sons were doing.*

The Hebrew is *he heard* — repeatedly.

He is the high priest and the judge of Israel. He knows.

## What he heard

*Everything his sons were doing to **all Israel**, and how they **slept with the women who
served at the entrance to the tent of meeting**.*

The fork in the sacrificial pot was chapter 2's first charge. See the note on
1 Samuel 2:12–17.

This is the second, and it is inside the tabernacle precinct.

## The confrontation

*"**Why are you doing these things?** I hear from all these people about your evil ways."*

He asks a question and cites public report.

## The reproach

*"**No, my sons.** This is **no good report** I'm hearing — **you're leading the LORD's people
into sin**."*

The Hebrew is *it is no good report that I hear*.

He names the real damage: not what they take, but what it does to everybody else.

## The argument

*"If a man sins against another man, **God may mediate for him**. But **if a man sins against
the LORD, who can plead his case?**"*

The Hebrew is *the judge shall judge him*, and then: who shall intercede?

It is a genuine theological question, and the Old Testament never answers it — Job asks
the same thing: *there is no umpire between us, no one to lay his hand on us both.* See the
note on Job 9:25–35.

The answer is centuries away: *there is **one Mediator** between God and men, the Man Christ
Jesus*.

## What Eli did not do

Anything. God's charge against him comes two chapters on, through the boy Samuel:

*I will judge his house forever for the iniquity which he knows, because **his sons made
themselves vile, and he did not restrain them**.*

And through the man of God in this chapter: *why do you **honour your sons more than Me**,
by making yourselves fat with the best of all the offerings?*

**Making yourselves** — plural. Eli was eating it too.

He talked and did not act, and he ate.

## The response

*But his sons **would not listen to their father**.*

And then the hardest clause in the chapter: *for **it was the LORD's will to put them to
death**.*

The Hebrew is *because the LORD desired to kill them*.

The narrator states it and does not explain it. Scripture does this occasionally — Pharaoh's
hardened heart, the lying spirit in Ahab's prophets — and offers no commentary.

## The cut

*And **the boy Samuel kept growing, both in size and in favour — with the LORD and with
people**.*

The chapter alternates between the two households throughout, and this is the last line of
it.

Luke uses almost the same sentence twice: of John, and of Jesus at twelve — *and Jesus
increased in wisdom and stature, and in favour with God and men*. See the note on
Luke 2:51–52.

## What happens next

The famous line opens chapter 3: *and the word of the LORD was rare in those days; there was
no widespread revelation.*

Then a voice in the night, three times, and Eli — who could not restrain his own sons —
telling the boy what to say: *speak, LORD, for Your servant hears.*

The old man gives the right instruction to somebody else's child.

> **What the warning lacked.** Not clarity. Eli says exactly what is wrong and asks a
> question nobody in the Old Testament can answer — and the chapter's charge against him,
> delivered later, is that he did not restrain them.
