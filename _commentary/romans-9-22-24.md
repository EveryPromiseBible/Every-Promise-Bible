The hardest paragraph in the letter, and the sentence it is heading for is about kindness.

## The construction

*"**And what if God… carried with great patience the vessels of wrath?**"*

**Paul** frames the whole thing as a question — *ei de* — and never completes it into a flat
assertion. The paragraph is a hypothetical the reader is invited to sit with.

## The verb

*"**Carried with great patience.**"*

**Enegken en polle makrothymia** — bore with much longsuffering.

What God is described doing with the vessels of wrath is putting up with them, at length.

*Or do you despise the riches of His goodness, forbearance and **longsuffering**, not knowing that
the goodness of God leads you to repentance?*

*The Lord is not slack concerning His promise… but is **longsuffering toward us, not willing that any
should perish**.*

## The two participles

The vessels of wrath are **katertismena eis apoleian** — fitted for destruction. A perfect passive
participle with no agent named, and **Paul** does not supply one.

The vessels of mercy are **ha proetoimasen eis doxan** — which **He** prepared beforehand for glory.
Active, and the subject is God.

The asymmetry is deliberate and it is in the grammar. **Paul** attributes the preparing for glory to
God and declines to attribute the fitting for destruction to anybody.

## What the whole thing is for

*"**That He might make known the riches of His glory on the vessels of mercy.**"*

**Ploutos tes doxes autou** — the riches of His glory. That is the purpose clause of the paragraph,
and the exhibit is the vessels of mercy.

*That in the ages to come He might show **the exceeding riches of His grace** in His kindness toward
us in **Christ Jesus**.*

## Who they are

*"**Even us whom He called, not of the Jews only, but also of the Gentiles.**"*

The paragraph ends by identifying the vessels of mercy as the readers, and the next verse quotes
Hosea. See the note on Romans 9:25–26.

## And the potter

*Does not the potter have power over the clay?*

*So the potter **gathered up the same clay** and shaped it into another vessel.* See the note on
Jeremiah 18:1–4.

Jeremiah's potter does not fetch new material. **Paul** knows the passage; it is where his image
comes from.

*But **we have this treasure in earthen vessels**, that the excellence of the power may be of God and
not of us.*

> **The asymmetry in the grammar.** *Fitted for destruction* is a passive with no agent. *Which He
> prepared beforehand for glory* is active, and God is the subject. Paul writes the two halves
> differently, and the purpose clause of the whole paragraph is the riches of His glory on the
> vessels of mercy.
