A young man arrives at David's camp with Saul's crown in his hands and a story
he has clearly rehearsed.

## Who he says he is

*An Amalekite.*

That is the detail the chapter is built on. Saul had been commanded to destroy
the Amalekites and had spared what he chose to spare. Now, at the end of his
life, it is an Amalekite standing over him — and an Amalekite who arrives to
profit from his death.

The thing kept back came back.

## The story he tells

He says Saul was leaning on his spear, called him over, and asked to be finished
off, and that he did it and took the crown and the armlet.

The account does not match what the previous chapter recorded, where Saul fell on
his own sword after his armour-bearer refused. Most readers conclude the
Amalekite found the body and invented a role for himself, expecting a reward from
the man Saul had hunted.

## What he misjudged

He read David the way the world reads: an enemy is dead, the throne is open, and
the man who brings news of it will be paid.

David's response is to tear his clothes, mourn until evening, and then execute
him for laying a hand on the LORD's anointed. Then he writes a lament for the man
who had spent years trying to kill him.

> **The pattern the chapter draws.** What Saul was told to deal with and did not,
> stood over him at the end and took the crown off his head. The narrative makes
> no comment. It simply gives the man's nationality in the fifth verse and lets
> the reader remember chapter 15.

## What Amalek was

Deuteronomy describes the nation by its method: it came at Israel's back and cut
down the stragglers *when you were weary and worn out*. It hunts what is already
tired.

Saul was told to blot it out. He kept the king and the best of the livestock —
what looked useful — and destroyed the rest.

What is spared because it still seems worth something does not stay where it was
put. It stands over you at the end and takes the crown off your head, and four
hundred years later it signs a decree in Persia with a king's ring on its hand.
See the notes on Deuteronomy 25:17–19 and Esther 3:1–4.
