**Luke** quotes Isaiah further than the other gospels do, and the extra line is the point.

## The baptism

*"**A baptism that went with a whole new way of thinking, so that their sins could be carried away
and forgiven.**"*

**Baptisma metanoias eis aphesin hamartion** — a baptism of repentance for the release of sins.

**Metanoia** — a change of mind. Not remorse, and not reparation; the Greek is about what somebody
thinks.

**Aphesis** — release, discharge, the cancelling of a debt. It is the word used of the Jubilee.

## The road

*"**Every valley shall be filled and every mountain and hill brought low; the crooked places shall
be made straight and the rough ways smooth.**"*

Isaiah 40, and the image is an engineering corps going ahead of a king: filling, levelling,
straightening. Nobody in the picture is asked to climb.

*"**Comfort, comfort My people**," says your God.*

That is how the chapter **John** is quoting opens.

## The line Luke adds

*"**And all flesh shall see the salvation of God.**"*

**Matthew** and **Mark** stop before it. **Luke** carries the quotation two clauses further to reach
**pasa sarx** — all flesh.

It is the same evangelist who puts Simeon's *a light to bring revelation to the Gentiles* in chapter
2, the Gentile cities in the crowd in chapter 6, and the gospel in Rome at the end of **Acts**.

*"**And I, if I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all peoples to Myself.**"*

## What the road was for

*"**Behold! The Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world!**"*

**John**'s own summary of his job, in the fourth gospel. The forerunner levels a road and then points
at somebody walking down it.

> **The clause Luke will not leave off.** *All flesh shall see the salvation of God.* Matthew and
> Mark stop one line short of it; Luke, writing to a Gentile, does not.
