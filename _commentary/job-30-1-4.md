The chapter turns on two words, and then Job says something he would not have said
in chapter 1.

## "But now"

Chapter 29 was *oh, that I were as in months past*. Chapter 30 begins *but now*.

The two chapters are a hinge — everything he was, and everything he is.

## What he says about them

*"Those younger than I make sport of me, men whose fathers **I would not have
trusted even with my sheepdogs**."*

That is the line. He is not describing what they did to him; he is placing them.

Their fathers were beneath the dogs that watched his flock.

## Who they actually are

Read the next three verses, because Job describes them himself:

*"Gaunt from want and hunger, they gnawed the dry ground… They picked salt herbs
among the bushes, and the roots of the broom tree were their bread."*

Starving people. Eating roots and weeds in waste ground.

The man who was *eyes to the blind and feet to the lame*, *a father to the needy*,
is looking at the destitute and measuring them against his sheepdogs.

## What has been done to him

This is chapter 30 of a book in which three men have spent twenty-five chapters
telling him his ruin is his own fault.

A man forced to defend himself for that long ends up with a case, and the case
needs someone to be beneath him.

The bitterness in chapter 30 and the self-justification in chapter 29 are the same
thing looked at from two sides, and both of them arrived after his comforters
started talking. He did not sound like this in chapter 1 or 2.

## The honesty of the book

Nothing here is tidied up. The writer records the greatest man in the East
sneering at starving men and does not annotate it.

Job has to hear from God before this comes off him.

> **Where the whole speech is going.** By the end of the chapter: *I cry out to
> You, and You do not answer me… I am a brother of jackals and a companion of
> ostriches… my harp is turned to mourning.* He is not enjoying the contempt he is
> handing out. He is a man in pieces, and the pieces cut in every direction.
