Two images in one verse, and both come from the same place in the law.

## The first handful of dough

*If the first handful of dough is holy, so is the whole lump.*

Numbers 15 required the first of the ground meal to be offered as a contribution.
Once that portion was given, the rest of the batch was reckoned holy — set apart,
usable.

Paul's logic runs from the part to the whole: what is true of the first is true of
what follows it.

## The root and the branches

*If the root is holy, so are the branches.*

Same argument, different picture — and this one he will spend the next ten verses
on: an olive tree, natural branches broken off, wild branches grafted in, and a
warning to the wild ones not to be arrogant about it.

Both images are making his point about Israel: the patriarchs are the root, and
what happened at the root is not undone by what happened to some branches.

## What the passage is actually about

The subject is not personal blessing. It is whether God has finished with Israel,
and Paul's answer is a flat no — *their being received back* would be *life from
the dead*.

He also says something disarming about his own motives: he makes much of his work
among Gentiles partly hoping to provoke his own people to jealousy and save some.
An apostle admitting to an ulterior motive is not something the New Testament
does often.

> **On lifting the first line out.** *If the first handful is holy, so is the
> whole lump* is sometimes applied to giving — set apart the first portion of
> income and the rest is blessed. The image comes from an offering, so the
> association is understandable, but Paul's own use of it here is about Israel
> and the patriarchs, and that is what the sentence is arguing.
