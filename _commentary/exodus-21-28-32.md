A law about a dangerous animal, and the last figure in it turns up again at a betrayal.

## The first case

*If an ox gores a man or woman to death, **the ox is to be stoned** and its meat not
eaten, and **the owner is cleared**.*

The animal is executed and the owner goes free. Nobody could have foreseen it.

The meat is not eaten — the animal is not to be salvaged as property.

## The second case

*But **if the ox had a habit of goring** and its owner **had been warned yet did not keep
it penned**.*

Two conditions: a known pattern, and a warning already given.

*Then **the ox is stoned and its owner too is to be put to death**.*

The negligence is treated as capital.

## The ransom

*If **a ransom is set for him**, he may **pay whatever is required to redeem his life**.*

The Hebrew is **kopher** — a ransom, a covering price. It shares a root with **kaphar**,
to atone, and with **kapporeth**, the mercy seat.

A life is forfeit and a payment is accepted instead. That is the only place in the law
where a capital sentence can be commuted for money.

## The equality clause

*The same ruling holds whether the ox gores **a son or a daughter**.*

## The exception

*But if the ox gores **a manservant or a maidservant**, the owner pays **thirty shekels of
silver** to the servant's master, and the ox is stoned.*

A slave was somebody's property, so the compensation goes to the owner and the figure is
fixed.

Thirty shekels is the valuation of a slave in this law.

## Where the figure turns up

Zechariah 11, in the strange passage where the prophet acts out a rejected shepherd and
asks for his wages:

*So they weighed out for my wages **thirty pieces of silver**. And the LORD said to me,
"**Throw it to the potter**" — that princely price they set on me. So I took the thirty
pieces of silver and **threw them into the house of the LORD for the potter**.*

The tone is contempt. It is what you pay for a slave.

## Where it turns up again

*Then one of the twelve, called Judas Iscariot, went to the chief priests and said, "**What
are you willing to give me if I deliver Him to you?**" And they counted out to him **thirty
pieces of silver**.*

And afterward: *he threw down the pieces of silver in the temple and departed.*

The chief priests would not put it in the treasury *because it is the price of blood*, and
bought **the potter's field** with it.

Matthew quotes Zechariah's line and says it was fulfilled.

## What the price says

Nobody in the room negotiated. The chief priests named the sum, and the sum was the
statutory compensation for a dead slave.

## What Philippians says about the form

*Who, being in the form of God… **made Himself of no reputation, taking the form of a
bondservant**.*

The Greek is **doulos** — a slave.

> **The figure in the law.** Thirty shekels, for a servant killed by somebody else's
> animal, paid to the owner. It is the only price in the Torah for a human life, and it is
> the one that was counted out in the temple.
