The argument of the whole letter in two sentences, and it runs from the lesser to the greater.

## The invitation

*Think it through.*

The Greek is **ei gar** — for if. Hebrews sets up a conditional and lets the reader finish it.

## The granted premise

*The blood of bulls and goats, and **the ashes of a burned young cow** sprinkled over people who had
been made unclean, **set them apart and got them clean on the outside**.*

Hebrews concedes that the old system worked.

It did what it was designed to do. The writer does not dismiss it.

## The three sacrifices named

**Bulls and goats** — the Day of Atonement, when the high priest went in with blood once a year.

**The ashes of a heifer** — the red heifer of Numbers 19, burned outside the camp, its ashes kept
and mixed with running water for anyone defiled by a corpse. See the note on
Numbers 19:1–10.

## What they achieved

*Set them apart and got them clean **on the outside**.*

The Greek is **pros ten tes sarkos katharoteta** — to the purity of the flesh.

Ceremonial standing. A man could re-enter the camp and go to the tabernacle.

The letter has already said what it could not do: *sacrifices which cannot make him who performed
the service perfect **in regard to the conscience***.

## The comparison

*Then **how much more** will the blood of Christ do.*

The Greek is **poso mallon**.

The rabbinic argument from lesser to greater. Hebrews uses it repeatedly, and Paul uses it in
Romans 5.

## The three clauses about Him

*Who through **the eternal Spirit** offered **Himself** to God with **no flaw in Him anywhere**.*

The Greek is **dia pneumatos aioniou** — through eternal Spirit.

**Heauton prosenenken** — offered Himself. Priest and offering in one person.

**Amomon** — without blemish. The technical word for a sacrificial animal that passed inspection.

## What the animals lacked

Willingness.

Every animal was brought. This one is described as offering itself.

## The effect

*To **scour your conscience clean of all that dead work**.*

The Greek is **kathariei ten suneidesin humon apo nekron ergon** — will cleanse your conscience
from dead works.

## The word

**Suneidesis** — conscience. What the old system could not reach.

*For then would they not have ceased to be offered? For the worshippers, once purified, would have
had **no more consciousness of sins**.* See the note on Hebrews 10:1–2.

## Dead works

**Nekron ergon** — works of a corpse.

The phrase appears twice in Hebrews, and both times among the elementary things.

Under Numbers 19, contact with a corpse was the thing the ashes were for. The letter takes the
category and applies it to religious effort.

## The purpose

*So that **you can serve the living God**.*

The Greek is **latreuein Theo zonti** — to serve as a priest serves.

Dead works, and the living God. The contrast is deliberate.

## What the cleansing is for

Not comfort. Service.

The conscience is scoured so the person can approach and work, which is what a defiled priest
could not do.

## What follows

*And for this reason **He is the Mediator of the new covenant**… that those who are called may
receive the promise of the eternal inheritance.*

*And **without shedding of blood there is no remission**.* See the note on
Hebrews 9:21–22.

## Where the chapter ends

*So Christ was offered once to bear the sins of many. To those who eagerly wait for Him **He will
appear a second time, apart from sin, for salvation**.*

> **The premise granted.** Hebrews does not say the old system failed. It says it cleansed the
> outside, and asks what the greater blood does — and the answer is the conscience.
