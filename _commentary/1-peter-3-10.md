Peter is quoting a psalm, and he changes almost nothing.

## Where it comes from

Psalm 34: *who is the man who desires life, and loves many days, that he may see good?
Keep your tongue from evil, and your lips from speaking deceit.*

Peter lifts four verses of it into the middle of a letter about suffering.

## The psalm's own setting

The title says David wrote it *when he pretended madness before Abimelech, who drove him
away, and he departed.*

He was on the run from Saul, had fled to enemy territory, was recognised, and escaped by
scratching at the doors and letting spit run down his beard.

Then he wrote a psalm about wanting good days.

## The wanting

*Anyone who **wants to enjoy life and see good days**.*

The verse begins with a desire, not a duty. It is addressed to whoever wants this — and
takes the wanting as reasonable.

## Days, plural

Trouble in this psalm is singular and days are plural. The psalm says *many are the
afflictions of the righteous, but the LORD delivers him out of them all* — and the
delivering is what the whole psalm is about.

## What is asked

*Shut your mouth on the rotten word, and **do not let a lie past your lips**.*

Two instructions and both are about the mouth. Nothing about circumstances, nothing
about effort.

The Hebrew of the psalm is *keep your tongue from evil* — **natsar**, to guard, as a
sentry guards a gate.

## What follows in Peter

*Let him **turn away from evil and do good**; let him **seek peace and pursue it**. For
the eyes of the LORD are on the righteous, and His ears are open to their prayers.*

Turn, do, seek, pursue. And then the reason: He is watching and listening.

## Why Peter needed it

The letter is written to *the pilgrims of the Dispersion* — believers scattered across
five Roman provinces, and the letter is full of *fiery trial*, *suffering wrongfully*,
and *when you do good and suffer for it*.

Into that Peter puts a quotation about wanting good days, and does not qualify it.

## What is around it in the chapter

Immediately before: *not returning evil for evil or reviling for reviling, but on the
contrary **blessing**, knowing that you were called to this, **that you may inherit a
blessing**.*

The mouth again, and the blessing named as the inheritance.

## The rest of the psalm

*Oh, taste and see that the LORD is good; blessed is the man who trusts in Him. The
young lions lack and suffer hunger, but those who seek the LORD shall not lack any good
thing.*

## Peter's own mouth

The man quoting *do not let a lie past your lips* had denied three times with an oath
in a courtyard, and had been restored by a charcoal fire on a beach. See the note on
Luke 22:31–34.

## Where else this psalm turns up

Peter quotes Psalm 34 for daily conduct. John quotes the same psalm for something else entirely.

*For these things were done that the Scripture should be fulfilled, "**Not one of His bones shall be
broken**."*

That is Psalm 34:20, and John reaches for it standing at the cross, watching soldiers decide not to
break the legs of a Man they thought was already dead.

So the psalm Peter uses to teach people how to speak on an ordinary Tuesday is the psalm that
describes the body of Jesus Christ under Roman hands. And its most famous line — *O taste and see
that the LORD is good* — is the one Peter has already quoted earlier in this letter, applying it to
Him: *if indeed you have tasted that the Lord is gracious.*

The good days are not being sold to you on terms. They come from the same place the psalm's own hope
came from.

> **What the verse offers.** Not survival, not endurance, not compensation later. Life
> enjoyed and good days seen — and the condition attached is about what a man says.
