Some of the loveliest promises in the Old Testament are in this speech, and the
speaker is one of Job's three friends.

## Who is talking

Eliphaz the Temanite, in his third and harshest speech. A few verses earlier he
has accused Job of stripping the clothes off the poor and sending widows away
empty — charges the book has already told the reader are false.

So the passage is a true thing said by a man who is wrong about the person he is
saying it to.

## What he says

*Make peace with Him, Job, and be at rest. Then good things will find their way
back to you… You will pray, and He will hear you… Whatever you decide will hold
firm, and light will shine on every path you take.*

Taken as statements about God, these are sound and the rest of Scripture backs
them. Taken as Eliphaz means them — repent of the sins we have decided you
committed and your fortunes will return — they are a cruelty, and God says so at
the end of the book.

## How the book settles it

God's verdict on the three friends is unusually blunt: *you have not spoken of
Me what is right, as My servant Job has.* They are told to bring sacrifices and
have Job pray for them.

Which makes the last line here almost unbearable in hindsight. Eliphaz tells Job
that God *will rescue even the guilty, and they will be delivered through the
cleanness of your hands* — and that is precisely what happens, with Eliphaz as the
guilty party and Job praying for him.

## True sentences from the wrong mouth

*"Now acquaint yourself with Him, and be at peace; thereby good will come to you… **you will
also declare a thing, and it will be established for you**; so light will shine on your ways."*

Every line of it is quotable and most of it is true. It is also Eliphaz, mid-argument, telling
a blameless man to repent of sins he has invented for him — *is not your wickedness great, and
your iniquity without end?*

## How Scripture handles this

It records the speech and then rules on the speaker.

*"**You have not spoken of Me what is right**, as My servant Job has."* See the note on
Job 42:7–8.

The words are in the Bible. The verdict on the man saying them is in the Bible too, four
chapters later.

## The one line that survives the verdict

*"**He will even deliver one who is not innocent**; yes, he will be delivered by the purity of
your hands."*

Eliphaz says it as flattery about intercession, and the book then does it to him — literally.
He is the one who is not innocent, and he is delivered when Job prays for him. See the note on
Job 42:10.

*And **the LORD accepted Job**.*

## Where the deliverance of the guilty is settled

*For **when we were still without strength**, in due time Christ died for the ungodly.*

*But God demonstrates His own love toward us, in that **while we were still sinners**, Christ
died for us.*

Eliphaz was closer to the gospel than he knew, and on the wrong side of it.

> **How to use a passage like this.** The sentences are true of God; that is why
> they read so well on their own. What the book adds is the warning that true
> statements can be aimed at a suffering person as a weapon. Job's friends were
> at their best in the seven days they sat with him and said nothing.
