The passage answers a question that is put in the middle of it, and the question
is a good one.

## The objection, raised inside the text

*Can plunder be taken from a warrior? Can captives be rescued from the grip of
the strong?*

The expected answer is no. That is the point of asking — it is the objection of
someone looking at a situation and concluding that whatever has hold of it is
stronger than anything likely to intervene.

Isaiah puts the doubt in the text rather than talking past it.

## The answer

*Yes, even the captives of the warrior will be taken back… I Myself will fight
those who fight you, and I Myself will save your children.*

The emphatic pronoun appears twice. Not *your children will be saved* — **I
Myself**. The rescue is claimed personally, by name, against a stronger captor.

## The imagery at the end

*I will make your oppressors eat their own flesh. They will be drunk on their own
blood as if it were sweet wine.*

It is violent, and it should not be trimmed. Isaiah is writing about empires that
took children into captivity, and the language of judgement in the prophets is
deliberately unbearable — it is meant to sound like what it describes.

## Who answered the objection

*Can plunder be taken from a warrior? Can captives be rescued from the grip of the strong?*

The expected answer is no.

*"Or **how can one enter a strong man's house and plunder his goods, unless he first binds the
strong man**? And then he will plunder his house."*

**Matthew** 12, and **Jesus** takes up Isaiah's rhetorical question and answers it with the same
picture. He has just been accused of casting out demons by Beelzebub, and His reply is that
somebody has been into the house.

*"But when a **stronger than he** comes upon him and overcomes him, he takes from him all his
armour in which he trusted, and divides his spoils."*

**Luke**'s comparative is **ischyroteros** — a stronger one. Isaiah asks whether anything is
stronger than the strong; the gospels name Him.

## I Myself will save your children

The emphatic pronoun, twice.

*"When He ascended on high, **He led captivity captive**, and gave gifts to men."*

**Ephesians** 4, quoting Psalm 68 — captives taken back out of the hand that held them, in a
procession going up.

*Inasmuch then as the children have partaken of flesh and blood, He Himself likewise shared in
the same, that through death He might destroy him who had the power of death… **and release those
who through fear of death were all their lifetime subject to bondage**.*

## The complaint it answers

*Can a mother forget the child at her breast?… **Yet I will not forget you. See, I have inscribed
you on the palms of My hands.***

Written to people who had concluded God had forgotten them.

*Then He said to Thomas, "**Reach your finger here, and look at My hands**."*

> **The setting.** This whole chapter is addressed to people who had concluded
> God had forgotten them; a few verses earlier is the famous *can a mother forget
> the child at her breast?* The promise about children is the answer to a
> complaint about abandonment, which is why it is stated in the first person
> twice over.
