The passage answers a question that is put in the middle of it, and the question
is a good one.

## The objection, raised inside the text

*Can plunder be taken from a warrior? Can captives be rescued from the grip of
the strong?*

The expected answer is no. That is the point of asking — it is the objection of
someone looking at a situation and concluding that whatever has hold of it is
stronger than anything likely to intervene.

Isaiah puts the doubt in the text rather than talking past it.

## The answer

*Yes, even the captives of the warrior will be taken back… I Myself will fight
those who fight you, and I Myself will save your children.*

The emphatic pronoun appears twice. Not *your children will be saved* — **I
Myself**. The rescue is claimed personally, by name, against a stronger captor.

## The imagery at the end

*I will make your oppressors eat their own flesh. They will be drunk on their own
blood as if it were sweet wine.*

It is violent, and it should not be trimmed. Isaiah is writing about empires that
took children into captivity, and the language of judgement in the prophets is
deliberately unbearable — it is meant to sound like what it describes.

> **The setting.** This whole chapter is addressed to people who had concluded
> God had forgotten them; a few verses earlier is the famous *can a mother forget
> the child at her breast?* The promise about children is the answer to a
> complaint about abandonment, which is why it is stated in the first person
> twice over.
