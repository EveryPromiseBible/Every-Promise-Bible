She gets in without an invitation, and the host says nothing out loud.

## The invitation

*One of the Pharisees invited Jesus to eat with him, so **He went** to the man's house
and took His place at the table.*

He accepts. Luke's Gospel has Him at three different Pharisees' tables, and He goes every
time.

## How they were sitting

*Took His place at the table.*

At a formal meal guests reclined on couches around a low table, propped on the left
elbow, feet pointing away.

That is why she can be at His feet without being under the table.

## Who she was

*A woman in that town who was **known for her sinful life**.*

Luke gives her no name. The town knew her; the Gospel does not say for what.

She is not Mary Magdalene, and she is not Mary of Bethany. Luke introduces both of them
elsewhere and does not connect them to this.

## How she got in

*When she **learned that Jesus was eating at the Pharisee's house**, she came.*

Formal meals in that world were semi-public — the door stood open and people came in to
listen from the edges of the room. Getting in was not the hard part.

Getting in as her was.

## What she brought

*An **alabaster jar of perfume**.*

She came carrying it, which means she had decided what she was going to do before she
left home.

The jar had to be broken to be used.

## What she does

*She **stood behind Him at His feet, weeping**, and her tears began to fall on His feet.
She **wiped them with her own hair**, and **kissed His feet over and over**, and **poured
the perfume on them**.*

The plan was the perfume. The tears were not planned — they start, and then she has to
improvise a way to deal with them.

A woman's hair was bound up in public. She unbinds it in a room full of reclining men.

**Kissed His feet over and over** — the Greek is intensive and continuous.

## What the host thinks

*He said **to himself**, "**If this man were really a prophet, He would know who is
touching Him, and what kind of woman she is**."*

He does not say it aloud. Luke records the interior sentence.

And the sentence is a test of prophecy: a real prophet would know.

## The irony Luke leaves standing

Jesus answers the thought. The next verse is *Simon, I have something to say to you* —
and the host had never opened his mouth.

The test was passed in the act of answering it.

## What Simon had not done

Water for the feet, a kiss of welcome, oil for the head. Three ordinary courtesies, and
he had skipped all three. Jesus lists them a few verses later. See the note on
Luke 7:44–50.

## The parable in between

Two debtors, five hundred denarii and fifty, both forgiven. *Which of them will love him
more?* See the note on Luke 7:40–43.

Simon answers it correctly and does not see himself in it.

> **What she does not do.** Ask for anything, say anything, or explain herself. Luke
> records no words from her in the whole account — only the weeping, the hair, the
> kissing, and the jar.
