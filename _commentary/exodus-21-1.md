One verse of heading, and it introduces the oldest legal code in Scripture.

## The sentence

*These are **the rulings God gave Moses to set before the people**.*

The Hebrew is **mishpatim** — judgments, decisions. The word comes from the root for
judging, and it means case law: rulings on situations.

The block that follows is called *the book of the covenant* in chapter 24, and it runs
from here to the end of chapter 23.

## Where it comes

Directly after the ten commandments and the people's request that God stop speaking to
them. See the note on Exodus 20:18–21.

The ten were spoken to the whole nation. These are given to Moses to set before them,
which is the arrangement the people asked for.

## "To set before them"

The Hebrew is *which you shall place before them*.

Not imposed from a distance. Laid out in front of them.

## What kind of law it is

Almost all of it is casuistic — *if a man does this, then that*. It reads like a
collection of decided cases rather than a list of principles.

The ten commandments are apodictic: *you shall not*. These are the working-out.

## What it covers

The first thing after this verse is the Hebrew servant who may go free in the seventh
year and chooses not to — the man whose ear is pierced at the doorpost. See the note on
Exodus 21:2–6.

Then: injuries, an ox that gores, a pit left uncovered, theft, fire in a field, borrowed
property, a garment taken in pledge, bribes, the sabbath year, the sabbath day, and three
annual feasts.

Oxen, pits, fires and coats.

## What that says about the code

The first case in it is a slave being offered his freedom, and the last chapter of it is
about resting the land so the poor can eat and resting the household so the animals and
the foreigner can breathe. See the notes on Exodus 23:10–11 and Exodus 23:12.

The bookends are both about people with no power.

## The word *plain*

The Illumination calls them *plain, fair guidelines for how they were to live justly with
one another*.

The horizontal direction is right — the ten commandments open toward God and this block
is almost entirely about the neighbour.

## What happens at the end of it

Chapter 24: Moses reads the book of the covenant in the hearing of the people, and they
say *all that the LORD has said we will do, and be obedient.*

Then blood is thrown on the people and on the altar: *this is the blood of the covenant
which the LORD has made with you.*

Hebrews cites that moment: *not even the first covenant was dedicated without blood.*

## What the ox-and-pit laws became

Jesus argued from them twice on sabbath days — *which one of you, having a donkey or an ox
that has fallen into a pit, will not immediately pull him out?* See the note on
Luke 14:1–6.

The case law about a pit in Exodus 21 is what He reaches for when the lawyers cannot
answer him.

> **What the heading says the code is for.** Not ceremony and not devotion. Rulings, set
> in front of the people, about how they were to deal with each other.
