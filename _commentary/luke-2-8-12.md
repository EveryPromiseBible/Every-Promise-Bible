The announcement of the birth goes to the people who were working the night
shift.

## Who they were

Shepherds were not romantic figures. The work was dirty, the hours were
antisocial, and their testimony was not accepted in a Jewish court — a shepherd
could not have served as a witness.

Luke, who has just named Caesar Augustus and Quirinius governor of Syria in the
opening verses, brings the news to men in a field.

## "For you"

*Today, in the town of David, a Saviour has been born **for you**.*

The angel does not say a Saviour has been born, and you should be pleased about
it. The birth is announced as being on their account.

And the titles stack: Saviour, Messiah, Lord — three of the heaviest words
available, said to men holding sticks.

## The sign

*This is how you will know Him: you will find a baby wrapped in strips of cloth
and lying in a feeding trough.*

A strange sign. Every baby in Judea was wrapped in cloth; only one was in a
manger.

So the identifying mark is ordinariness in an impossible place. Nothing about the
sign suggests grandeur, and the shepherds are told to look for it in a town, not
a palace.

## "A great joy meant for everyone"

The Greek is *all the people* — and Luke, who will end his Gospel with the good
news going to all nations, starts widening it here in the second chapter.

> **What the glory did.** *The glory of the Lord blazed all around them, and they
> were terrified.* Then the first words: *do not be afraid.* Every angelic
> announcement in these two chapters begins the same way, because the reaction is
> always the same.
