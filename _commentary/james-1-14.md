Having ruled out one explanation, James gives the real one in a single sentence.

## The opening

*Here is what actually happens.*

The Greek is *but each one is tempted…* — and the **each one** matters. It is not a
description of humanity in general but of one person at a time.

The previous verse removed God from the account. See the note on James 1:13.

## Whose it is

*Your **own** craving.*

The Greek is **epithumia** — desire, longing, appetite. The word is not automatically
negative; Jesus uses it of Himself: *with fervent desire I have desired to eat this
Passover with you.*

What makes it lethal here is the possessive. **His own.**

## The two verbs

*Hauls you off and **baits you in**.*

Both are hunting and fishing words, and James chose them as a pair:

- **Exelko** — to drag out, to draw away. It is what you do to a fish, pulling it out of
  cover.
- **Deleazo** — from **delear**, bait. To lure with something on a hook.

The order is deliberate. First drawn out of position, then baited.

## What is not in the sentence

No devil, and no God. James is at his most stripped-down here — he mentions the devil
elsewhere in the letter (*resist the devil and he will flee from you*), but not in this
diagnosis.

The mechanism he describes needs no external agent at all.

## What follows

*Then, when desire has conceived, **it gives birth to sin**; and sin, when it is
full-grown, **brings forth death**.*

He switches metaphors mid-paragraph, from fishing to childbirth, and lays out a
sequence: desire, conception, sin, maturity, death.

Nothing in the chain is instantaneous. Every stage takes time, and each one is the
parent of the next.

## The point of describing it

James is not writing a psychology. He is writing so that the thing can be recognised at
the first stage rather than the last.

## The verse after the sequence

*Do not be deceived, my beloved brethren.*

And then, immediately, the good gifts and the Father of lights.

He puts the two accounts side by side: what comes up out of a man, and what comes down
from above.

## The word he uses again

**Epithumia** turns up in the letter's fourth chapter: *you lust and do not have… you ask
and do not receive, because you ask amiss, that you may spend it on your pleasures.*

Same root. James's diagnosis is consistent from one end of the letter to the other.

> **What is honest about it.** The bait works because it is wanted. James does not
> pretend otherwise — he says the pull comes from the person's own appetite, and calls
> it theirs.
