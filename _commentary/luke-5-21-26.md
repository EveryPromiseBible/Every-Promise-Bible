He answers an objection nobody voiced, and settles it with something they can check.

## What has just happened

A house so full that nobody can get in, and four men who take the roof off.

*And when they could not find how they might bring him in because of the crowd, they went up on
the housetop and let him down with his bed through the tiling into the midst before Jesus.*

*When He saw their faith, He said to him, "Man, your sins are forgiven you."*

Luke notes whose faith: *their* faith. The men on the roof.

And notes who was in the room: *Pharisees and teachers of the law were sitting by, who had come
out of every town of Galilee, Judea, and Jerusalem*.

## The objection

*"**Who does this man think he is, talking that way? Only God can forgive sins**."*

The Greek is **blasphemias** — blasphemies.

They are right about the premise. Only God can forgive sins, and everybody in the room knows
it.

That is the argument, and it is a good one. The conclusion is the only part in dispute.

## The reading

*Jesus **knew exactly what they were thinking**.*

The Greek is **epignous tous dialogismous** — knowing their reasonings.

They had not said it aloud. Luke says they *began turning it over among themselves*.

## The question

*"**Which is easier to say** — 'Your sins are forgiven,' or 'Get up and walk'?"*

The Greek is **eukopoteron** — easier, requiring less labour.

Easier to *say*. That is the word.

Forgiveness is unverifiable. Anybody can announce it and nobody in the room can check.

Telling a paralysed man to walk is verifiable within seconds, and if nothing happens the
speaker is finished.

He offers the falsifiable claim as evidence for the unfalsifiable one.

## The stated purpose

*"**But so that you will know the Son of Man has authority here on earth to forgive sins**."*

The Greek is **exousian** — authority, the right to act.

The sentence breaks off mid-clause. Luke marks the turn: *and here He turned to the paralyzed
man*.

The healing is inserted into the middle of a sentence addressed to the scribes.

## The command

*"**Get up, pick up your mat, and go home**."*

Three imperatives, and the third is the one that sends him out through the crowd carrying the
evidence.

## The result

*And **immediately, in front of them all**, the man stood up, took the mat he had been lying on,
and **went home praising God**.*

The Greek is **enopion auton** — before them.

## The reaction

*Wonder swept over every person in the room. They **gave glory to God**, and they were **filled
with awe**, and they kept saying, "**We have seen astonishing things today**."*

The Greek is **paradoxa** — things contrary to expectation. Where *paradox* comes from.

Luke says *every person*, which includes the men who had just accused Him of blasphemy.

## What the title is

**Son of Man** — His usual self-designation, and it comes from Daniel 7: *one like the Son of
Man, coming with the clouds of heaven… and to Him was given dominion and glory and a
kingdom*.

It sounds modest and it is not.

## The parallel accounts

Mark has the same episode with the roof and the four friends. See the note on
Mark 2:1–5.

Matthew compresses it and adds the crowd's conclusion: *they marvelled and glorified God, who
had given such power to men*.

> **The word doing the work.** *Easier to say.* One claim can be checked in the next ten
> seconds and the other cannot — and He offers the checkable one as proof of the other.
