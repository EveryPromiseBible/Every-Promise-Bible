He describes her, and every image is drawn from things that grow or graze.

## Where this comes

She has been searching the city for him at night, and the watchmen have struck her.

Then the daughters of Jerusalem ask *what is your beloved more than another beloved?* and she
answers at length — *my beloved is white and ruddy, chief among ten thousand*.

And then he speaks, and this is part of what he says.

## The teeth

*Your teeth are like **a flock of ewes come up from the washing**, each one **paired with its
twin**, **not one of them alone**.*

The Hebrew is **shekullam mat'imot** — all of them bearing twins. And **veshakkulah eyn bahem** —
and none bereaved among them.

## What the picture is

White sheep on a hillside, just washed, in matched pairs, with none missing.

The upper and lower teeth as matched pairs, and the whole set complete.

## The word for bereaved

**Shakkulah** — the word used of a mother who has lost a child, and of a bear robbed of her cubs.

It is a strong word to use of a gap in a row of sheep, and the poem uses it because the flock is
being described as a family with nothing lost.

## The repetition

He said the same thing in chapter 4, almost word for word: *your teeth are like a flock of shorn
sheep which have come up from the washing*.

The Song circles back. Images recur, with variations, the way a refrain does.

## The cheek

*Your cheek **behind your veil** is **a slice of pomegranate**.*

The Hebrew is **kephelach harimmon** — like a section, a split piece, of the pomegranate.

## Why a pomegranate

Cut one open and the inside is deep red, densely packed, glowing.

The image is of colour beneath a covering — a flush visible through the veil.

## Where pomegranates appear

On the hem of the high priest's robe, alternating with golden bells: *a golden bell and a
pomegranate, a golden bell and a pomegranate, all around the hem*.

Carved on the capitals of the two pillars at the temple porch, two hundred in rows.

One of the seven species of the good land: *a land of wheat and barley, of vines and fig trees
and pomegranates*. See the note on Deuteronomy 8:6–9.

And in the cluster the spies carried back from Eshcol, with the figs and the grapes.

## The veil

**Tsammah** — a veil or covering.

The description is of a woman partly hidden, and the poem says so rather than looking past it.

## What kind of book this is

Poetry, in the voices of two lovers and a chorus, without a narrator to explain it.

Rabbi Akiva said of it that all the writings are holy, but this one is the holy of holies.

## What comes immediately after

*There are sixty queens and eighty concubines, and virgins without number. **My dove, my perfect
one, is the only one**.*

The Hebrew is **achat hi** — she is one.

Against a court full of women, a single sentence of exclusivity.

## The question the chapter ends with

*Who is she who looks forth as the morning, **fair as the moon, clear as the sun**, awesome as an
army with banners?*

The largest image in the book, applied to the same woman whose cheek was compared to fruit.

> **The word.** **Shakkulah** — bereaved, robbed of young. Used of a gap in a flock, because the
> row is being described as a family with nothing missing from it.
