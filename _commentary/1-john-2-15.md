One sentence and its consequence, and everything depends on what the noun means.

## The instruction

*Do not give your love to **the world**, or to anything in it.*

The Greek is **me agapate ton kosmon**.

Present imperative — stop loving, or do not go on loving.

## What *kosmos* does not mean here

Not the planet. God made it and called it good.

Not people. John's own Gospel has *God so loved the world that He gave His only begotten Son*.
Same word, same author.

## What it does mean

John defines it himself in the next verse, which is why the two belong together:

*All that is in the world — **the lust of the flesh, the lust of the eyes, and the pride of
life** — is not of the Father but is of the world.* See the note on
1 John 2:16–17.

A system, running on three engines. Appetite, acquisition, and status.

## The consequence

*If anyone loves the world, **the love of the Father is not in him**.*

The Greek allows two readings: the Father's love for him, or his love for the Father.

The second fits John's usage better, and the argument does not depend on it either way. The two
loves cannot occupy the same space.

## The parallel elsewhere

*No one can serve two masters; for either he will hate the one and love the other, or else he
will be loyal to the one and despise the other. **You cannot serve God and mammon**.*

Same structure. Not *should not*. Cannot.

And James, more bluntly: *do you not know that friendship with the world is enmity with God?*

## Why it is not asceticism

The letter is not against material things.

John opens his third letter wishing his friend prosperity and health. Paul tells Timothy that
God *gives us richly all things to enjoy*.

The object of the verb is what is at issue. **Agapate** — set your love on.

## The reason given

The verse after the definition: *and the world is passing away, and the lust of it; but **he who
does the will of God abides forever***.

The Greek is **paragetai** — is passing by, going past. A present tense.

Not *will pass away*. It is going now, while being loved.

That is the argument. Not that the world is bad, but that it is in motion.

## Who is being written to

The letter addresses three groups by name — little children, young men, fathers — and repeats
each.

*I write to you, young men, because you have overcome the wicked one.*

*I write to you, fathers, because you have known Him who is from the beginning.*

The instruction about the world comes immediately after that paragraph, and the young men have
just been told they are strong.

## What John says at the end of the letter

*And we know that we are of God, and **the whole world lies under the sway of the wicked one**.*

Which is the reason for the whole passage.

## The other sentence John wrote about the world

The same writer, the same word — **kosmos** — and the most famous verse in the Bible:

*For **God so loved the world** that He gave His only begotten Son.*

Put beside *do not love the world*, that looks like a contradiction until you notice what each
sentence is doing. God loves the people in it and gave His Son for them. What believers are told not
to love is the system: the arrangement of desire, display and ambition that John itemises in the
very next verse.

So the two commands point the same way. You are not being asked to care less about anybody in the
world; you are being asked not to build your life on something Jesus Christ came to rescue people
out of — and John gives the reason plainly: *the world is passing away, and the lust of it, but he
who does the will of God abides forever.*

> **What the noun means.** John supplies it in the next verse — appetite, acquisition, status.
> And his reason for the instruction is not that the system is evil but that it is in motion.
