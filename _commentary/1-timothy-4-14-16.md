Take these things to heart, our text says — and that is the right feel for it. Underneath sits a Greek word with more packed into it than any one English phrase can carry, so here is what is in there.

## It is the Psalm 1 word

The Greek word is meletao. It is the word the Greek Old Testament uses in Psalm 1 — the man whose delight is in the law, and who meditates in it day and night.

And look at what Paul said two verses earlier: give your attention to the reading of the Scriptures aloud. Then he says this. He is telling a young minister to do with these things what the blessed man of Psalm 1 does with the law.

## It is not emptying your mind. It is filling it.

Behind the Greek stands a Hebrew word, hagah. It does not mean sitting still and going blank. It means muttering, murmuring, going over something under your breath.

The same Hebrew word gets used for a lion growling over its food, and for a dove moaning. It is a noise word. Biblical meditation is closer to chewing something over out loud than it is to silence.

## And it also means practice

In ordinary Greek, meletao is what a public speaker does when he drills a speech — going over it until it is in him and he no longer has to reach for it.

So both are in the one word: turn it over, and let it become yours. Say it until it lives in you.

> One thing not to push too far. The Greek word for meditate does cover quiet reflection as well — the noisy, muttering sense comes from the Hebrew word behind the Psalm, not from the Greek on its own. The claim that holds is this: biblical meditation means filling your mind with something and going over it, not emptying your mind. That much is solid, and it is enough.

## Why "profiting"

The word is prokope. It comes from a verb meaning to cut forward — the way someone hacks a path through undergrowth ahead of everyone else. It is not money quietly accumulating. It is ground gained.

Paul is saying: stay in this, and people will be able to see the path opening up in front of you.

## And now the word this block ends on

Look at how it finishes: you will be the saving of yourself and of everyone who listens to you.

The word is sozo.

**Sozo means to save, to rescue, to heal, to make whole, to preserve, to bring safely through. Complete salvation and complete deliverance — in any and every sense a person could need it.** That is not a devotional stretch. That is the size of the word.

In English, saved has narrowed until it means one thing: getting out of hell. The Greek word never narrowed like that, and it never had to.

## A picture of it

Think of a man pulled out of the sea.

The English word stops at the moment he stops sinking. He did not drown. That is saved.

But the rescue is not finished there, and no one who has actually been rescued thinks it is. He is hauled over the side. The water is got out of him. Somebody wraps him in something dry, puts hot food in his hands, and sits with him until he stops shaking. He is carried to shore. He is taken home. In the morning he wakes up in his own bed.

Sozo is the whole of that. Not the instant he stopped going under — everything from the water to the front door.

## The same word does all of it

It is the word the disciples shout when the boat is going down in the storm and they wake Jesus: save us, we are perishing. Nobody in that boat is thinking about the afterlife. They mean get us out of this.

It is the word used when a blind beggar receives his sight. It is the word used of the woman who had been ill for twelve years, when she touches Him and the bleeding stops. It is the word James uses for a sick man raised up when the church prays over him.

And it is the word for the rescue of a life, finally and for ever.

One word, wide enough to hold all of it — because what God does with a person was never divided into compartments in the first place.
