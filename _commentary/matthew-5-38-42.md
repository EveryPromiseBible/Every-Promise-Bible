The fifth of the six *you have heard it said* sayings, and every example in it is a small,
specific humiliation.

## What was said

*"You have heard that it was said, '**An eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth**.'"*

The **lex talionis**, and it appears three times in the Torah.

It was not a licence for revenge. It was a cap on it — a limit stopping a feud from escalating,
and it was administered by courts rather than by injured parties.

Being quoted, by His day, to justify personal retaliation.

## The instruction

*"But I say to you, **do not strike back at the one who wrongs you**."*

The Greek is **me antistenai to ponero** — do not resist, or do not set yourself against, the
evil one, or evil.

**Anthistemi** is a legal and military word. It is used of standing against in court, and of
withstanding in battle.

Paul uses it of himself: *I withstood Peter to his face*. And James: *resist the devil, and he
will flee*.

The word is capable of a narrower sense here, and the four examples that follow are what
define it.

## The first

*"If someone slaps you on **the right cheek**."*

The Greek specifies which one.

A right-handed man striking your right cheek is using the back of his hand. In that culture a
backhanded blow was not a fight — it was how a superior corrected an inferior.

Offering the other cheek forces the striker to use an open hand, which is how equals hit each
other.

## The second

*"If someone wants to **drag you into court and take your shirt**, let him have your coat
too."*

The **chiton** is the inner garment; the **himation** is the outer cloak.

The law protected the cloak specifically: *if you ever take your neighbour's garment as a
pledge, you shall return it to him before the sun goes down. For that is his only covering.*

This is somebody suing a poor man for the last thing he owns.

## The third

*"If someone **forces you to carry his load a mile**, go with him two."*

The Greek is **angareusei** — a Persian loan-word for official requisition.

A Roman soldier could legally compel a provincial to carry his pack for one mile. It was a
standing grievance and a daily humiliation.

The instruction concerns the second mile, which no law required and no soldier could ask for.

## The fourth

*"**Give to the one who asks** you, and when someone wants to borrow from you, **do not turn your
back on him**."*

## What the four have in common

Every one is a small, specific, legal indignity: a backhand, a lawsuit, a requisition, a
request for money.

None is a violent crime, and none is about a third party in danger.

The passage is about personal insult and personal loss, in the ordinary transactions of an
occupied country.

## What He does with it Himself

*And when He had said this, one of the officers who stood by **struck Jesus with the palm of his
hand**.*

And His answer: *if I have spoken evil, bear witness of the evil; but if well, why do you strike
Me?*

He does not hit back, and He does not stay silent either.

## What follows

The sixth and last: *love your enemies, bless those who curse you… that you may be sons of your
Father in heaven; for He makes His sun rise on the evil and on the good*.

And then the standard: *therefore you shall be perfect, just as your Father in heaven is
perfect*.

## Paul's version

*Do not pay anyone back evil for evil… **if it can be done, and as far as it rests with you, be
at peace with everyone**.* See the note on Romans 12:17–21.

Paul keeps the instruction and adds two qualifications the sermon does not.

> **What the four examples are.** A backhand, a lawsuit over a shirt, a soldier's pack, and a
> loan. Small humiliations in an occupied country — and the second mile is the one nobody could
> demand.
