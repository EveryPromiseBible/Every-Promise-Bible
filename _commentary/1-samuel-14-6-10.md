The best sentence anybody says in the book of Samuel, and it has a *maybe* in it.

## The proposal

*"Come on, let's cross over to the outpost of **these uncircumcised men**."*

The insult marks them as outside the covenant, and it is how Israel referred to Philistines.

David uses the same word to Goliath.

## The hesitation

*"**Maybe** the LORD will act for us."*

The Hebrew is **ulai** — perhaps, it may be.

Jonathan does not claim a promise. He has had no word, no prophet, and no instruction.

He says *perhaps*, and goes anyway.

That is the honest part, and it is why the sentence carries. He is not certain and he is not
paralysed.

## The conviction

*"**Nothing can stop the LORD from saving, whether by many or by few**."*

The Hebrew is *there is no restraint to the LORD to save by many or by few*.

**Ma'tsor** — a restraint, a hindrance.

What he is sure of is not the outcome. It is that the arithmetic is not a factor.

The chapter before had established the arithmetic precisely: two swords in the army. See the
note on 1 Samuel 13:19–23.

## The answer

*"**Do whatever's in your heart**," his armour-bearer said. "Go ahead — **I'm with you, heart and
soul**."*

The Hebrew is *behold, I am with you according to your heart*.

An unnamed young man, whose job is carrying somebody else's equipment, agreeing to climb a
cliff into a garrison.

He gets one line in Scripture and it is that one.

## The sign

*"We'll cross over toward them and **let them see us**."*

Deliberately. They do not sneak.

*"If they say, '**Wait there till we come to you**,' then we'll stay put and not go up."*

*"But if they say, '**Come on up to us**,' then we'll climb up — because that will be our sign
that the LORD has handed them over to us."*

## What the sign is

Two options, and Jonathan assigns the meanings before either happens.

It reads like a fleece, and it is not quite one. Both outcomes are plausible; he is not asking
for anything improbable.

What he has done is commit himself in advance to whichever answer comes.

## Why that reading is better than the alternative

If they are told to wait, the garrison is coming down to level ground and the two of them are
finished.

If they are told to come up, they climb a cliff face into a defended position, on hands and
feet, with no cover.

Jonathan has chosen the *worse* military option as his sign to advance.

## What happens

*"Come on up here — **we've got something to teach you**!"* See the note on
1 Samuel 14:11–15.

## Where the same principle turns up

*"The LORD does not save with sword and spear; for **the battle is the LORD's**."*

David to Goliath, in the same book, with a sling.

And Gideon, told to send twenty-two thousand men home and then most of the remainder: *the
people who are with you are too many*.

And Zechariah: *not by might nor by power, but by My Spirit*. See the note on
Zechariah 4:6–10.

> **The word that makes the sentence honest.** *Perhaps.* He has no promise and no prophet, and
> what he is certain of is only that the numbers are not the deciding factor.
