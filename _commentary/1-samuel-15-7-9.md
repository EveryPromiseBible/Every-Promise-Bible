Saul wins the battle and loses the kingdom, and the difference is a list of what
he decided to keep.

## The sorting

*They kept the best of the sheep and cattle, the fat calves and lambs —
everything that was good. These they were unwilling to destroy. But whatever was
weak or worthless, they devoted to destruction.*

He obeyed the command exactly, on everything he did not want.

The narrator lays it out as a sorting exercise: good things kept, poor things
destroyed. Obedience applied to the portion where obedience cost nothing.

## Agag

*He took Agag, king of the Amalekites, alive.*

The king is the other thing kept. And that detail runs a long way — Haman, in the
book of Esther, is called an Agagite, and he comes within a decree of destroying
the Jewish people centuries later.

What Saul kept alive turned up again.

## What he says afterwards

Read on and it is the excuses that undo him. He tells Samuel he *has* performed
the LORD's command; when the bleating of sheep gives him away, he says the people
took them, and then that it was to sacrifice to the LORD.

Samuel's answer is the line the chapter is remembered for: *to obey is better
than sacrifice.*

## What was kept

*The best of the sheep and the oxen, the fatlings and the lambs, and **all that was good**.*

He destroyed *everything despised and worthless*. What he kept was the part that looked
useful.

Amalek is the one enemy Scripture says is fought in every generation, and the reason is that
what looks worth keeping comes back. Four hundred years later Haman the Agagite has a signet
ring. See the note on Deuteronomy 25:17–19.

## What Samuel says about it

*"**To obey is better than sacrifice**, and to heed than the fat of rams."*

Saul's defence is that the livestock were kept *to sacrifice to the LORD your God*. He is
proposing to offer God the thing God told him to destroy.

## Where the obedience came from in the end

*For as by one man's disobedience many were made sinners, so also by **one Man's obedience**
many will be made righteous.*

*And being found in appearance as a man, He humbled Himself and **became obedient to the point
of death**, even the death of the cross.*

The king who could not carry out one command is in the same book as the promise of a King who
would.

> **The word for the sparing.** *They were unwilling* — the Hebrew is about not
> being willing, refusing. It is not that Saul lacked the opportunity or the
> means. Everything in the sentence turns on what he wanted to keep.
