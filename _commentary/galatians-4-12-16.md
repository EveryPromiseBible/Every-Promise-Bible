Paul stops arguing for five verses and talks about the first time he came.

## The plea

*Brothers, **I am begging you**.*

The Greek is **deomai** — to plead out of need. The tone changes completely from the
chapters either side.

## The exchange

*Become like me, **for I became like you**.*

He had described what that cost in the first letter to Corinth: *to those who are under the
law, as under the law… to those who are without law, as without law… **I have become all
things to all men***.

## The clearance

*You have **not wronged me at all**.*

He removes any suggestion that this is personal grievance before saying anything else.

## Why he stopped there

*You know that it was **because of a weakness of the flesh** that I preached the good news
to you **the first time**.*

The Greek is **astheneian tes sarkos** — bodily weakness, infirmity.

He had not planned to preach in Galatia. Something physical stopped him there.

Nothing in the New Testament says what it was. Paul mentions *a thorn in the flesh* in
2 Corinthians and does not identify that either.

## What they did with it

*And the trial that was in my flesh **you did not treat with contempt**, and you **did not
turn away from me**.*

The Greek for *treat with contempt* is **exoutheneo** — to reject as worthless. The other
verb is literally *you did not spit* — a gesture used against illness and the evil eye in
that world.

Whatever the condition was, it was visible and it was the kind of thing people reacted to.

## The welcome

*You welcomed me **as an angel of God, as Christ Jesus Himself**.*

## The question

*Where, then, is **the blessing you spoke of in those days**?*

The Greek is *where is your blessedness* — the happiness they had at the time.

## The measure of it

*For I can bear you witness that, **had it been possible, you would have torn out your own
eyes and handed them to me**.*

The detail has made readers wonder about his eyes ever since. He ends this letter *see with
what large letters I have written to you with my own hand*.

The Illumination keeps the sentence as it stands and offers no diagnosis.

## The question at the end

*So **have I become your enemy now, because I tell you the truth**?*

The Greek is **aletheuon** — telling the truth to you.

He does not soften anything in the letter. He asks whether the telling is what changed
things.

## Where this sits

Between the allegory of the two women and the sentence about freedom. See the notes on
Galatians 4:21–27 and Galatians 4:28–31.

The hardest argument in his letters has a personal paragraph inserted in the middle of it.

## What he says two verses on

*My little children, for whom **I labour in birth again until Christ is formed in you**.*

The Greek is **odino** — the word for labour pains.

Paul, writing as a mother.

> **What he reminds them of.** Not his credentials. That he arrived ill, that they did not
> recoil, and that they were happy — and then he asks what happened to that.
