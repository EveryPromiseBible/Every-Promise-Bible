This verse gets quoted at people in hard circumstances more often than almost any
other, and usually with one word quietly dropped.

## The word that is always dropped

*Anyone who is **unwilling** to work should not eat.*

The Greek does not say *does not work*. It says *does not want to*. Paul is
describing a decision, not a situation. Everyone who cannot find work, cannot
work, or has run out of options is outside the sentence entirely.

## Who he was actually writing to

The next line names them: people in that church who were *drifting, doing no work
at all, and yet somehow finding the hours to busy themselves in everyone else's
affairs.*

The likeliest cause is in the letter itself. Some of them had concluded the Lord's
return was imminent and had downed tools to wait. Paul is not scolding the poor.
He is dealing with a specific enthusiasm that had turned into idleness — and, as
he notes drily, into meddling.

## What he tells them to do

*Settle down, do your own work without all the noise, and eat food you have
earned.* Then, immediately after, he tells the rest of the church not to grow
weary in doing good.

Both instructions in the same breath: work quietly, and keep being generous.

> **How Paul argues it.** He appeals to his own habits first — he paid his way
> in Thessalonica rather than take support, though he says outright he had the
> right to. He is asking of them something he had already done in front of them.
