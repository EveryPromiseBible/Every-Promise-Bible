Luke stops the narrative to write out a table of nations.

## Why the list is there

He could have written *people from everywhere*. Instead he gives fifteen entries by
name, and the naming is the point — every one of them heard their own language.

## The geography

The list moves roughly in a circle, east to west and back:

- **Parthians, Medes, Elamites** — the far east, beyond the empire, in what is now Iran.
- **Mesopotamia** — where Abraham came from.
- **Judea** — the middle, and the odd one on the list.
- **Cappadocia, Pontus, Asia, Phrygia, Pamphylia** — Asia Minor, and every one of them
  turns up later in Acts or the letters. Peter addresses his first letter to four of
  them.
- **Egypt, Libya toward Cyrene** — North Africa. Simon who carried the cross was from
  Cyrene.
- **Visitors from Rome** — the capital, *both Jews and converts to the Jewish faith*.
- **Cretans and Arabs** — the last two, and the least expected.

## Who they were

Jews of the diaspora, in Jerusalem for the feast, plus proselytes — Gentiles who had
come into Judaism.

Most of them would have spoken Greek or Aramaic well enough to get by. The languages
they hear are the ones from home.

## What they heard

*"**We are hearing them tell the wonderful works of God** in our own tongues!"*

The Greek is **ta megaleia tou theou** — the great things, the magnificent deeds.

Nobody in the list reports hearing a sermon, an instruction or an appeal. What they hear
is the works of God being told.

## What that is

The Greek for *tell* is the ordinary word for speaking. The content is God's own acts.

Peter's sermon comes afterward, in Aramaic or Greek, and it is a separate thing.

## The reversal

Babel is the other passage where languages multiply in one event, and there the result is
that nobody understands anybody and the people scatter.

Here the languages multiply and everybody understands, and the people gather.

Luke never mentions Babel. He writes out the table of nations and leaves it there.

## The feast

Pentecost — **Shavuot**, fifty days after Passover, the wheat harvest, and the feast at
which two loaves *baked with leaven* were waved before the LORD.

Jewish tradition also counted it as the anniversary of the giving of the law at Sinai.

## The two crowds

*They were all amazed and perplexed, saying to one another, "Whatever could this
mean?"*

*Others mocking said, "They are full of new wine."*

Both reactions are recorded in the next verses, from the same event.

## The number

Three thousand were added that day.

At Sinai, on the day the law was given and the calf was made, three thousand fell. See
the note on Exodus 32:25–29.

> **What the list is for.** Luke is a careful writer and he spends three verses on
> place names. Every one is a claim that a specific person heard the great things of God
> in the language they grew up speaking.
