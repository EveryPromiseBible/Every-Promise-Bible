Two verses that are almost always quoted apart, and the second is the reason the
first is possible.

## "Work out" is not "work for"

*Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling.*

The verb means to bring to completion, to work something through to its end —
what you do with something you already have. It was used of working a mine:
extracting what is in there.

Paul is not telling them to earn it. The letter has already called them saints
and said God began a good work in them.

## The word "for"

*For it is God who is at work in you.*

Everything turns on that connective. The reason to work it out is that Someone
else is working in you.

And the verb is **energeo** — the word behind our *energy*. God is the one
supplying the power; the working out is what that power produces on the outside.

## Both halves of the willing

*Both the willing and the doing.*

Not merely the strength to do what you already wanted. The wanting itself is
described as His work.

That is the sentence that takes the pressure out of the first verse. If even the
desire is being supplied, then *fear and trembling* is not anxiety about
performance — it is the seriousness appropriate to God working inside a person.

## Why Paul says it here

Look at what precedes it: the hymn about Christ emptying Himself, taking the form
of a servant, obedient to death on a cross, and being exalted.

*And so, my beloved friends* — the instruction flows out of that. The working in
is by the One who already did the emptying.

> **The purpose clause.** *For the sake of what pleases Him.* The whole operation,
> willing and doing alike, is aimed at His good pleasure — which is where Paul
> located the reason for everything back in Ephesians 1. See that note.
