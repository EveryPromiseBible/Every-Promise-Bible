The letter has spent twelve chapters explaining that the sacrifices are finished.
Then it names three that are not.

## The first

*Through Him let us keep bringing God an offering of praise, words out of our
mouths thanking His name.*

The phrase is from Hosea — *the fruit of our lips*. Under the old system you
brought an animal; here the offering is speech.

Two details keep it from being sentimental. It is **through Him** — the same
mediation the whole letter has been arguing for. And it is *continually*, a
present-tense keeping on rather than an occasion.

## The second and third

*Do not forget to do good and to share what you have; those are the offerings God
takes pleasure in.*

Praise, then generosity — and the writer uses sacrificial vocabulary for both.
The word for *takes pleasure* is the one used of a sacrifice being accepted as a
pleasing aroma.

So the offerings that remain are: what you say about God, and what you do for
people.

## The instruction about leaders

*They stay awake over your souls, and they will have to give an account, so let
it be a glad job for them and not a groaning one — groaning would do you no
good.*

An unusually practical note. The reason given for cooperating is not the leaders'
authority but the readers' own benefit: a discouraged shepherd is no use to the
sheep.

## The request

*Pray for us… I ask it all the more urgently, so that I can be given back to you
sooner.*

The writer of the most theologically dense letter in the New Testament ends by
asking for prayer and admitting he wants to see them.

> **Why "through Him" carries the weight.** Praise offered by people who know
> their own record can only be brought if somebody else makes it acceptable —
> which is precisely what the gold plate on Aaron's forehead was for, and what the
> letter says Christ now is. See the note on Exodus 28:36–38.
