Two nouns, and the whole difference is in which of them you are owed.

## The wage

*Sin **pays a wage**, and the wage is death.*

The Greek is **opsonia** — a soldier's pay, the rations and coin issued at the end
of a stint. It is what a man has earned and can demand.

Nobody is given death. It is drawn.

## The gift

*God **gives a gift**, and the gift is life that has no end.*

The Greek is **charisma**, from **charis** — grace. A thing handed over free, with
no work behind it.

Paul deliberately does not use the same word twice. He could have written *the
wages of sin is death, and the wages of God is life*, and the sentence would have
collapsed. The two halves are not the same kind of transaction.

## What kind of life

**Zoe aionios** — and it is not primarily about duration. The New Testament's word
for endless time is doing something else here.

Jesus defines it in John 17: *this is eternal life, that they may know You, the
only true God, and Jesus Christ whom You have sent.*

It is God's own kind of life, given to a man, and the fact that it does not run out
is one of its properties rather than its definition.

## "In Christ Jesus"

The last three words locate it. The gift is not posted separately; it is in a
Person, and it comes with Him.

## The verse before

*Having been **set free from sin** and made slaves of God, you have your fruit, and
it leads to being made holy.*

That is the state Paul is describing the gift arriving into. Freed first, and then
the fruit follows.

The Greek for *made slaves of God* is the strong word — bondservants. Paul's
picture is not neutrality; a man is owned either way, and the question is by
whom.

## Why the chapter exists

Romans 6 opens with the objection: *shall we continue in sin that grace may
abound?* — which is the question grace always provokes, and Paul's answer is not to
add rules.

It is a funeral: *we died to sin; how shall we live any longer in it?* You cannot
go back to a house you were carried out of.

> **The most quoted half.** *The wages of sin is death* is preached far more often
> than the clause after it, and the clause after it is where the sentence is going.
> Paul wrote one sentence with a *but* in the middle.
