The first Old Testament text ever preached about the resurrection, and **Peter** takes it apart in
public.

## The quotation

*"**I foresaw the LORD always before my face, for He is at my right hand, that I may not be
shaken**… **For You will not leave my soul in Hades, nor will You allow Your Holy One to see
corruption**."*

Psalm 16, quoted in full.

## The argument

*"**Men and brethren, let me speak freely to you of the patriarch David, that he is both dead and
buried, and his tomb is with us to this day.**"*

**Peter** is standing a few hundred yards from it. The argument is archaeological: the man who wrote
*You will not allow Your Holy One to see corruption* is still in the ground, so he was not writing
about himself.

*"**He, foreseeing this, spoke concerning the resurrection of the Christ, that His soul was not left
in Hades, nor did His flesh see corruption.**"*

## What Paul does with the same psalm

*"**Therefore He also says in another Psalm: 'You will not allow Your Holy One to see corruption.'
For David, after he had served his own generation by the will of God, fell asleep, was buried with
his fathers, and saw corruption; but He whom God raised up saw no corruption.**"*

**Acts** 13, in a synagogue in Antioch, and **Paul** runs **Peter**'s argument almost word for word.
Two apostles, years apart, in different cities, reach for the same psalm and make the same case
from it. See the note on Acts 13:34–37.

## The line before the resurrection clause

*"**Therefore my heart rejoiced, and my tongue was glad; moreover my flesh also will rest in
hope.**"*

**Kataskenosei ep' elpidi** — will encamp in hope. The Greek Old Testament's verb is the one for
pitching a tent.

*Who for **the joy that was set before Him** endured the cross, despising the shame.*

**Hebrews** 12 says the same thing about the same person: there was gladness on the far side of it,
and He was looking at it.

## The last line

*"**You have made known to me the ways of life; You will make me full of joy in Your presence.**"*

**Peter** quotes the psalm to the end. The argument is about a body not decaying, and the sentence he
finishes on is about somebody's face.

*In Your presence is fullness of joy; at Your right hand are pleasures forevermore.*

> **The argument.** *His tomb is with us to this day.* Peter proves the psalm is not about David by
> pointing at David's grave — and Paul makes the identical case in Antioch years later.
