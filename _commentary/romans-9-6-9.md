The question underneath chapters 9 to 11, stated in the first sentence.

## What has just been said

*I tell the truth in Christ, I am not lying… that **I have great sorrow and continual grief in my
heart**.*

*For I could wish that I myself were accursed from Christ **for my brethren, my countrymen
according to the flesh**.* See the note on Romans 9:1–3.

Paul has just finished eight chapters ending *nothing shall be able to separate us from the love
of God*, and immediately says he would give up his own place for his people.

## The problem

Israel had the covenants, the law, the promises, the fathers, and the Messiah came from them —
and largely did not receive Him.

If the promises were to Israel, and Israel has not taken them, then either the promises failed or
something else is going on.

## The denial

*It is not as though **the word of God has fallen to the ground**.*

The Greek is **ouch hoion de hoti ekpeptoken** — from **ekpipto**, to fall out, to drop away. The
word used of a ship drifting off course and of flowers falling.

Paul states the conclusion before the argument.

## The distinction

*Not everyone descended from Israel **is Israel**.*

The Greek is **ou gar pantes hoi ex Israel houtoi Israel**.

The same noun twice, meaning two different things in one sentence.

## The second

*And not everyone who comes from Abraham **is counted as his children**.*

Physical descent, and something else.

## The proof

*As it stands written, "**It is through Isaac that your offspring will be named**."*

Genesis 21, when Sarah demanded Hagar and Ishmael be sent away and Abraham was distressed.

Both sons were Abraham's. Only one carried the line.

## The principle

*That is to say, **it is not the children born in the ordinary way** who are the children of God; it
is **the children of the promise** who are counted as the offspring.*

The Greek is **ta tekna tes sarkos** — the children of the flesh. And **ta tekna tes epangelias**
— the children of the promise.

## The quotation

*For the promise ran like this: "**At this time next year I will come, and Sarah will have a
son**."*

Genesis 18, at the tent door in the heat of the day, with Sarah listening behind the flap and
laughing. See the note on Genesis 18:9–15.

## What the Greek says about the timing

**Kata ton kairon touton** — according to this **kairos**.

The appointed time. Paul quotes the Septuagint, which renders the Hebrew **la'et chayyah** —
at the time of life, at the reviving season.

## What makes Isaac different

Not his character. He is a passive figure through most of his story.

He was born to a woman past childbearing after twenty-five years of waiting, which means he
exists because of a promise rather than because of anything anybody managed.

*And not being weak in believing, he did not consider his own body, already dead… **being fully
convinced that what He had promised He was also able to perform**.* See the note on
Romans 4:20–22.

## The next example

Rebekah's twins — *for the children not yet being born, nor having done any good or evil… it was
said to her, "The older shall serve the younger."*

Same womb, same father, same day. Paul picks the case where every variable is controlled.

## Where the argument arrives

*And so **all Israel will be saved**, as it is written: "The Deliverer will come out of Zion."*

*For **the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable**.* See the note on
Romans 11:28–32.

The Greek is **ametameleta** — not to be repented of, not withdrawn.

Three chapters after saying the word has not fallen, Paul says why.

> **The first sentence.** *The word of God has not fallen to the ground.* Paul states the verdict
> before the argument, and spends three chapters on why the promise ran through a promised son.
