God describes what a ruined country is going to look like, and the order of
events is the point.

## What comes first

*On the day I cleanse you from all your sins, I will settle people in your towns
again, and the ruins will be rebuilt.*

The cleansing is stated before the rebuilding, and the rebuilding is dated from
it: **on the day** I cleanse you.

The chapter has already said how — *I will sprinkle clean water on you… I will
give you a new heart and put a new spirit in you.* The restoration of the land
follows the restoration of the people, not the other way round.

## The comparison the neighbours make

*People will say, "This land that was ruined has become like the garden of Eden."*

The observation is put in the mouths of outsiders. God does not describe the
country that way Himself; He says that is what will be said about it.

Eden is the highest comparison available in Hebrew — the garden before anything
went wrong.

## What the towns were

*Rubble, deserted and torn down*, and now *walled and full of life*. Ezekiel is
writing to people who had watched Jerusalem fall and had walked away from the
smoke.

To them, this is not poetry about an abstract future. It is their own streets.

## Why God says He does it

*Then the nations left around you will know that I, the LORD, have rebuilt what
was torn down.*

The stated audience is the surrounding nations. Earlier in the chapter He says it
plainly: this is not for Israel's sake but for the sake of His name, which they
had profaned among the nations.

## The last line

*I, the LORD, have spoken, and I will do it.*

Two verbs, and the second guarantees the first. Ezekiel ends the promise the way
Isaiah ends his — with the fact that it was said out loud by someone who finishes
what He says. See the note on Isaiah 55:10–11.
