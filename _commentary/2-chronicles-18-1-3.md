A good king ties himself to a bad one, and the chapter opens by telling you
exactly how it happened.

## The order of the sentence

*Now Jehoshaphat had great riches and honour, **and he joined himself to Ahab by
marriage**.*

The prosperity and the alliance are in the same breath. Jehoshaphat was one of
Judah's better kings — the previous chapter has him removing idols and sending
teachers out with the law through every town.

The marriage was almost certainly a sensible piece of statecraft: his son to
Ahab's daughter, two kingdoms at peace.

## The visit

*Some years later he went down to visit Ahab in Samaria. Ahab slaughtered many
sheep and cattle for him… and he urged him to march up against Ramoth Gilead.*

The Hebrew for *urged* is a strong word — enticed, persuaded, worked on him. The
banquet came first and the request came after.

## The answer

*I am as you are, and my people as your people; we will join you in the war.*

He agrees before asking a single question. Only afterwards does he think to say
*first seek the counsel of the LORD* — and when four hundred prophets say go, and
one says otherwise, and Ahab admits he hates that one for never prophesying
anything good, Jehoshaphat still goes.

He had already given his word at the dinner.

> **How it ends for him.** He survives, but a prophet meets him on the way home:
> *should you help the wicked and love those who hate the LORD?* And years later
> the marriage he made brings Ahab's daughter Athaliah into Judah's royal house,
> where she murders almost the entire line of David. The alliance outlived the
> battle by a generation.
