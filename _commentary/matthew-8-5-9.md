A Roman officer stops Jesus in the street and makes an argument about authority
that Jesus calls the greatest faith in Israel.

## Who he was

A centurion — roughly eighty to a hundred men under him, the backbone of the
Roman army, and an occupier in a country that hated the occupation.

And the person he is asking about is *his servant*. A man with the power to
replace a slave is out in the road pleading for one, describing the condition in
detail: paralysed, in terrible pain.

## What he refuses

*Lord, I am not worthy to have You come under my roof.*

Jesus has already said He will come. The centurion turns down the visit — partly
because entering a Gentile home would make a Jewish teacher ceremonially unclean,
and he knows it.

He asks for less access than he is being offered.

## The argument

*Only say the word… I am a man under orders myself, and I have soldiers under
me. I say to one, "Go," and he goes.*

Here is the reasoning: I know how authority works. I do not need to be present
for my orders to be carried out, because the authority is not in my presence. It
is in my position.

He has understood that Jesus operates the same way — and, crucially, that Jesus
is *under* authority too, which is why the word carries.

He never asks Jesus to do anything except speak.

## What Jesus says

*I have not found such great faith, not even in Israel.* And then the servant is
healed at that moment, at a distance, without a word being spoken over him.

> **What he built.** Luke's account adds that the Jewish elders vouched for him:
> *he loves our nation and he built our synagogue.* An occupying officer paid for
> the local place of worship. That is the man who says he is not worthy to have
> Jesus in his house.
