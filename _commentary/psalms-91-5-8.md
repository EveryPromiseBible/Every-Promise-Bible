Four dangers named in two lines, and the way they are arranged tells you the
psalm means all of them.

## The four

*The terror of the night, the arrow that flies in the day, the plague that moves
in the dark, the ruin that strikes at noon.*

Two at night, two by day. Two hidden, two out in the open. It is a Hebrew way of
saying *everything* by naming the poles — the whole clock, and both kinds of
threat, the sort you can see coming and the sort you cannot.

Nothing is left outside the sentence.

## "It will not come near you"

*A thousand may fall at your side, ten thousand at your right hand, but it will
not come near you.*

The psalm does not pretend the danger is imaginary. People are falling — that is
in the verse. What it says is that the thing sweeping the ground around you does
not arrive where you are standing.

The Hebrew for *come near* is the ordinary word for approach. It is a promise
about proximity, spoken over someone in the middle of a field where others are
going down.

## Who it is spoken over

Verse 1 sets the terms for everything that follows: *the one who lives in the
shelter of the Most High, who stays in the shadow of the Almighty.*

Dwelling is the posture. Not visiting in an emergency — living there. The
promises in this psalm are addressed to somebody already at home in that place,
and the rest of the psalm reads as a description of what that address is like.

> **How it ends.** God takes over the sentence at verse 14 and speaks in the
> first person — *I will set you free… I will answer… I will be with you in
> trouble… with long life I will fill your hands.* The One making the promises
> stops being described and starts speaking. See the note on Psalms 91:14–16.
