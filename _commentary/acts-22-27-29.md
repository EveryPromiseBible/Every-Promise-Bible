A short exchange between a prisoner and the officer holding him, and the ranks reverse
inside three sentences.

## The commander comes

*The commander came and said to him, "**Tell me, are you a Roman?**"*

He comes down himself. He does not send for Paul, and Paul is still tied.

Claudius Lysias is a **chiliarch**, commander of a thousand men, the senior Roman
officer in Jerusalem.

## The answer

*He said, "**Yes**."*

One word. Luke gives him nothing else.

## What it cost the commander

*"**It cost me a large sum of money** to buy this citizenship."*

The Greek is *with a great sum I obtained this citizenship*.

Citizenship was for sale under Claudius, and the trade was notorious — the emperor's
freedmen and his wife were known to be selling it. The commander's name, **Claudius**
Lysias, is almost certainly the emperor's name taken at purchase, which was the custom.

He is a Greek who bought his way in, and he mentions the price.

## Paul's reply

*And Paul said, "**But I was born with it**."*

The Greek is *but I was even born so*.

Six words, and they settle the question of rank. Inherited citizenship outranked
purchased citizenship socially and absolutely.

The man in chains has more standing than the man who put him in them.

## How Paul had it

Luke never explains. Tarsus was a free city but that did not confer citizenship; someone
in the family must have been granted or bought it a generation or more earlier.

Paul mentions it three times in Acts and never in a letter.

## What happened immediately

*So the men who were about to examine him **stepped away from him at once**.*

The Greek is they *departed from him*. The straps are dropped where they stand.

## The commander's own state

*And **the commander himself was afraid** when he realized that **he had put a Roman in
chains**.*

Not the flogging he nearly ordered. The binding, which had already happened.

Merely chaining a citizen without trial was an offence. He is frightened of what he has
already done.

## What he does about it

He keeps him — but the next morning he releases him from the chains and convenes the
Sanhedrin to find out the charge.

And when a plot to ambush Paul is discovered, this same commander sends him to Caesarea
under escort: two hundred soldiers, seventy horsemen, two hundred spearmen, at nine
o'clock at night.

The Roman army moves one prisoner out of Jerusalem with four hundred and seventy men.

## The letter he writes

*This man was seized by the Jews and was about to be killed by them. Coming with the
troops I rescued him, **having learned that he was a Roman**.*

He rearranges the order of events slightly in his own favour. Luke includes the letter
anyway.

## Where the citizenship takes him

Two years later, in front of Festus: *I appeal to Caesar.* And the appeal is what puts
him on a ship to Rome.

He had been told he would go: *as you have testified for Me in Jerusalem, so you must
also bear witness at Rome.*

> **What it was used for.** Paul does not use the citizenship to get out. He uses it to
> stop a flogging, and then again to appeal — and the appeal sends him further into
> custody, all the way to the capital.
