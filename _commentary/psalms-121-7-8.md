The end of a short psalm, and one Hebrew verb is repeated through the whole of it.

## The word

**Shamar** — to keep, to guard, to watch over.

It appears six times in eight verses, and every occurrence has God as the subject.

It is the word used of Adam in the garden — *to tend and **keep** it* — and of the priestly
blessing: *the LORD bless you and **keep** you*.

## The psalm's shape

It opens as a question: *I will lift up my eyes to the hills — **from whence comes my
help**?*

And answers itself: *my help comes from the LORD, who made heaven and earth.*

## What comes before these verses

*He will not allow your foot to be moved; **He who keeps you will not slumber**.*

*Behold, **He who keeps Israel shall neither slumber nor sleep**.*

*The LORD is your keeper; the LORD is your shade at your right hand. The sun shall not
strike you by day, nor the moon by night.*

Sun and moon — day and night, the whole of a day covered.

## The scope

*The LORD will keep you **from all harm**.*

The Hebrew is *from all evil* — **kol ra**.

## What is kept

*He will keep **your very life**.*

The Hebrew is **nephesh** — soul, life, the person themselves.

The psalm moves from the foot in verse 3 to the life in verse 7.

## The two directions

*The LORD will keep **your going out and your coming in**.*

A Hebrew merism — naming the two ends to mean everything between.

It is the standard phrase for a whole life's activity. Moses uses it of himself at the end:
*I can no longer go out and come in*. And of Joshua: *he shall go out before them and come
in before them*.

## The two times

*From **this moment** and **forever**.*

The Hebrew is *from now and unto everlasting*.

## What the psalm is

One of the Songs of Ascents — Psalms 120 to 134, sung by pilgrims walking up to Jerusalem
for the feasts. See the note on Psalms 128:3–4.

The hills in the opening line are the ones on the road. Bandits worked them, and the shrines
on the high places were on them too.

The question *from whence comes my help* is asked by somebody looking at a hill country
they have to cross.

## What is not promised

That nothing happens. The psalm is about a keeper rather than about a smooth road, and it
is sung by people on a journey.

## The one who does not sleep

Twice: *will not slumber*, and *shall neither slumber nor sleep*.

Elijah's taunt on Carmel — *perhaps he is asleep and must be awakened* — was about a
different god. See the note on 1 Kings 18:25–29.

## The six-fold guard

*The LORD shall **preserve you from all evil**; He shall preserve your soul. The LORD shall
preserve **your going out and your coming in** from this time forth, and even forevermore.*

The Hebrew **shamar** — keep, guard, watch over — appears six times in eight verses. It is the
word for a sentry.

## The going out and the coming in

A Hebrew way of saying the whole of a life. Every departure and every return, and nothing
between them left out.

**Jesus**: *"this is the will of Him who sent Me, that of all He has given Me **I should lose
nothing**, but should raise it up at the last day."*

*"**Neither shall anyone snatch them out of My hand.**"*

## What a song of ascents is

Sung on the road up to Jerusalem for the feasts. This is travelling music — the going out and
the coming in are literal.

*"**I am the way**, the truth, and the life."*

## Who does not sleep

*Behold, **He who keeps Israel shall neither slumber nor sleep**.*

The one place in the psalm where God is contrasted with something is a contrast with a
watchman who might nod off.

*He gives His beloved sleep.* See the note on Psalms 127:1–2. The people can sleep precisely
because the sentry does not.

> **The verb the psalm is built on.** *Keep*, six times in eight verses, and God is the
> subject every time — over the foot, the head, the life, the going out and the coming in,
> now and forever.
