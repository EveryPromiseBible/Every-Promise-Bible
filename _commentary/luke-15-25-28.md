The parable does not end with the party. It ends in the yard.

## Where he is

*The older son was **out in the field**.*

Working, which is where he has been the whole time.

He has appeared once before in the story — in the opening line, *a certain man had two sons* —
and then not at all.

## What he hears first

*As he came back toward the house he heard **music and dancing**.*

The Greek is **sumphonias kai choron** — symphony and dancing.

He hears the celebration before he knows what it is for. Nobody came to tell him.

## The question

*He called **one of the servants** over and asked what all of it meant.*

He does not go in and ask his father. He stops a member of staff in the yard.

## The report

*"**Your brother has come home**," the servant told him, "and your father has **killed the
fattened calf**, because **he has him back safe and well**."*

The Greek is **hugiainonta** — in good health, sound.

The servant's summary is warm. Nothing in his phrasing is provocative.

## The reaction

*At that the older brother's **face hardened, and he would not go in**.*

The Greek is **orgisthe** — he was angry, enraged. And **ouk ethelen eiselthein** — he was not
willing to enter.

A door, a party, and a man on the wrong side of it by choice.

## Who comes out

*So **his father came outside to him and began pleading with him**.*

The Greek is **parekalei** — an imperfect. He kept entreating.

The father goes out twice in this parable. He ran down the road to the younger son. See the
note on Luke 15:17–20.

Now he leaves his own feast and goes out to the older one.

Two sons, two exits, and the father makes the first move both times.

## What the older son says

*"Look, **these many years I have been serving you; I never transgressed your commandment at
any time**. And yet **you never gave me a young goat**, that I might make merry with my
friends."*

*"But as soon as **this son of yours** came, who has devoured your livelihood with harlots, you
killed the fatted calf for him."* See the note on Luke 15:29–32.

He calls the years *serving* — the Greek is **douleuo**, the slave verb. And he will not say
*my brother*.

## The answer

*"**Son, you are always with me, and all that I have is yours**."*

The Greek for *son* here is **teknon** — child. An affectionate word, not the formal one.

*All that I have is yours* — legally true. The younger son took his share and spent it; what
remains belongs to the elder.

He has been standing in a field that is his, complaining that he was never given a goat.

## What is never said

Whether he goes in.

Luke stops the parable with the father outside, still pleading, and the door open.

## Who was listening

The Pharisees and scribes who had just complained *this man receives sinners and eats with
them*. See the note on Luke 15:1–2.

The ending is left open because the answer is theirs to give.

## The one who never left

*"These many years I have been serving you."*

Two sons, and both are outside the house at some point. One goes to a far country; the other
is in the yard, at the party, refusing to come in.

> **What the last scene is.** Not a reunion. A father standing outside his own celebration
> asking his elder son to come in — and Luke does not record an answer.
