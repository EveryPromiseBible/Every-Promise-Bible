Practical advice about standing in front of somebody who can do whatever he likes.

## The instruction

*Keep the king's command, I say, **for you swore it before God**.*

The Hebrew is *because of the oath of God*.

Loyalty to the throne is grounded in an oath sworn in God's name, which makes it a
matter between the subject and God rather than only between the subject and the king.

## The court manners

*Do not **rush to leave his presence**, and do not **take up a bad cause**.*

Leaving abruptly was an insult; a Persian or Egyptian court had elaborate rules about
withdrawal.

**A bad cause** — the Hebrew is *an evil word*, or *an evil matter*. Do not attach
yourself to a losing case in front of a man who cannot be appealed against.

## The reason

*For **the king does as he pleases**.*

Stated without approval or complaint. Ecclesiastes is describing how it is.

## The sentence

*His **word carries power**.*

The Hebrew is *the word of a king is authority* — **shilton**, dominion, mastery, the
right to act.

A king's speech is not a request or an opinion. It is the thing itself: the word and the
event are the same object.

## The question nobody asks

*And **no one can say to him, "What are you doing?"**"*

The same clause is used of God in Job — *who can say to Him, "What are You doing?"* — and
in Daniel, of the Most High: *no one can restrain His hand or say to Him, "What have You
done?"*

Ecclesiastes applies to a human throne the phrase reserved elsewhere for the throne
above it.

## Who is writing

*The words of the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem.*

A king, writing about what it is like to stand in front of a king. He knew both sides of
the audience chamber.

## Where the book goes next

Immediately after: *he who keeps his command will experience nothing harmful, and a wise
man's heart discerns both time and judgment.*

And then, four verses on: *no one has power over the spirit to retain the spirit, and no
one has power in the day of death.*

The same word for power. The king's word rules everything except that.

## What the New Testament says about the readers

*To Him who loved us and washed us from our sins in His own blood, **and has made us
kings and priests** to His God and Father.* See the note on Revelation 1:4–6.

## The other king in the book

*Blessed are you, O land, when your king is the son of nobles, and your princes feast at
the proper time — for strength and not for drunkenness.*

Ecclesiastes has opinions about what makes a throne worth serving. It just does not
pretend that a bad one has less authority.

> **What the verse is about.** Not obedience for its own sake. A word spoken from a
> throne is an act — and Ecclesiastes borrows the language it uses of God to say so.
