A father's appeal, and then the longest description of drunkenness in the Bible.

## The request

*My son, **give me your heart**, and let your eyes delight in my ways.*

The Hebrew is **tenah beni libbecha li** — give, my son, your heart to me.

Not obedience. Not attention. The heart.

## What the book has been doing

Twenty-two chapters of instruction — on money, work, speech, friendship, adultery, laziness,
kings and fools.

And here the father stops and asks for the one thing the instruction cannot compel.

## The eyes

*And let **your eyes delight in my ways**.*

The Hebrew is **tirtzenah** — take pleasure in, be pleased with.

Heart and eyes, in that order. What a man looks at follows what he loves.

## The first warning

*For **a harlot is a deep pit**, and a strange woman is **a narrow well**.*

Two images of the same thing: something you fall into and cannot climb out of.

*She lies in wait like a robber.*

## The riddle

*Who has **woe**? Who has **sorrow**? Who has **quarrels and complaints**? Who has **needless wounds and
bloodshot eyes**?*

Six questions in a row, with no subject named.

The Hebrew opens with **oy** and **avoy** — two interjections of lament, and the second occurs
only here.

## The answer

*Those who **linger long over wine**, who go to **sample bowl after bowl**.*

The Hebrew is **hamachar'im al-hayyayin** — those who tarry over wine.

## The instruction

*Do not gaze at wine **when it is red, when it sparkles in the cup and slides down smoothly**.*

Three attractions: colour, sparkle, and how easily it goes down.

The proverb concedes every one of them. It does not pretend the thing is unappealing.

## The turn

*In the end it **bites like a serpent and stings like a viper**.*

The Hebrew is **acharito** — its end, its afterwards.

The word Ecclesiastes uses: *the end of a thing is better than its beginning*. See the note on
Ecclesiastes 7:8–10.

## The effects

*Your eyes will see strange things, and **your heart will speak crooked words**.*

The heart again. The chapter opened by asking for it and now describes what happens to it.

## The image

*You will be like **one asleep out on the high seas, lying atop the swaying mast**.*

A man asleep in the crow's nest in a swell.

The most vivid picture of intoxication in ancient literature, and it is about the loss of any
fixed point.

## The self-report

*"**They struck me, but I felt nothing; they beat me, but I did not know it**."*

The insensibility is stated by the man himself, which is how the proverb makes its point about
what has been lost.

## The last line

*"**When will I wake, so I can find another drink?**"*

The Hebrew is **osiph avaqshennu od** — I will seek it again.

The chapter ends there. No verdict, no summary.

The last words in the mouth of the man who has just described being beaten without noticing are
about the next drink.

## What the appeal at the top was

*Give me your heart.*

The father asks for the heart, and then spends nine verses on what happens to a man who has given
it elsewhere.

## What the New Testament says

*And do not be drunk with wine, in which is dissipation; but **be filled with the Spirit**.* See the
note on Ephesians 5:18–21.

> **The structure.** *Give me your heart* — and then nine verses on a man whose heart *speaks
> crooked words*, ending with him asking when he can wake up and start again.
