Paul closes his earliest letter with a prayer and then answers it.

## The title

*And may **the God of peace Himself**.*

The Greek is **autos de ho theos tes eirenes** — and the *Himself* is emphatic and first.

Paul has just given them fifteen rapid instructions — rejoice always, pray without ceasing,
give thanks in everything, do not quench the Spirit, test all things.

Then he stops giving instructions and prays, and the first word is *Himself*.

## The name

**God of peace** — Paul's habitual sign-off. Romans, Philippians, 2 Corinthians, Hebrews all
have it near the end.

**Shalom** behind it: not the absence of trouble but the presence of wholeness, the state of
nothing being missing.

## The request

*Make you **holy, through and through**.*

The Greek is **hagiasai holoteleis** — sanctify you wholly. **Holoteles** occurs once in the
New Testament: complete to the end, whole all the way through.

## The three

*And may your whole **spirit and soul and body** be **kept without fault**.*

The only place in Scripture the three are listed together in this order.

**Pneuma**, **psuche**, **soma**.

Hebrews has the sword *piercing even to the division of soul and spirit*, which implies a
seam. Paul names all three and asks for all three.

## The second adjective

*Your **whole** spirit and soul and body.*

The Greek here is **holokleron** — a different word from the one before it. Whole in every
part, with nothing lacking.

James uses it of the man who lets endurance finish its work: *perfect and complete, lacking
nothing*. And Peter uses it in Acts of the healed man at the temple gate — *this perfect
soundness*.

Paul asks for both kinds of wholeness in one sentence: complete to the end, and complete in
every part.

## The deadline

*At **the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ**.*

The Greek is **parousia** — the arrival, the royal visit.

The word occurs in every chapter of this letter. Every one of the five ends by pointing at
it.

## The verb

*Be **kept**.*

The Greek is **teretheie** — guarded, watched over, preserved.

Passive. The Thessalonians are not asked to do the keeping.

## The answer

*The One who **calls you is faithful**. **He will do it**.*

Three words in Greek: **hos kai poiesei** — who also will do it.

Paul makes a request, and then says who it is addressed to and what He is like, and the
consequence is that it will happen.

The calling is present tense — **ho kalon**, the one calling you. Not a past event he is
appealing to; an ongoing act.

## The same construction elsewhere

*Being confident of this very thing, that **He who has begun a good work in you will complete
it** until the day of Jesus Christ.* See the note on Philippians 1:3–6.

*God is faithful, by whom you were called into the fellowship of His Son.*

*If we are faithless, He remains faithful; He cannot deny Himself.*

## What the letter is

The earliest surviving Christian document — written to a church Paul had spent about three
weeks with before being run out of town.

He had been worried sick about them: *when I could no longer endure it, I sent to know your
faith*. Timothy came back with good news, and the letter is mostly relief.

## The last lines

*Brethren, pray for us. Greet all the brethren with a holy kiss. I charge you by the Lord
that this epistle be read to all the holy brethren.*

*The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with you. Amen.*

> **Who does the sanctifying.** Every verb in the prayer is passive or has God as its
> subject, and Paul answers his own prayer in the next sentence: *He will do it.*
