The only Gospel that opens by explaining its own method.

## The form

One sentence in Greek, running the whole four verses, in the most polished Greek in the New
Testament.

It is a formal literary preface — the kind of dedication a historian of the period put at the
front of a work. Josephus opens the same way.

Luke then switches, at verse 5, into Greek that reads like translated Hebrew.

## The others

*A good many people have set out to write down **the account of everything that has come true
among us**.*

The Greek is **polloi** — many. Luke says there were already several attempts.

He does not disparage them. He is one more.

## The word

**Peplerophoremenon** — fulfilled, brought to completion, fully accomplished.

Not *events that happened*. Things that came true, which is a claim about promises.

## The chain

*Telling it **just as it was handed to us** by those who were there from the very beginning — **the
ones who saw it with their own eyes** and then **spent their lives serving the message**.*

The Greek is **paredosan** — handed over, delivered. The verb behind *tradition*.

**Autoptai** — eyewitnesses. The word a Greek doctor used of what he had observed himself.

Luke was not one of them, and says so.

## His own qualification

*So it seemed right to me, **after going back over all of it carefully and following every thread
to its source**.*

The Greek is **parekolouthekoti anothen pasin akribos** — having followed everything closely from
the start, accurately.

**Akribos** — precisely, exactly. It is the vocabulary of investigation.

## What he did

Luke travelled with Paul. Acts contains the *we* passages, where the narrator joins the story.

He spent two years in Caesarea while Paul was imprisoned there, within reach of everybody who had
been present.

## The order

*To **write it out for you in order**.*

The Greek is **kathexes** — in sequence, in orderly succession.

## The reader

*Most excellent **Theophilus**.*

The Greek is **kratiste** — the form of address used for officials. Paul uses it of Felix and
Festus.

The name means *lover of God*, or *loved by God*, and it was a real name in the period. Luke
dedicates Acts to him too.

## The purpose

*So that you can **know with settled confidence** that **the things you were taught are solid and
true**.*

The Greek is **hina epignos… ten asphaleian** — that you may know the certainty.

**Asphaleia** — from *a-sphallo*, not-slipping. Firm footing.

## What that implies

Theophilus has already been taught. Luke is not evangelising him.

The Gospel is written for somebody who has heard it and wants to know whether it holds.

## What Luke's Gospel contains that no other does

The annunciation to Mary. The Magnificat, the Benedictus, the Nunc Dimittis. The shepherds. The
boy in the temple at twelve.

The good Samaritan, the prodigal son, the rich man and Lazarus, Zacchaeus, the road to Emmaus.

Almost half of it is found nowhere else.

## Who is in it

Women, repeatedly — Elizabeth, Mary, Anna, the widow of Nain, Mary Magdalene and Joanna and
Susanna who supported the work from their own means.

Shepherds. Samaritans. Tax collectors. A dying criminal.

The Gospel that opens with a formal address to a man of rank is the one most interested in people
who had none.

## What Paul calls him

*Luke the beloved physician greets you.*

> **The word.** **Asphaleia** — the state of not slipping. Written for a man who had already been
> taught, so he could find out whether it held.
