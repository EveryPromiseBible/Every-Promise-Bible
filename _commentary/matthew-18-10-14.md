The same story as Luke 15, told to different people, for a different reason.

## The setting

The disciples had asked *who then is greatest in the kingdom of heaven?*

*Then Jesus called a little child to Him, set him in the midst of them*, and said *unless you are
converted and become as little children, you will by no means enter*. See the note on
Matthew 18:1–5.

The whole chapter runs from that argument.

## The warning

*"**See that you never look down on one of these little ones**."*

The Greek is **me kataphronesete** — do not despise, do not think down on.

Not *do not harm*. Do not hold in low regard, which is what an argument about greatness produces
automatically.

## The reason

*"For I tell you, **in heaven their angels are always looking on the face of My Father in
heaven**."*

The Greek is **dia pantos blepousi** — continually see.

To see the king's face was the privilege of the highest courtiers. The claim is that whoever
represents these little ones has standing access.

## Who the little ones are

The chapter begins with a child. But *these little ones who believe in Me* is the phrase used two
verses earlier, which widens it to anyone small in the congregation's reckoning.

## The mission statement

*"For **the Son of Man came to save what was lost**."*

## The question

*"**What do you think?** If a man has a hundred sheep and one of them wanders off, **will he not
leave the ninety-nine on the hills** and go out looking for the one that strayed?"*

He asks them to answer it themselves.

## The word

**Planethe** — wanders, goes astray. The root of *planet*, the wandering star.

In Luke's version the sheep is simply lost. Here it wandered — which is the difference between
the two tellings, and it fits an audience of disciples arguing about rank.

## The arithmetic

Leaving ninety-nine on open hills to look for one is not sound shepherding.

The question is put as though the answer is obvious, and commercially it is not.

## The joy

*"And if he finds it, I tell you the truth, **he is happier over that one sheep than over the
ninety-nine that never wandered away**."*

The Greek is **chairei mallon** — rejoices more.

Not that the one matters more. That the finding produces more joy than the never-losing.

## The conclusion

*"In just the same way, **it is not the will of your Father in heaven that even one of these little
ones should be lost**."*

The Greek is **ouk estin thelema** — it is not the will.

## The two tellings

Luke tells it to Pharisees who complained *this Man receives sinners and eats with them*, and
the sheep is lost. See the note on Luke 15:3–7.

Matthew tells it to disciples arguing about greatness, and the sheep wandered off from inside the
flock.

Same story, two audiences, two edges.

## What follows here

*"Moreover **if your brother sins against you, go and tell him his fault between you and him
alone**."*

The procedure for restoring someone, then Peter asking *how often shall my brother sin against me
and I forgive him? Up to seven times?*

*"I do not say to you, up to seven times, but **up to seventy times seven**."* See the note on
Matthew 18:21–22.

And then the parable of the servant forgiven ten thousand talents.

## What the chapter is

One argument, start to finish. It opens with men wanting to know who ranks highest and ends with
a man thrown into prison for refusing to cancel a small debt.

The shepherd going after one sheep sits in the middle.

> **The comparison.** Not that the one is worth more than the ninety-nine. That finding produces
> more joy than never having lost.
