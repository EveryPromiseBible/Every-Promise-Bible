The chapter is about what does not last, and this is the line about what does.

## The verb

*And now these three **remain**.*

The Greek is **menei** — abides, stays, continues.

Singular verb with a plural subject, which is normal Greek but has been noticed for centuries. The
three are treated as one standing thing.

## What has just been said

*Love never fails. **But whether there are prophecies, they will fail; whether there are tongues,
they will cease; whether there is knowledge, it will vanish away**.* See the note on
1 Corinthians 13:8–10.

Three things named as temporary, immediately before three named as remaining.

## The reason given

*For we know in part and we prophesy in part. **But when that which is perfect has come, then that
which is in part will be done away**.*

The Greek is **to teleion** — the complete, the whole.

## The two pictures

*When I was a child, I spoke as a child… **but when I became a man, I put away childish things**.*

And *now we see **in a mirror, dimly**, but then face to face*. See the note on
1 Corinthians 13:11–12.

Corinth manufactured bronze mirrors. The image would have been local.

## The three

*Faith, hope, love.*

The Greek is **pistis, elpis, agapē**.

## The triad elsewhere

Paul uses it repeatedly: *your work of faith, labour of love, and patience of hope*, in what is
probably his earliest letter.

And *we have heard of your faith… the love which you have for all the saints, because of the hope
laid up for you in heaven*.

## The problem with the sentence

Faith and hope look temporary by definition.

*Hope that is seen is not hope; for why does one still hope for what he sees?*

And faith is *the evidence of things not seen*.

## What the verse says anyway

They remain.

Paul does not resolve the tension. He puts all three under **menei**.

## The comparison

*But **the greatest of these is love**.*

The Greek is **meizōn de toutōn hē agapē**.

## Why greatest

The chapter has already said why: love is the only one of the three that describes something God
does toward us as well as something we do.

God does not have faith or hope. *God is love.*

## The word

**Agapē** — the word Paul has spent the chapter defining by verbs, not adjectives.

*Love suffers long and is kind; love does not envy; love does not parade itself.* See the note on
1 Corinthians 13:4–7.

Fifteen verbs, and not one feeling among them.

## Where the chapter sits

Between two chapters about spiritual gifts.

*But earnestly desire the best gifts. **And yet I show you a more excellent way**.* See the note on
1 Corinthians 12:29–31.

And immediately after: *Pursue love, and desire spiritual gifts*. See the note on
1 Corinthians 14:1–5.

The chapter is not a digression. It is placed in the middle of the argument on purpose.

## The opening of it

*Though I speak with the tongues of men and of angels, but have not love, **I have become sounding
brass or a clanging cymbal**.* See the note on 1 Corinthians 13:1–3.

> **The odd survivors.** Faith and hope look temporary by definition — Paul says they remain anyway,
> and does not explain how.
