Four verbs with God as the subject, and the last one has no object.

## The time

*Years passed, and **the king of Egypt died**.*

Moses is eighty by the end of this stretch. Forty years in Pharaoh's house, forty in Midian keeping
another man's sheep.

The king whose death is reported here is the one he fled from.

## What did not change

*The Israelites **still groaned under their slavery**.*

A new Pharaoh, and the same bricks.

## The sound

*And they **cried out**, and their **cry for help rose up to God**.*

The Hebrew is **vayyiz'aqu** — they cried out. And **vatta'al shav'atam** — their cry went up.

## What is missing from it

No one is said to pray.

The text does not record a petition, a confession, or a request. It records a noise made by people
in pain, and says it went up.

## The first verb

*God **heard** their groaning.*

The Hebrew is **vayyishma Elohim et-na'aqatam**.

**Ne'aqah** — a groan. The word is used of the wounded and the dying.

## The second

*And He **remembered** His covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob.*

The Hebrew is **vayyizkor**.

## What that word does not mean

Not that He had forgotten.

**Zakar** in Hebrew is remembering that acts — the verb used when God *remembered Noah* and the
waters went down, and when He *remembered Rachel* and she conceived. It is the point at which
something long held turns into something done.

## Which covenant

The one cut in Genesis 15, where the man who asked *how can I know* was put to sleep and God walked
the aisle alone. See the note on Genesis 15:17–21.

And in that same night he was told: *know certainly that your descendants will be strangers in a
land that is not theirs… four hundred years*. See the note on Genesis 15:12–16.

The groaning in this verse is inside a period God had already named.

## The third

*God **looked on** the people of Israel.*

The Hebrew is **vayyar Elohim** — and God saw.

## The fourth

*And **God knew**.*

The Hebrew is **vayyeda Elohim**.

## The missing object

The sentence stops there.

Every English translation has to decide what He knew, and most supply something — *knew their
condition*, *took notice of them*, *was concerned about them*. The Hebrew supplies nothing.

**Yada** is the verb of intimate knowledge, not of information: the word used of Adam knowing Eve,
and of God saying to Israel *you only have I known of all the families of the earth*.

## The shape of it

Heard, remembered, saw, knew. Four verbs, each shorter than the last, and the last one left open.

## What happens in the next verse

*Now Moses was tending the flock of Jethro his father-in-law… and the Angel of the LORD appeared to
him in a flame of fire from the midst of a bush.* See the note on Exodus 3:1–3.

The chapter break is a modern addition. In the Hebrew, *God knew* and the burning bush are
consecutive sentences.

## What He says at the bush

*"**I have surely seen the oppression of My people** who are in Egypt, and **have heard their cry**…
**for I know their sorrows**."* See the note on Exodus 3:7–10.

The same four verbs, said back to Moses in the first person.

**Yada'ti et-mak'ovav** — I know his pains.

> **The open verb.** *And God knew.* The Hebrew gives it no object, and every translation has to
> put one in.
