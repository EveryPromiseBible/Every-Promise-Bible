The end of a long description, and the last line withdraws every qualification.

## What comes before

Seven verses of description, item by item: her eyes, her hair, her teeth, her lips, her
temples, her neck.

*Behold, you are fair, my love! Behold, you are fair!*

## The line

*Until the day **breathes cool** and the **shadows flee away**.*

The Hebrew is *until the day breathes* — **yaphuach hayyom**.

The evening breeze. In that climate the wind comes up as the heat drops, and the long shadows
stretch and vanish into general dark.

The same phrase appears in chapter 2, in her mouth: *until the day breaks and the shadows
flee away, turn, my beloved*.

## The destination

*I will go to the **mountain of myrrh**, to the **hill of frankincense**.*

Both are burial spices and both are temple spices.

Myrrh is in the anointing oil of Exodus 30 and in the burial of Jesus — *Nicodemus came,
bringing a mixture of myrrh and aloes, about a hundred pounds*.

Frankincense went on the grain offering and burned on the golden altar.

And both were brought to a house in Bethlehem by men from the east. See the note on
Matthew 2:1–2.

## The verdict

*You are **altogether beautiful**, my love.*

The Hebrew is **kullak yaphah** — all of you is fair.

## The clause

*And **there is no flaw in you**.*

The Hebrew is **mum ein bak** — there is no blemish in you.

**Mum** is the sacrificial word. It is the term for what disqualifies an animal from the
altar: *whatever has a **blemish** you shall not offer, for it shall not be acceptable on
your behalf*.

And the term for what disqualified a priest from serving.

## Who says it

He does. It is his assessment, not hers.

Hers, from chapter 1: *I am dark, but lovely… do not look upon me, because I am dark, because
the sun has tanned me. They made me the keeper of the vineyards, but my own vineyard I have
not kept.*

The two statements are in the same book about the same woman.

## Where the sentence goes

*That He might present her to Himself a glorious church, **not having spot or wrinkle or any
such thing**, but that she should be **holy and without blemish**.* See the note on
Ephesians 5:25–27.

The Greek is **amomos** — the Septuagint's word for **mum**. Paul is using the sacrificial
vocabulary too.

And Jude's closing line: *to Him who is able to keep you from stumbling, and to present you
**faultless** before the presence of His glory with exceeding joy*.

Same word again.

## Of the Lamb

*A lamb **without blemish and without spot**.* See the note on 1 Peter 1:14–16.

The verdict pronounced over her in the Song is the verdict required of the offering.

## What he calls her next

*Come with me from Lebanon, my spouse… **you have ravished my heart**, my sister, my spouse;
you have ravished my heart with one look of your eyes.*

The Hebrew for *ravished my heart* is a single verb built on the noun for heart — *you have
hearted me*.

One look.

## What the book never does

Record her improving.

The compliments do not track any progress. He says it at the beginning and says it again in
chapter 6 and chapter 7, and nothing in between accounts for it.

> **The word in the last clause.** *Mum* — the blemish that disqualifies an animal from the
> altar. He looks at her and says there is none, and she had said in chapter 1 that she was
> sunburnt and had not kept her own vineyard.
