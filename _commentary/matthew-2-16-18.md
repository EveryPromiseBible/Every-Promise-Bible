The part of the Christmas story that does not appear on cards, and Matthew does
not soften it.

## What happened

*Every boy two years old and under was put to death.*

Bethlehem was a small town; historians estimate the number of children killed in
the low dozens. That is not a comfort, and Matthew does not offer it as one.

Herod's record makes the account entirely credible — he executed his own sons and
had ordered notable men killed at his death so the country would mourn.

## The quotation

*A voice is heard in Ramah… Rachel crying for her children, and she will not be
comforted, because they are no more.*

Matthew reaches into Jeremiah 31, where Rachel — buried near Bethlehem, mother of
the northern tribes — is pictured weeping as her descendants are marched into
exile.

Note what Matthew does **not** do. He does not say the mothers should be
comforted. He quotes a verse in which comfort is explicitly refused.

## Where the quotation comes from

Read on in Jeremiah 31 and the next verse is God's answer: *restrain your voice
from weeping and your eyes from tears, for your work will be rewarded — they will
come back from the land of the enemy.*

And a few verses further, the same chapter contains the promise of the new
covenant — the law written on hearts.

Matthew quotes the grief and leaves his readers to know where it is going.

## Why it is in the Gospel at all

Because it happened, and because Matthew is telling the truth about the world
Jesus was born into. A king who kills children is part of the Christmas story.

> **The one detail of mercy.** Joseph is warned in a dream and takes the child to
> Egypt. Jesus survives — and Matthew, who has already had Him called out of
> Egypt like Israel, is showing a nation's story replayed in one family: down into
> Egypt, and out again.
