David is not king yet. He is a fugitive in a stronghold, and armed men keep
arriving — some from Saul's own tribe, which is exactly the problem.

## The question he asks them

*If you come to me in peace, to help me, then my heart will be knit to yours. But
if you come to betray me…*

He puts it to them straight, and leaves the judging to God rather than
interrogating them or turning them away. A hunted man cannot afford either
paranoia or naivety, and David does something in between: he names both
possibilities out loud and waits.

## The answer

The Spirit comes on Amasai, and what comes out is not a speech but a chant, three
beats of *peace*:

> Peace, peace to you, and peace to all who help you, for your God is your help.

He does not offer terms or negotiate a position. He declares whose side they are
on and blesses the man they are joining — and the blessing is grounded not in
David's prospects but in God being his help.

> **What David does with it.** He welcomed them and *made them leaders of his
> raiding bands* — command, straight away, to men who had been potential
> assassins ten minutes earlier. The chapter is a long list of people who came
> over to David before he had anything to give them, and the writer is keeping
> count on purpose.
