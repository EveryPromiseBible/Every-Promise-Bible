Three words in English, one in Greek, and it was a word people used every day.

## Tetelestai

The verb is **teleo** — to complete, to bring to its end. In the perfect tense:
finished, and the result stands.

It turns up on business documents of the period stamped across a bill: **paid in
full**. It was used of a task discharged, a debt settled, an obligation met.

Not *I am finished*. **It** is finished.

## What John says was finished

*Knowing that everything had now been brought to completion.*

He says it before the word is spoken, and he uses the same root. The completion
is stated as an accomplished fact, and the shout is Jesus announcing it rather
than discovering it.

## The hyssop

*They soaked a sponge in it, put it on a stalk of hyssop, and lifted it to His
mouth.*

Only John mentions hyssop, and it is a strange plant for the job — a small bushy
herb, not an obvious tool for reaching a man on a cross.

It is the plant Israel was told to use to brush the lamb's blood on the doorposts
at Passover, and the plant used in the cleansing rituals of Leviticus. John is
writing his Gospel around a Passover, and he puts hyssop at the mouth of the Lamb
of God.

## "Gave up His spirit"

The verb is active. John does not say He died; he says He handed it over — the
same claim Jesus made in chapter 10: *no one takes it from Me. I lay it down of
My own accord.*

> **What He was thirsty for.** John notes it was said *so that the Scripture would
> come true* — Psalm 69, in a psalm of suffering. The last thing recorded before
> the shout is a prophecy being deliberately completed. Even the thirst is part of
> what was being finished.
