Two pictures of the same arriving God, one verse apart, and they do not match.

## The commission

*Climb the high mountain, **you who bring good news to Zion**.*

The Hebrew is **mevasseret** — a feminine participle: *she who brings good news*.

The Septuagint translates it **euangelizomenos** — the word that becomes *gospel*.

## What she is told to do

*Lift your voice with strength… **Lift it, do not be afraid**.*

Twice: lift, lift. And then the reassurance, which implies there was a reason not to.

The audience is a defeated people. The message will sound implausible.

## The message

*Say to the cities of Judah, **Here is your God**.*

Two words in Hebrew: **hinneh Eloheichem**.

The whole content of the good news, in the passage's own account of it, is a pointing.

## Where the chapter starts

*Comfort, yes, comfort My people, says your God.*

*The voice of one crying in the wilderness: prepare the way of the LORD.* See the note on
Isaiah 40:1–2.

All four Gospels apply that verse to John the Baptist, which puts this chapter at the front of
every account of Jesus.

## The first picture

*Look, **the Lord GOD comes with power**, and **His arm rules for Him**.*

The Hebrew is **bechazaq** — with strength, and **zeroo** — His arm.

The arm is the standard image for God's fighting power: *with a mighty hand and an outstretched
arm*.

*His reward is with Him, His recompense goes before Him.*

A conqueror arriving with the spoils, in the manner of a returning army.

## The second picture

*He will **tend His flock like a shepherd**.*

One verse later.

The Hebrew is **yireh** — he will pasture, feed, shepherd.

## What He does with the arm

*He will **gather the lambs into His arms** and **carry them close against His heart**.*

The same word — **zeroo**, arm — that ruled in verse 10.

The arm that rules is the arm that carries lambs. Isaiah uses the noun twice in consecutive
verses and lets the two uses sit against each other.

**Cheiqo** is the last word: the bosom, the fold of the garment at the chest where a shepherd
carried a lamb too small to keep up.

## The last line

*And **the ones still nursing their young, those He will gently lead**.*

The Hebrew is **yenahel** — to lead to a watering place, to guide at a manageable pace.

The ewes with young cannot be driven. Jacob knew it: *if the men should drive them hard one day,
all the flock will die*.

The pace of the whole flock is set by the slowest members in it.

## What the shepherd says about Himself

*I am the good shepherd. **The good shepherd gives His life for the sheep**.* See the note on
John 10:11–15.

And *He calls His own sheep by name and leads them out*.

## Where the chapter ends

*He gives power to the weak… **they shall mount up with wings like eagles**, they shall run and
not be weary.* See the note on Isaiah 40:27–28.

The chapter that starts with comfort and ends with eagles has this in the middle: lambs carried,
and the nursing ewes led slowly.

> **The arm.** *His arm rules for Him*, and one verse later that same arm is what the lambs are
> gathered into. Isaiah uses the word twice and does not explain the join.
