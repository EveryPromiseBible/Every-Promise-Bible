A slave in a foreign house, with nobody watching and nothing to lose, gives a
reason nobody could have checked.

## What his position was

Seventeen when his brothers sold him. No family, no rights, no legal standing, and
no prospect of the story ever reaching Canaan.

He was, in every practical sense, unobserved.

## What he says

He gives three reasons and then a fourth, and the fourth is the one that matters.

- *my master gives no thought to anything in this house*
- *he has entrusted everything he owns to me*
- *he has held nothing back from me **except you, because you are his wife***

Then: *"**How then could I do such a wicked thing and sin against God?**"*

## Whose wife

He does not say *because I would be caught*, or *because it is against the law*, or
*because I would lose my position*.

He names two people who would be wronged — Potiphar, and God — and Potiphar is the
lesser of them.

The same instinct is in David's psalm after Bathsheba: *against You, You only, have
I sinned.* See the note on Psalms 51:3–4.

## "Day after day"

*Day after day she pressed him, but he would not listen; **he would not lie with
her or even be near her**.*

Two refusals. The second is the practical one — he stopped being in the room.

The Hebrew is *to be with her*. He removed the opportunity as well as declining
it.

## The day it came to a head

*One day he went into the house to do his work, and **none of the household servants
were inside**.*

The narrator supplies that detail. Empty house, no witnesses, nobody to report
anything.

*She caught him by his garment.*

## What he does

*But he **left the garment in her hand and fled outside**.*

He runs, and he lets go of the coat to do it.

It is the second coat he has lost. His brothers stripped him of the long robe and
dipped it in goat's blood to lie to his father with. Now a garment is left in a
woman's hand and used to lie about him again.

Both times a coat is the evidence in a false story.

## What it cost him

Prison. Immediately, without a hearing.

The chapter's last line: *the LORD was with Joseph and showed him mercy, and gave
him favour in the sight of the keeper of the prison.*

The same sentence that opened the chapter about Potiphar's house. Same clause,
worse address.

## What it gained

Nothing he could see for two more years. The cupbearer forgot him.

Then Pharaoh's dream, and a man who could be trusted with everything in Egypt
because he had already been trusted with everything in one house and not touched
the one thing withheld.

> **What he says at the end.** *You meant evil against me, but God meant it for
> good.* Said to the brothers, about the pit — but it covers the prison too. Nothing
> in these six verses looks like it is going anywhere good, and Genesis takes
> another eight chapters to show what it was for.
