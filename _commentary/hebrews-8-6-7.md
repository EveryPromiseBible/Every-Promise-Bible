Two verses, and the second is an argument from the existence of a second covenant.

## The comparison

*The work He has been given is **finer by far**.*

The Greek is **diaphoroteras leitourgias** — a more excellent ministry.

**Leitourgia** is the priestly service word, and it is what the word *liturgy* comes from.

## The ratio

*Exactly as much finer as **the covenant He stands in the middle of is better than the old
one**.*

The Greek is **kreittonos diathekes mesites** — mediator of a better covenant.

**Kreitton** — better. The letter's favourite word. It uses it thirteen times: a better hope, a
better covenant, better promises, better sacrifices, a better country, a better resurrection.

## The mediator

**Mesites** — a go-between, one who stands in the middle.

The word appears three times in Hebrews and always of Christ.

Moses was the mediator of the first: *it was appointed through angels by the hand of a
mediator*.

## The promises

*And that covenant was **set up on better promises**.*

The Greek is **nenomothetetai** — enacted, made law.

The promises come first and the covenant is legislated on them. Not the other way round.

Which is the point of the whole argument: the old covenant's promises were conditional on
performance. *If you diligently obey… then all these blessings shall come upon you.*

The new one's promises are the four in the Jeremiah quotation that follows: I will put My laws
in their minds, I will be their God, they shall all know Me, I will remember their sins no
more.

All four are *I will*.

## The argument

*If there had been **nothing wrong with the first one**, **no one would have gone looking for a
second**.*

The Greek is **ei amemptos** — if faultless.

The logic is simple and it is the letter's method throughout: the existence of the replacement
proves the inadequacy of the original.

Nobody drafts a second contract for a working one.

## Where the second is announced

The next verses quote Jeremiah 31 at length — the longest Old Testament quotation in the New
Testament. See the note on Hebrews 8:10–12.

*"Behold, the days are coming… when I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel."*

The announcement is in the Old Testament itself, which is the writer's strongest card. He is
not arguing against Scripture; he is quoting it.

## Where the fault lay

*Because **finding fault with them**, He says…*

The Greek grammar is ambiguous and can be read as finding fault with the covenant or with the
people.

The letter has already made the point elsewhere: *for the law made nothing perfect*, and *what
the law could not do in that it was weak through the flesh*.

The fault is in the material it was working with.

## The conclusion of the chapter

*In that He says, "**A new covenant**," **He has made the first obsolete**. Now what is becoming
obsolete and growing old is ready to vanish away.*

The Greek is **pepalaioken** — has made old.

Written, on most datings, within a few years of AD 70, when the temple and its whole apparatus
were destroyed.

## What the mediator does now

*Therefore He is also able to save to the uttermost those who come to God through Him, since He
always lives to make intercession for them.* See the note on
Hebrews 7:15–17.

> **The argument in one sentence.** Nobody looks for a second covenant if the first one works —
> and the search is recorded in the first covenant's own documents.
