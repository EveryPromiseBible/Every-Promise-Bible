The third seal, and it comes with prices.

## The horse

*There was **a black horse**, and its rider had **a set of scales in his hand**.*

The Greek is **zugon** — a yoke, or a balance beam.

Scales meant rationing. Leviticus uses the image for famine: *when I have cut off your supply of
bread, ten women shall bake your bread in one oven, and **they shall bring back your bread by
weight**, and you shall eat and not be satisfied*.

Bread sold by weight rather than by the loaf is a society in trouble.

## The voice

*And I heard a voice, **out from somewhere among the four creatures**.*

The voice comes from the middle of the throne area, not from the rider.

## The first price

*A **day's pay for one measure of wheat**.*

The Greek is **choinix sitou denariou** — a quart of wheat for a denarius.

A denarius was a labourer's daily wage — the figure in the parable of the vineyard workers.

A **choinix** was roughly a day's food for one man.

So: a full day's work buys one man's food for one day, and nothing else. No margin for a
family.

## The second

*A **day's pay for three measures of barley**.*

Barley was the cheaper grain, the poor man's bread. Three measures for the same money.

A man could feed three people on barley for a day's wage, or himself on wheat.

Those are the terms. Not starvation — inflation of roughly eight to twelve times normal prices,
which is what famine looked like in an ancient economy.

## The restriction

*And **the oil and the wine — leave those alone**.*

The Greek is **me adikeses** — do not harm, do not injure.

An explicit limit placed on the rider mid-sentence.

Oil and wine are the other two staples of that agriculture — *grain, new wine and oil* is the
standard triad. Olive and vine are deep-rooted perennials that survive a drought which kills a
grain crop.

Whether the clause means the luxuries are untouched while bread fails, or that the damage has a
boundary, is argued. The grammar is a prohibition either way.

## The pattern

Every rider in the chapter operates within a limit.

The first is given a crown. The second is given leave and a sword. This one is told what not to
touch. The fourth is given *a quarter of the earth*. See the note on
Revelation 6:7–8.

Nothing in the sequence is unbounded.

## Where it sits in the discourse

*"And there will be **famines**, pestilences, and earthquakes in various places."* See the note
on Matthew 24:4–8.

Third in the list there, and third here.

## Why famine follows war

The second rider took peace. Armies live off the land they cross, and a harvest lost in spring
is a famine by autumn.

The sequence is not arbitrary.

## What the psalm says about the other side

*I have been young, and now am old; yet I have not seen the righteous forsaken, nor his
descendants begging bread.*

And the manna, which came six days a week for forty years, and never priced. See the note on
Exodus 16:22–26.

## Who set the limit

The rider is given scales and a scarcity, and then a voice interrupts to fence it: *do not harm the
oil and the wine.*

Two things worth noticing about that voice. It comes *from among the four living creatures* — that
is, from the immediate surround of the throne. And it arrives as a restriction, in the middle of a
judgement, limiting what the rider may touch.

Nothing in this sequence is loose. Every seal in the chapter is opened by the same hand — *the Lamb
opened one of the seals* — so the famine does not begin until He breaks the wax, and it does not
extend past where He draws the line.

And of all the commodities that could have been protected, the two named are the pair a Samaritan
poured into a stranger's wounds by the side of a road: *he went to him and bandaged his wounds,
pouring on **oil and wine**.* Even here, the things that heal are put out of reach of the rider.

> **The clause in the middle.** *Do not harm the oil and the wine.* The rider is given scales
> and a mandate, and then told what he may not touch.
