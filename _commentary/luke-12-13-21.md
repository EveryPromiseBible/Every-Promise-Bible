A man interrupts a sermon with a legal complaint and gets a story instead.

## The request

*"Teacher, **tell my brother to split the family inheritance with me**."*

A rabbi was a normal arbiter for this kind of dispute, and the request is not absurd.

Under the law the elder brother took a double portion. The man may well have had a case.

## The refusal

*"**Friend, who put Me in charge of settling your affairs?**"*

The Greek is *man, who made Me a judge or an arbitrator over you?*

He declines the jurisdiction. It is one of the few times He simply refuses a request.

## The diagnosis

*"**Be on your guard against greed**."*

The Greek is **pleonexia** — the desire for more, from **pleon**, more.

He is speaking to a man with a legitimate grievance about property, and names what is
underneath it.

## The sentence

*"**A person's life is not made out of the things he owns**."*

The Greek is *not in the abundance of the things he possesses*.

## The story

*"There was a rich man **whose land produced a wonderful crop**."*

He has done nothing wrong. The land produced; he did not defraud anybody.

## The soliloquy

*"And **he thought it over and said to himself**, 'What am I going to do?'"*

Count the pronouns. In four sentences he says *I* six times and *my* five times.

Nobody else appears in his deliberation. He has no advisers, no family, no poor at the
gate — he is having a conversation with himself.

## The plan

*"I will **pull down my barns and build bigger ones**, and store all my grain and all my
goods in them."*

It is prudent. Joseph did the same thing on a national scale and was made second in Egypt.
See the note on Genesis 41:25–32.

The difference is what the storage is for.

## The projection

*"'You have **plenty laid up for years to come**. **Take life easy. Eat, drink, enjoy
yourself**.'"*

Ecclesiastes says something similar and grounds it elsewhere: *eat your bread with joy…
**for God has already approved what you do**.* See the note on Ecclesiastes 9:7–9.

The rich man has the same activity and a different footing.

## The interruption

*"But **God said to him, 'You fool'**."*

The Greek is **aphron** — senseless, without mind.

The only speaker in the parable besides the man himself.

## The night

*"**Tonight your life will be asked back from you**."*

The Greek is *they require your soul of you* — an impersonal plural, which is how the
language avoids naming God directly.

**Asked back.** The life is described as on loan.

## The question

*"And **all these things you have arranged so carefully — whose will they be?**"*

The one thing his planning could not settle.

## The moral

*"That is how it is with anyone who **piles up treasure for himself** and is **not rich toward
God**."*

Two clauses, and the second names what was missing.

## What follows immediately

*"Therefore I say to you, **do not worry about your life**, what you will eat; nor about the
body, what you will put on."*

The whole passage about ravens and lilies. See the note on Matthew 6:25–34.

He answers the man who has too much and then turns to the people with too little, in the
same breath.

And: *"**Sell what you have and give alms**; provide yourselves money bags which do not grow
old, a treasure in the heavens that does not fail."*

> **What the rich man does wrong.** Nothing, in the farming. The parable's whole weight is
> in a conversation he has with himself, in which nobody else is present — and God's first
> word to him is the only one from outside it.
