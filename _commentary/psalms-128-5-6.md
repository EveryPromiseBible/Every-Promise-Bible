The last two verses widen from a table to a nation.

## Where the blessing comes from

*May the LORD **bless you from Zion**.*

The household blessing is routed through the city.

This is a pilgrim song — one of the fifteen Songs of Ascents, sung on the road up. See the
note on Psalms 128:3–4.

The people singing it are walking toward the place the blessing is said to come out of.

## The city

*May you see **Jerusalem prosper all the days of your life**.*

The Hebrew is *see the good of Jerusalem*.

A man's own prosperity is not mentioned. He is wished a long life in which the city does
well.

The two are tied: nobody's household thrives in a country under siege.

## The generations

*May you see **your children's children**.*

The Hebrew is **banim levaneikha** — sons to your sons.

The longest view in the psalm, and the least arrangeable thing in it. A man can work a field.
He cannot organise living long enough to meet his grandchildren.

Jacob got it, and said so: *I had not thought to see your face; but in fact, **God has also
shown me your offspring***.

And Joseph: *he saw Ephraim's children to the third generation*. See the note on
Genesis 50:22–26.

## The last line

*Peace be upon Israel.*

The Hebrew is **shalom al-Yisrael**.

The psalm zooms out at the final word: one man, one table, one city, one nation.

## The same ending elsewhere

Psalm 125 closes with the identical line: *peace be upon Israel*.

And the priestly blessing, which the whole psalm is an expansion of: *the LORD bless you and
keep you; the LORD make His face shine upon you… and **give you peace**.*

## What the fifteen songs are

Psalms 120 to 134. Short, memorable, and about the journey and what is at either end of it.

*I was glad when they said to me, "Let us go into the house of the LORD."*

*I will lift up my eyes to the hills — from whence comes my help?*

*Behold, how good and how pleasant it is for brethren to dwell together in unity.* See the
note on Psalms 133:1.

*Unless the LORD builds the house, they labour in vain who build it.*

## What is being asked for

Read as a list: God's blessing from His own city, a long life, a prospering country, and
grandchildren.

Nothing about wealth or position. The whole psalm's picture of a blessed man is a full table
and a peaceful nation, and he does not arrange either.

## Where the psalm started

*Blessed is every one who fears the LORD, who walks in His ways.*

And in the middle: *behold, thus shall the man be blessed who fears the LORD*.

Said at the beginning and again at the halfway point, and then these two verses spell out
what it comes to.

> **The last thing asked for.** Grandchildren — the one item on the list that no amount of
> work secures, and the psalm ends by widening past it to the whole country.
