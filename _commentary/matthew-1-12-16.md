Thirty-nine times in this chapter Matthew writes the same formula. On the
fortieth he changes it, and the change is the point of the whole genealogy.

## The pattern

*Shealtiel was the father of Zerubbabel. Zerubbabel was the father of Abiud.
Abiud was the father of Eliakim…*

Down through the list, one word doing the work each time — the ordinary verb for
begetting.

## The break

Then:

> *Jacob was the father of Joseph, **the husband of Mary**, and it was from Mary
> that Jesus was born, the One who is called the Messiah.*

Joseph is not said to be the father of Jesus. He is identified by his marriage,
and the birth is attributed to Mary. In Greek the relative pronoun before *from
whom* is feminine singular — it can only refer to her.

Matthew has just spent forty-one verses establishing a legal line and then, at
the last link, declines to say the thing the whole form was built to say.

## Why the legal line still matters

Jesus needed a claim to David's throne, and in that culture it came through the
legal father. Joseph, descended from David through Solomon and the kings, gives
Him that claim by adoption.

What Joseph does not give Him is blood — which is exactly as well, because that
line runs through Jeconiah, and Jeremiah had declared that none of Jeconiah's
descendants would ever sit on David's throne. See the note on Jeremiah 22:28–30.

The title comes down the legal line. The life does not.

> **Who else is in the list.** Four women are named before Mary, and Matthew did
> not have to name any of them: Tamar, who posed as a prostitute; Rahab, who was
> one; Ruth, a Moabite; and *the wife of Uriah*, whom he pointedly does not call
> Bathsheba. A genealogy designed to impress would have left all four out. This
> one puts them in.
