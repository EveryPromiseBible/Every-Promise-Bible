Almost everyone reads this backwards, and the grammar is what gives it away.

## The verb tense nobody points out

*For sin shall not be your master, because you are not under law but under
grace.*

In Greek that is a **future indicative** — a plain statement of what will be the
case. It is not an imperative. Paul is not commanding *do not let sin master
you*; he already gave that instruction two verses earlier, and he used a
different form to do it.

So the sentence is not a rule with a penalty. It is a promise, and it comes with
a reason attached.

## The reason is the whole point

Look at what he gives as the ground of the promise:

- not *because you are trying hard*
- not *because you have dealt with that sin*
- **because you are not under law but under grace**

Which means Paul is claiming something most people find hard to believe: being
under grace is what breaks sin's power, and being under law is what leaves it
in place. He says it plainly enough that the next verse has him answering the
obvious objection — *shall we sin because we are under grace?*

That objection only makes sense if he really did say what he seems to have said.
Nobody raises it against a preacher of rules.

---

## The word for "master"

*Kurieuo* — the verb from *kurios*, lord. To have lordship over, to be the one
whose word settles things. Paul is not talking about whether a believer ever
sins. He is talking about who is in charge.

That distinction matters when the verse is read by someone who has just failed.
The sentence does not say sin will never touch you. It says sin will not be the
one giving orders — and it locates the reason for that entirely outside their
performance.

## What it asks of the reader

The instruction in the two verses before this one is worth reading in the same
breath: *hand yourselves over to God as people brought from death to life.*

Not *become* people brought from death to life. As people who already are.
Everything Paul asks here is asked on the basis of something he says has already
happened, and the promise at the end is the reason he can ask it at all.

> **On "under grace."** It is a position, not a mood — the same construction as
> *under law*. Paul is describing which of two authorities a person lives under,
> and the entire argument of chapters 5 to 8 turns on the claim that a Christian
> has changed jurisdictions. That is why the promise can be stated as fact.
