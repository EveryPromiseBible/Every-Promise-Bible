A new king asks the same question twice and gets two answers. The kingdom splits
over which one he takes.

## The first answer

*If you will be a servant to them today — if you answer them kindly — they will
be your servants forever.*

The old men who had stood before Solomon give him a piece of counsel that is
almost a paradox: serve them today and they are yours for life.

It costs one conversation. The return they promise is permanent.

## The second answer

*My little finger is thicker than my father's waist… My father disciplined you
with whips? I'll use whips with iron barbs.*

The men he grew up with tell him to escalate. Their advice is not strategy; it is
posture — a way of appearing strong to people who had asked for relief.

## What he was asked

Read what the people actually wanted. Solomon's building programme had been paid
for in forced labour, and the request was *lighten the load your father put on
us, and we will serve you.*

They were not rebelling. They were offering loyalty in exchange for relief. Both
sets of advisers understood that; they simply disagreed about whether to take the
offer.

## The cost

Rehoboam takes the harsher counsel and ten tribes walk away that day. The kingdom
never reunites. Every king after this, north and south, lives inside the
consequences of one afternoon's advice.

> **The detail about who he asked.** He went to the old men first — which was
> right — and then went and asked again. Getting counsel and taking counsel are
> two different acts, and a man who keeps asking until he hears what he wanted is
> not being advised. See the note on Proverbs 11:8–14 on many counsellors, and
> what the surrounding verses assume about their quality.
