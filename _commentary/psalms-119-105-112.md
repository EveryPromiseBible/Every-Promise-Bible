The best-known verse of the longest psalm, and the two halves describe two
different distances.

## Lamp and light

*Your word is a **lamp for the ground beneath my feet**, a **light laid down
along the path** I walk.*

An oil lamp of that period was small — a clay dish you carried in one hand or
tied at the ankle. It threw enough light for the next step and no further.

The second image widens it: light along the path. Near and far, in one verse.

Neither is floodlighting. You get the next step, and enough sense of the road to
keep going.

## What surrounds it

The psalm does not use the lamp verse as a comfortable saying. Read the eight
lines it belongs to:

- *I am worn thin, brought low almost to nothing*
- *my life is always cupped here in my hands* — an idiom for constant danger
- *the wicked set their snare along my way*

The lamp is being carried by a man in trouble, at night, with traps on the road.
That is the setting the verse belongs to.

## The oath

*I've sworn an oath, and I intend to keep it.*

He binds himself twice in the passage — the oath here, and at the end *I've bent
my heart to living out Your statutes, forever, faithful, all the way to the end.*

The Hebrew for *bent my heart* is about inclining or stretching something toward
a direction.

## The inheritance

*Your steady words I've taken as my heirloom, my heart's own gladness, treasure
without end.*

**Heirloom** is the word for an inherited portion — the family land that passed
down. A man with nothing else says the words are the estate he was left.

> **The whole psalm.** 176 verses, eight per Hebrew letter, and almost every one
> mentions God's word under one name or another — law, precepts, statutes,
> promises, judgments. It is the longest sustained meditation on Scripture inside
> Scripture, and it was written by somebody being hunted.
