Two verses in which the man who found nothing lasting says what does.

## The claim

*I know that **whatever God does endures forever**.*

The Hebrew is **le'olam** — forever, to the vanishing point.

Everything Qoheleth has examined so far is **hevel**, vapour. See the note on
Ecclesiastes 1:1–3.

Here he names an exception, and the word he uses for it is the opposite of vapour.

## What cannot be done to it

*Nothing can be **added** to it, and nothing **taken away**.*

The Hebrew is **lehosif… ligroa**.

The same pair used of the law — *you shall not add to the word which I command you, nor take from
it* — and at the end of Revelation, of the prophecy.

A completed work. Human contribution can neither improve nor damage it.

## The purpose

*God has made it so, **that people would stand in awe before Him**.*

The Hebrew is **sheyir'u milephanav** — that they may fear from before Him.

The permanence is deliberate and the intended effect is named. Qoheleth does not present it as
an interesting fact.

## The refrain

*Whatever is, **has already been**. Whatever will be, **already was**.*

The line from chapter 1, restated: *whatever has been is what will be… there is nothing new under
the sun*. See the note on Ecclesiastes 1:4–11.

He has said it before as a complaint. Here it sits beside a sentence about God's work enduring,
and the tone changes.

## The last clause

*And **God calls back what has slipped away**.*

The Hebrew is **veha'Elohim yevaqqesh et-nirdaph** — and God seeks what is pursued, or driven
away.

**Nirdaph** is a passive participle of **radaph**, to pursue, chase, hunt. What has been chased
off, what has fled, what is gone.

## What the clause is doing

It is the most difficult line in the book, and translations divide over it.

Read one way, it means God requires an account of what has passed. Read another, and it is what
the Illumination has: what has slipped past is fetched back.

Both readings agree on the grammar's main point — that the vanished is not out of reach.

## Why it matters here

Everything Qoheleth has complained about is loss.

*No one remembers the people who lived before.* The eye never full, the sea never full, the
generations walking off the stage.

And then: God seeks what has been driven away.

## Where this shows up as a person

*"What man of you, having a hundred sheep, if he loses one of them, **does not go after the one
which is lost until he finds it?**"* See the note on Luke 15:3–7.

The Greek verb is the same idea Qoheleth's Hebrew carries. Going after what is gone.

*"For **the Son of Man has come to seek and to save that which was lost**."*

## What the chapter is famous for

*To everything there is a season, a time for every purpose under heaven.*

*He has made everything beautiful in its time. Also **He has put eternity in their hearts***. See
the note on Ecclesiastes 3:9–15.

The Hebrew is **ha'olam** — the same word as *forever* in verse 14.

The thing God's work is, He has put inside them. Which is why the vapour is unsatisfying.

> **The last clause.** **God seeks what is driven away.** The book's whole complaint is that
> things vanish and nobody goes after them — and this is the one line that says somebody does.
