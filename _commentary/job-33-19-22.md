Elihu describes a man at the end of himself, and the passage exists to set up what
comes next.

## What it looks like

*"An ache that never leaves his bones. Now **the sight of food turns his
stomach**; even the finest dish is nothing to him. His flesh wastes away until you
can hardly see it, and **the bones that were hidden now jut out**."*

It is a clinical description, and it is unsparing. Constant pain, no appetite,
visible wasting.

Whoever wrote this had watched somebody go through it.

## Where it is heading

*"His soul drifts toward the grave. His life inches toward **the messengers of
death**."*

The Hebrew is the destroyers, the ones who bring death. He is not dying quickly.
He is being carried there by degrees.

## The word the whole passage waits for

The next verse begins: *"**Yet even then**, if there is a messenger at his
side…"*

Everything in these four verses is the *before*. It is written to be the darkest
possible position, so that the *yet even then* has something to stand against.

## Which messenger

Notice the two in the passage, and that they are the same Hebrew word.

- verse 22 — the **messengers** of death, carrying him toward the pit
- verse 23 — the **messenger** at his side, one in a thousand, who speaks for him

The same noun, and they are pulling in opposite directions over one man on a bed.

## What that messenger does not do

He does not tell the man what he did wrong. He does not prescribe a remedy or take
a history.

He shows him God's righteousness, and God answers: *I have found the price for his
release.* See the note on Job 33:23–24.

## And then

*"Then his flesh grows fresh again, softer than a child's."*

From bones jutting out to skin like a child's, and the thing that stands between
the two descriptions is a ransom found by God. See the note on Job 33:25–28.

## The word the passage is waiting for

Two verses on, and it is the clearest sentence about Christ in the book:

*"**If there is a messenger for him, a mediator, one among a thousand, to show man His
uprightness**, then He is gracious to him, and says, '**Deliver him from going down to the
Pit; I have found a ransom**.'"*

## The three words

**Malak** — a messenger. **Melitz** — an interpreter, a go-between, one who argues a case.
**Kopher** — a ransom, the covering price.

**Kopher** is from the same root as the pitch on Noah's ark and the **kapporet**, the mercy
seat. See the note on Genesis 6:13–22.

*For there is one God and **one Mediator between God and men, the Man Christ Jesus, who gave
Himself a ransom for all**.*

**Paul**'s sentence has all three of Elihu's terms in it — mediator, man, ransom.

## Who finds it

*"**I have found a ransom.**"*

God says it, about Himself, in the oldest book in the Bible. The man on the bed contributes
nothing to the sentence; he is the object of it.

*In this is love, **not that we loved God, but that He loved us and sent His Son** to be the
propitiation for our sins.*

## What happens next

*His flesh shall be young like a child's; he shall return to the days of his youth.* See the
note on Job 33:25–28.

> **Why Elihu is describing this at all.** He is talking to a man scraping boils
> with a piece of broken pot. The three friends looked at Job and produced an
> accusation. Elihu looks at the same man and produces a mediator.
