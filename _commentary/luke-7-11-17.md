Two crowds meet at a town gate, one coming in and one carrying a body out.

## Nain

The name means pleasant. It sits in the Jezreel valley, in the territory of
Issachar — the tribe whose blessing was about seeing that *the resting place was
good and the land was pleasant*, and taking up a load anyway. See the note on
Genesis 49:14–15.

## Who she was

*The **only son** his mother had, and she was **a widow**.*

Luke gives the two facts that decide everything. No husband, and now no son. In
that world she has no income, no legal standing and no protection.

She is burying her boy and her own future in the same procession.

## Nobody asked

This is the point at which the story differs from almost every other healing in
the Gospels. There is no request.

She does not know who He is. She has not called out, touched a hem, sent friends,
or torn open a roof. She is walking behind a bier, crying.

*When the Lord saw her, **His heart went out to her**.*

The Greek is **splagchnizomai** — moved in the inward parts, the strongest word
the language had for compassion. Luke says the compassion is for **her**, not for
the boy.

## "Please, do not cry"

An impossible thing to say to a widow at her only son's funeral, unless you are
about to do something about it.

## The touch

*Then He **stepped up and laid His hand on the frame** they were carrying, and the
men bearing it **stood still**.*

Touching the bier made a man ceremonially unclean. He does it anyway, in public,
in front of a great many people from the town.

The bearers stop. Nobody has said anything yet.

## The word

*"**Young man, I say to you, get up.**"*

Five words to a corpse. Not a prayer — He does not ask God for anything. He speaks
to the boy.

*The dead man sat up and began to speak.*

## The handing over

*And Jesus **gave him back to his mother**.*

That is the last act, and it is the whole point of the story. He does not enlist
him, instruct him, or ask anything of either of them.

The boy is put back into his mother's hands, and Jesus walks on.

## What Elisha needed

The other raising in that valley is Elisha and the Shunammite's son, a few miles
away over the hill.

That one took a shut door, a prayer, lying over the boy mouth to mouth, getting
up and pacing the room, and stretching over him a second time. Then *call the
Shunammite*, and *take your son* — she had to come and get him.

Here: one sentence, and He carries the boy across Himself. See the note on
2 Kings 4:32–37.

> **What the crowd said.** *"A great prophet has risen among us! God has come to
> help His people!"* The Greek for *has come to help* is God visiting His people —
> the same word Zechariah used at the start of Luke, when *the Dayspring from on
> high has visited us.*
