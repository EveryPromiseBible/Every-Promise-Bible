The narrator tells you who it is in the first line, and then describes three men.

## The disclosure

*The LORD appeared to Abraham near the great trees of Mamre.*

Genesis says it before anything happens. The reader knows; Abraham does not.

Mamre is where he settled after Lot left, and where he built an altar. See the note on
Genesis 13:14–18.

## The hour

*While he sat at the entrance of his tent **in the heat of the day**.*

Noon, when nobody travels and nobody works.

He is doing the one sensible thing available: sitting in the shade of a doorway.

## The arrival

*Abraham **looked up and saw three men standing nearby**.*

The Hebrew is *and behold, three men were standing over against him*.

Standing, already there. No approach is described.

## The response

*He **hurried** from his tent to meet them and **bowed low to the ground**.*

He is ninety-nine years old, and three days out from circumcision — chapter 17 records it, and
chapter 18 opens immediately after.

Everything he does in this passage is at speed. The Hebrew has him running twice more in the
verses that follow.

## The address

*"**My lord**," he said, "if I have found favor in your eyes, **please don't pass by your
servant**."*

The Hebrew is **adonai** — which is a form used both of a human superior and of God, and the
manuscripts are divided on the pointing.

Whatever Abraham thought he was looking at, he treats it as somebody he does not want to lose.

## The offer, as stated

*"Let **a little water** be brought so you can wash your feet and rest under the tree. Let me
bring you **something to eat**, so you can be refreshed."*

The Hebrew is *a morsel of bread*.

## The offer, as delivered

Three seahs of fine meal, kneaded into cakes.

A tender calf, killed and dressed — which takes hours.

Butter and milk.

He offers a bite of bread and serves a banquet, and stands under the tree while they eat rather
than joining them.

## The Hebrew hospitality code

In that world a traveller's need was an obligation, and the desert made it a matter of life.
Job lists it as a defence: *the stranger did not lodge in the street, but I opened my doors to
the traveller*.

Abraham exceeds it, and the passage is careful to record the gap between what he promised and
what he produced.

## What Hebrews makes of it

*Do not forget to entertain strangers, for by so doing **some have unwittingly entertained
angels**.*

The Greek is **elathon** — they escaped notice, they did it without realising.

That is exactly the situation in this passage, and it is why the writer of Hebrews can assume
his readers will recognise it.

## What the visit is for

Two things.

*"I will surely return to you about this time next year, and **Sarah your wife will have a
son**."* See the note on Genesis 18:9–15.

And then the second: *shall I hide from Abraham what I am doing?* — and two of the three go on
toward Sodom while one stays. See the note on Genesis 18:27–33.

Announcement and disclosure, in one visit.

## Who arrives at the other house that evening

*The two angels arrived at Sodom in the evening, and **Lot was sitting at the city gate**.* See
the note on Genesis 19:1–3.

The same day, two men, two doorways. One sits at a tent flap in a field and one sits in the
gate of a city.

Both get up, both bow to the ground, both press the visitors to come in.

Genesis puts the two scenes back to back and does not comment.

> **The gap the passage records.** He offers a little water and a morsel of bread, and serves a
> calf, cakes and butter — and does not sit down.
