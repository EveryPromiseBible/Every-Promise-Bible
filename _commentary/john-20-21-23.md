The second *peace* in three verses, and this one has a job attached.

## The repetition

*Then Jesus said to them **again**, "Peace be with you."*

He has just said it. See the note on John 20:19–20.

The first was greeting. The second is the preface to a commission.

## The sending

*"**As the Father has sent Me, so I am sending you**."*

The Greek uses two different verbs: **apestalken** of the Father sending Him — a perfect,
sent and still sent — and **pempo** of Him sending them.

*As* is **kathos** — in the same manner.

The pattern of the sending is the incarnation. Into a place, at cost, to be with people.

John has been building the theme all through the Gospel: *the Father who sent Me* occurs more
than thirty times. And the prayer: *as You sent Me into the world, I also have sent them into
the world*.

## The breath

*And when He had said this, **He breathed on them**.*

The Greek is **enephusesen** — He breathed into, or on.

The word occurs once in the New Testament.

It occurs once in the Greek Old Testament at a moment worth noticing: *and the LORD God formed
man of the dust of the ground, and **breathed** into his nostrils the breath of life*. See the
note on Genesis 2:4–7.

And once in Ezekiel's valley of dry bones: *come from the four winds, O breath, and breathe on
these slain, that they may live*.

Three uses, and all three are about life arriving in something that did not have it.

## The word

*"**Receive the Holy Spirit**."*

The Greek is **labete pneuma hagion** — an aorist imperative. Take, receive.

**Pneuma** does double duty: breath, wind, spirit. The same ambiguity as the Hebrew **ruach**,
and as the conversation with Nicodemus: *the wind blows where it wishes*. See the note on
John 3:4–8.

## What this is not

Pentecost. That is fifty days away, and Luke has Him telling them to wait for it. See the note
on Acts 1:4–5.

John records something that happened in a locked room on the Sunday evening, and does not
harmonise it with Acts.

## The clause about sins

*"If you **forgive** anyone their sins, those sins **are forgiven** them; if you **hold** them,
they are **held**."*

The Greek verbs in the second half of each pair are perfect passives — *have been forgiven*,
*have been retained*.

The tense matters. It does not read as the disciples deciding and heaven following. It reads
as the disciples announcing what has been settled.

Which is what they do in Acts. Peter at Pentecost: *repent, and let every one of you be
baptised in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins*. And to Cornelius: *whoever
believes in Him will receive remission of sins*. See the note on Acts 10:34–43.

They never absolve anybody. They announce terms.

## The parallel promise

*"I will give you the keys of the kingdom of heaven, and whatever you bind on earth will be
bound in heaven."*

Said to Peter at Caesarea Philippi, with the same perfect-tense construction in the Greek.

## What they had been doing an hour earlier

Sitting behind a locked door because they were afraid.

He arrives, shows them the wounds, says peace twice, commissions them, breathes on them, and
gives them authority to announce forgiveness.

None of it is conditional on the last four days going differently.

> **The verb used three times in Scripture.** Breathed into. Once over dust in a garden, once
> over bones in a valley, and once in a locked room on a Sunday evening.
