One verse for a whole career, and it is the shortest entry in the book.

## The name

*After Ehud came **Shamgar son of Anath**.*

The name is not Hebrew. It looks Hurrian, and **Anath** is a Canaanite war goddess — which
means either a place name, Beth-Anath, or a foreign parentage.

Nothing else is known about him. No tribe, no town, no burial notice.

Judges gives every other judge a paragraph at minimum. He gets a sentence.

## The number

*He **struck down six hundred Philistines**.*

The same figure as Saul's entire army a few centuries later. See the note on
1 Samuel 13:19–23.

## The weapon

*With **an ox goad — a simple farmer's tool**.*

The Hebrew is **malmad habbaqar** — a teaching-instrument for cattle.

The root is **lamad**, to teach or train. The same root as **talmid**, a disciple, and
**Talmud**.

An ox goad is literally a *teacher of oxen*.

## What it was

A pole, six to eight feet, with an iron point at one end for prodding and a flat blade at the
other for scraping mud off the plough.

Long, heavy, and pointed. It is the best improvised weapon a farmer had.

## Where it turns up again

On the Philistine price list: *a third of a shekel… for fixing an ox-goad*. See the note on
1 Samuel 13:19–23.

The occupying power charging Israelite farmers to sharpen the implement one of them had killed
six hundred with.

## The verdict

*He too **rescued Israel**.*

The Hebrew is **hoshia** — saved, delivered. The verb behind **Yeshua**.

*He too* — the same phrase used of the judges before him. Judges gives him no less credit for
having no biography.

## Where he is mentioned again

*In the days of **Shamgar son of Anath**, in the days of Jael, the highways were deserted, and
the travellers walked along the byways.*

Deborah's song, and it dates him. Roads unsafe, villages emptied, and nobody moving in the
open.

That is the period he worked in.

## The company he keeps

Judges is full of the wrong weapon.

Ehud, left-handed, with a homemade two-edged dagger a cubit long, strapped where nobody frisked.

Jael, with a tent peg and a hammer.

Gideon, with three hundred men carrying trumpets, pitchers and torches, and no hands free for a
sword.

Samson, with the jawbone of a donkey. See the note on Judges 15:14–17.

And later, David, with five smooth stones.

## What they have in common

Not one of them is equipped.

The book of Judges is a catalogue of improvised objects, and its refrain is *the LORD raised up
a deliverer*.

## What Judges says about the period

*In those days there was no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in his own eyes.*

## The one line

Everything known about Shamgar fits in twenty-five words, and one of them is *saved*.

## What he had in his hand

An ox goad. A pole with a point on one end for driving cattle and a blade on the other for
scraping the plough.

Not a weapon. Farm equipment, and the only thing a man under Philistine occupation was allowed
to own — *there was no blacksmith found throughout all the land of Israel, for the Philistines
said, "Lest the Hebrews make swords or spears."*

*But **God has chosen the foolish things of the world to put to shame the wise**, and God has
chosen the weak things of the world to put to shame the things which are mighty… **that no
flesh should glory in His presence**.*

## Where the goad turns up again

*"Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting Me? **It is hard for you to kick against the goads**."*

Said on the Damascus road, in Hebrew, to a man on the ground — and the implement in the image
is this one.

The tool a farmer uses to keep an ox going in the right direction, in a sentence about a man
who had been fighting the direction he was being turned.

## What is said about him

One verse. No genealogy, no length of rule, no burial notice — Judges gives him a line and
moves on.

*And he also **delivered Israel**.*

> **What the tool is called in Hebrew.** A teacher of oxen — from the root behind *disciple*. It
> is on the Philistine price list two hundred years later.
