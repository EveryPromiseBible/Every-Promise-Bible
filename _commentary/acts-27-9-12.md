A ship's council, a prisoner's opinion, and a decision made the sensible way that
turns out to be wrong.

## Who was in the room

- **the pilot** — the professional navigator
- **the ship's owner** — the money
- **the centurion** — the authority, and the man who had to decide
- **Paul** — a prisoner in chains, with no standing whatsoever

Luke notes that the centurion listened to the first two rather than the last, and
that *most of them were in favour* of sailing. Expertise, ownership and the
majority all lined up on one side.

## Why the timing mattered

*Sailing had now become dangerous because the Fast was already over* — the Day of
Atonement, which puts this in late September or October. Ancient sailing in that
sea effectively shut down for the winter, and everyone aboard knew it. The
question was not whether it was risky but whether they could make one short hop
to a better harbour.

It was a reasonable call. It was also the wrong one.

## What he says later in the voyage

**Paul**'s advice about the timing is ignored, and fourteen days into the storm he stands up in the
hold and says why he is confident:

*"**For there stood by me this night an angel of the God to whom I belong and whom I serve, saying,
'Do not be afraid, Paul; you must be brought before Caesar; and indeed God has granted you all those
who sail with you.'**"*

*"**Therefore take heart, men, for I believe God that it will be just as it was told me.**"*

**Pisteuo to theo** — I believe God. It is the sentence said about Abraham in Genesis 15, and
**Paul** builds two letters on that verse.

## Whose the ship's company became

*"**God has granted you all those who sail with you.**"*

**Kecharistai soi** — from **charizomai**, to give as a favour. The root is **charis**, grace.

Two hundred and seventy-six sailors, soldiers and prisoners, given to one man as a gift, and not one
of them had asked for anything.

*He who did not spare His own Son, but delivered Him up for us all, how shall He not with Him also
**freely give** us all things?*

**Romans** 8:32's verb is the same one.

## And the meal in the storm

*And when he had said these things, he took bread and **gave thanks to God in the presence of them
all**; and when he had broken it he began to eat.*

The four verbs of the upper room, on a sinking ship, in front of a Roman guard.

> **What the passage does not do.** It never says God told Paul. It says *Paul
> warned them* and reports what he could see. Later in the chapter an angel does
> speak to him, and Luke says so plainly — he distinguishes the two. This part of
> the story is a man's judgement being right while the experts were wrong, and
> Luke lets that stand without dressing it up as prophecy.
