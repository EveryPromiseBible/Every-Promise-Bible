A ship's council, a prisoner's opinion, and a decision made the sensible way that
turns out to be wrong.

## Who was in the room

- **the pilot** — the professional navigator
- **the ship's owner** — the money
- **the centurion** — the authority, and the man who had to decide
- **Paul** — a prisoner in chains, with no standing whatsoever

Luke notes that the centurion listened to the first two rather than the last, and
that *most of them were in favour* of sailing. Expertise, ownership and the
majority all lined up on one side.

## Why the timing mattered

*Sailing had now become dangerous because the Fast was already over* — the Day of
Atonement, which puts this in late September or October. Ancient sailing in that
sea effectively shut down for the winter, and everyone aboard knew it. The
question was not whether it was risky but whether they could make one short hop
to a better harbour.

It was a reasonable call. It was also the wrong one.

> **What the passage does not do.** It never says God told Paul. It says *Paul
> warned them* and reports what he could see. Later in the chapter an angel does
> speak to him, and Luke says so plainly — he distinguishes the two. This part of
> the story is a man's judgement being right while the experts were wrong, and
> Luke lets that stand without dressing it up as prophecy.
