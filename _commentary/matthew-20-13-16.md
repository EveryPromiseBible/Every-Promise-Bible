The complaint is entirely reasonable, and the answer does not deny it.

## The parable

A landowner hires labourers at dawn for a denarius. He hires more at nine, at noon, at three, and
at five in the afternoon.

At the end of the day he pays the last first, and everyone gets a denarius.

*And when they had received it, they **complained against the landowner**, saying, "**These last men
have worked only one hour, and you made them equal to us** who have borne the burden and the heat
of the day."* See the note on Matthew 20:8–12.

## What is true about the complaint

Everything.

The men hired at five worked one hour. The men hired at dawn worked twelve. The rate was not the
same.

The parable does not pretend otherwise.

## The address

*"**Friend**, I have done you no wrong."*

The Greek is **hetaire** — comrade, fellow.

Matthew uses it three times. To the man at the wedding with no garment. Here. And in the garden:
*friend, why have you come?* — to Judas.

It is not a warm word in this Gospel.

## The contract

*"**Did you not agree with me on a denarius?**"*

He did. Verse 2 records it: *now when he had agreed with the labourers for a denarius a day*.

A denarius was the standard daily wage. The first men got exactly what they negotiated.

## The dismissal

*"**Take what belongs to you and go home**."*

The Greek is **aron to son kai hupage** — take up what is yours and go.

## The choice

*"**I choose to give this last man the same as I gave you**."*

The Greek is **thelo** — I will, I want.

No justification is offered. He does not explain that the last men were poorer, or had tried
harder to find work.

## The question of ownership

*"**Am I not free to do what I want with what is mine?**"*

The Greek is **ouk exestin moi ho thelo poiesai en tois emois** — is it not lawful for me to do
what I wish with my own things?

Which is the argument. The money was never the first men's to allocate.

## The second question

*"Or **is your eye bitter because I am generous?**"*

The Greek is **ho ophthalmos sou poneros estin hoti ego agathos eimi** — is your eye evil because
I am good?

**The evil eye** is a Semitic idiom for grudging, envious, stingy. Deuteronomy uses it of the man
who looks coldly at his poor brother. See the note on Deuteronomy 15:7–11.

Proverbs uses its opposite: *he who has a generous eye will be blessed*. See the note on
Proverbs 22:8–16.

## What the question locates

Not an injustice. A reaction.

Nothing was taken from the first men. Something was given to somebody else, and that is what
produced the complaint.

## The refrain

*"So **the last will be first, and the first last**."*

The same sentence closes chapter 19 and opens this parable. The story is framed by it on both
sides.

## What prompted it

Peter: *see, we have left all and followed You. Therefore what shall we have?*

And the rich young ruler before that, who went away sorrowful.

The parable is the answer to a question about what the early ones get.

## What comes immediately after

*"Behold, we are going up to Jerusalem, and the Son of Man will be betrayed… and they will
condemn Him to death."*

And then the mother of James and John, asking for the two seats.

*"Whoever desires to become great among you, let him be your servant."* See the note on
Matthew 20:24–28.

The men in the parable wanted proportionate reward. The next scene is two of the twelve asking
for the best places.

> **What the second question does.** It relocates the grievance. Nothing was taken from anyone —
> something was given to somebody else, and the idiom for that reaction is *an evil eye*.
