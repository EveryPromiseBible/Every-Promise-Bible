The oldest psalm in the book, and it ends by asking for something very small.

## Who wrote it

*A prayer of Moses the man of God.*

The only psalm attributed to him, and the oldest in the collection by several centuries.

## What has come before

*Lord, You have been our dwelling place in all generations.*

*For a thousand years in Your sight are like yesterday when it is past.*

*The days of our lives are seventy years; and if by reason of strength they are eighty years, yet
their boast is only labour and sorrow.* See the note on Psalms 90:7–10.

*So teach us to number our days, that we may gain a heart of wisdom.* See the note on
Psalms 90:11–12.

## The turn

*Turn back to us, LORD. **How long will You wait?**"*

The Hebrew is **shuvah YHWH ad-matai** — return, LORD; until when?

**Ad-matai** — the psalms' standing question. *How long, O LORD? Will You forget me forever?*

## The request

*Have pity on Your servants.*

The Hebrew is **hinnachem** — be moved to compassion, relent.

## The morning

*Fill us **in the morning** with Your love that never fails.*

The Hebrew is **sabbe'enu babboqer chasdecha** — satisfy us in the morning with Your **chesed**.

**Sava** — the same root as *full of years*, said over Abraham and David. See the note on
Genesis 25:7–10.

## The result

*That we may **sing and be glad all our days**.*

Morning, and then the whole day. The psalm asks for the filling at the start of it.

## The exchange

*Give us **joy for the days You afflicted us**, gladness **for the years we saw trouble**.*

The Hebrew is **kimot innitanu** — according to the days You afflicted us.

Not more. Matched.

## Who wrote that

A man who spent forty years leading a generation that was told it would die in the wilderness.

The affliction he is asking to be matched is one he watched, day by day, for four decades.

## The children

*Let **Your work be seen by Your servants**, Your **splendor by their children**.*

He is asking for two generations to see it — his own, and the next.

He himself sees the land from Nebo and does not enter it. See the note on
Deuteronomy 34:1–4.

## The last request

*Let **the kindness of the LORD our God rest on us**.*

The Hebrew is **no'am** — beauty, pleasantness, favour.

## The repetition

*Steady the work of our hands for us, **yes, steady the work of our hands**.*

The Hebrew is **uma'aseh yadeinu konenah aleinu uma'aseh yadeinu konenehu**.

He says it twice. The last clause of the psalm is the same clause as the one before it.

## What he is asking for

**Kun** — to establish, to make firm, to set up.

A man who has just said human life is seventy years, grass in the morning and cut down by
evening, and that our days *pass away in Your wrath*.

His last request is that something made by hand should hold.

## What the tent was

The tabernacle. Made by hand, out of donated jewellery, by named craftsmen, to be carried through
a desert and put up and taken down at every stage. See the note on
Exodus 36:4–7.

## Where the psalm sits

At the head of Book Four of the Psalter, immediately after Psalm 89 ends *how long, LORD? Will You
hide Yourself forever?* and asks what became of the covenant with David.

Book Four opens with Moses, and its answer is *the LORD reigns*.

> **The last line, said twice.** *Steady the work of our hands.* Asked by a man who has just
> described a lifetime as grass, and who would not enter the land himself.
