He rules out date-setting in the first sentence and then describes what the time
will feel like instead.

## Nobody knows

*"About that day and hour **no one knows** — not even the angels of heaven — **only
My Father**."*

The strongest statement in Scripture against working it out. Angels do not have it.

## The comparison

*"**As it was in the days of Noah**, so it will be at the coming of the Son of
Man."*

Of everything He could have reached for, He picks the flood — and He treats it as
history, not a story. His argument depends on it having happened.

## What the days of Noah looked like

*"People were **eating and drinking, marrying and being given in marriage**."*

That is the list. Four ordinary things, and not one of them is a sin.

He does not describe the violence Genesis reports, or the corruption, though both
were there. He describes normal life continuing.

The point of the comparison is not wickedness. It is unawareness.

## "They knew nothing about it"

*"**And they knew nothing about it** until the flood came and swept them all
away."*

The Greek is that they did not know, did not perceive.

Noah had been building for a very long time in plain sight, and 2 Peter calls him
*a preacher of righteousness*. The information was available.

## "Right up to the day Noah entered the ark"

The door stayed open through the whole of the building. It shut on one particular
day, and the LORD shut it. See the note on Genesis 7:11–16.

## One taken, one left

*"Two men will be in the field; **one will be taken and the other left**. Two women
will be grinding at the mill; one will be taken and the other left."*

Two pairs, doing identical work, side by side. Nothing visible separates them.

Readers have long divided over which is the better outcome — taken, or left. In the
Noah comparison just made, the ones *swept away* were taken by the flood and Noah
was left; in the Greek of verse 40 the word for *taken* is the one Jesus uses in
John 14 for *I will come again and receive you to Myself*.

The passage does not settle it, and He does not pause to.

## What He tells them to do about it

Not calculate. The next verse: *"Watch therefore, for you do not know what hour
your Lord is coming."*

And then three parables in a row about it — the householder and the thief, the
servant left in charge, the ten virgins with their lamps. All three are about being
ready rather than being informed.

> **What He says about His own knowledge.** Mark's version keeps a clause Matthew
> also has: *nor the Son, but only the Father.* He states the limit about Himself,
> out loud, and then goes straight on teaching about the day anyway.
