Two tribes look at good grazing land on the wrong side of the river and ask to
stop there.

## How the request is framed

Every part of it sounds reasonable:

- they genuinely have enormous herds
- Jazer and Gilead genuinely are good cattle country
- they ask politely — *if we have found favour with you*

Nothing in the sentence is a rebellion. It is a sensible business decision made
by men who could see what was in front of them.

## The one line that gives it away

*Do not make us cross the Jordan.*

The Jordan is the whole point. Everything from Egypt onward has been aimed at
crossing it — the promise to Abraham, the plagues, the sea, the forty years. The
land on the other side is the land God swore to give them.

They are not asking for less than they were promised because they were refused
more. They are asking to be excused from receiving it.

## What made it look wise

The herds were real, and the grass was real. The decision was driven entirely by
what suited the livestock — good land, immediately, without a river crossing or a
war.

That is what makes the chapter worth reading slowly. Nothing here is obviously
sinful. It is a preference for the visible good over the promised one.

> **Moses' first reaction.** He hears it as the same failure as Kadesh — *are your
> brothers to go to war while you sit here?* — and reminds them what happened
> when ten spies discouraged the people forty years earlier. He treats it as
> serious immediately, and the rest of the chapter is the negotiation. See the
> note on Numbers 32:20–27.
