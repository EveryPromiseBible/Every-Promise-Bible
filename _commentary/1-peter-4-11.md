Two activities, two rules, and both rules are about the source.

## Where it sits

*The end of all things is at hand; therefore be serious and watchful in your prayers.*

*And above all things have fervent love for one another… **be hospitable to one another without
grumbling**.*

*As each one has received a gift, minister it to one another, **as good stewards of the manifold
grace of God**.* See the note on 1 Peter 4:7.

Then this verse divides the gifts into two kinds.

## The first

*If you talk, **talk like a man handing on words that came from God**.*

The Greek is **hos logia Theou** — as oracles of God.

**Logion** — a divine utterance. The word Stephen uses of what Moses received at Sinai — *the
living oracles* — and Paul of what was entrusted to Israel: *to them were committed the oracles
of God*.

## What the rule requires

Not that the speaker be eloquent, or right about everything.

That what he says be handed on rather than generated. The image is of a courier, not an author.

## The second

*If you serve, **serve out of the strength God hands you**.*

The Greek is **ex ischuos hes choregei ho Theos** — from the strength which God supplies.

**Choregeo** is a rich word. In classical Athens the **choregos** was the wealthy citizen who paid
for a chorus to be trained, costumed and staged — funding the whole production out of his own
pocket.

The verb came to mean: to bear the full cost of.

## Why the two categories

Speaking and serving. Peter divides all Christian activity into words and work.

Both are given the same shape: the resource comes from somewhere else.

## The purpose

*That way **God gets the glory in all of it, through Jesus Christ**.*

The Greek is **hina en pasin doxazetai ho Theos**.

Which is the reason the sources are specified. If the words are handed on and the strength is
supplied, nobody in the transaction has anything to claim.

## The doxology

*Praise and power belong to Him, **always and always**. Amen.*

The Greek is **eis tous aionas ton aionon** — to the ages of the ages.

Peter breaks into doxology in the middle of the letter. He does it again at the very end.

## What comes immediately after

*Beloved, **do not think it strange concerning the fiery trial which is to try you**, as though some
strange thing happened to you.*

*But rejoice to the extent that you partake of Christ's sufferings.* See the note on
1 Peter 4:12.

The instruction on speaking and serving is followed straight into a paragraph about suffering.

## Who is writing

A fisherman who denied Him three times and was told *when you have returned to Me, strengthen
your brethren*. See the note on Luke 22:31–34.

The letter is written to believers scattered across five Roman provinces, under pressure.

## What Paul says about the same thing

*And **what do you have that you did not receive?** Now if you did indeed receive it, why do you
boast as if you had not received it?* See the note on 1 Corinthians 4:6–7.

## The other place the strength word appears

*Now may **He who supplies seed to the sower**… supply and multiply the seed you have sown.*

The same verb, **epichoregeo**, in Paul's chapter on giving. See the note on
2 Corinthians 9:8–9.

> **The word.** **Choregeo** — what the Athenian patron did who paid for the entire production.
> The strength to serve is described as somebody else's outlay.
