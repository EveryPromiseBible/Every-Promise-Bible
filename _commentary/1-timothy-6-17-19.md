Paul gives Timothy instructions for the wealthy people in his congregation, and
they are not the ones anybody expects.

## What is not said

They are not told to stop being rich, to divest, or to feel guilty.

They are also not congratulated.

## The two warnings

*Not to **carry themselves high**, and not to **set their hope on riches**.*

Pride, and misplaced confidence. Both are about the inside of a person rather than a
bank balance.

## The reason

*Since **there is nothing certain about riches**.*

The Greek is the uncertainty of wealth. Paul states it as a plain fact about the
asset class.

## What to hope in instead

*But on **the living God, who pours out on us everything we have and means us to enjoy
it**.*

The Greek is *who gives us richly all things to enjoy* — **eis apolausin**, for
enjoyment.

That clause is doing a lot of work. Paul, in a passage warning about money, says God
supplies things generously and intends them to be enjoyed.

There is no asceticism in the instruction.

## The four commands

*Tell them to **do good**, to be **rich in the kind of works that count**, to be
**openhanded** and **quick to share**.*

Rich in works — Paul uses the wealth vocabulary for something other than money in the
same sentence.

**Openhanded** and **ready to share** are two different Greek words, and both are
about willingness rather than amount.

## The investment

*In that way they will be **laying by a good foundation for the age that is coming**,
so that they may **take hold of the life that lasts**.*

The Greek is a banking metaphor — storing up, laying away treasure.

Jesus says it more sharply: *lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven.*

## Where it sits in the letter

Ten verses earlier is the famous one: *the love of money is a root of all kinds of
evil.*

Not money — the love of it. And Paul distinguishes carefully: *those who **desire to be
rich** fall into temptation and a snare.*

He addresses the ones who *desire to be rich* first, and then, separately, those who
already are. Two different groups, two different instructions.

## And the sentence in the middle

*Now godliness with **contentment** is great gain.*

The Greek is **autarkeia** — sufficiency, having enough. The same word Paul uses of
himself: *I have learned, in whatever state I am, to be content.*

> **What he says just before the contentment line.** *We brought nothing into this
> world, and it is certain we can carry nothing out.* The argument for contentment is
> logistical.
