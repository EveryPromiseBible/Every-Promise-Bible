The last two verses of the chapter, and they are the harvest of everything in it.

## The two exchanges

*Where the thornbush grew, the cypress will rise. Where the briar grew, the
myrtle will stand.*

Thorns and briars are the first curse. When the ground was cursed in Genesis 3,
what it produced was *thorns and thistles* — and they have stood for that ruin
ever since.

Cypress and myrtle are the opposite kind of plant: tall evergreen timber, and a
fragrant shrub used at festivals. Neither is a crop. Both are things you would
plant because they last and because they are good to be near.

The exchange is not thorn-for-wheat. It is thorn-for-something-permanent.

## Where they grow

*Where the thornbush grew.* The same ground. Nothing is relocated.

That is the specific promise: the site of the curse becomes the site of the
cypress.

## The going out

*You will go out in joy and be led home in peace. The mountains and the hills
will burst into song before you, and all the trees of the field will clap their
hands.*

Written to exiles, so *go out* means leaving Babylon. The landscape is described
as celebrating the road home.

## The last line

*This will be a name for the LORD, a sign that lasts forever and is never cut
off.*

The trees are a monument. What the exchange is *for* is a name — a permanent,
visible marker of who did it.

The Hebrew for *cut off* is the word used of felling a tree, which is a deliberate
touch in a sentence about trees.

> **Where the crown of thorns fits.** The Gospels record it without comment.
> Readers have long noticed that the first curse on the ground ends up pressed
> onto a head — and Isaiah, four chapters after his Servant is pierced, ends with
> thorn ground growing cypress.
