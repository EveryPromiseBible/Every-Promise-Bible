The oldest written account of the Lord's Supper anywhere — earlier than any of
the four Gospels — and Paul says where he got it.

## "I received from the Lord"

*For I **received from the Lord** what I also delivered to you.*

Paul was not in the upper room. He was not a disciple then, and he does not say
Peter told him.

He says the Lord did. The same man tells the Corinthians in his next letter about
being caught up to the third heaven and hearing words no one is permitted to
repeat. Whatever else Paul received, he received this — and he passed on this one.

## "Broken for you"

*"Take, eat: this is My body, **which is broken for you**."*

Neither Matthew nor Mark has this phrase. Luke has *given for you*. Paul has
**broken**, and he adds the two words that make it personal: *for you*.

It is the only place in the New Testament where the breaking of the bread is
stated as being for the eater's sake.

Isaiah 53 says *by His stripes we are healed* in the same breath as *He was
wounded for our transgressions*. Two provisions in one passage, and two elements
on one table.

## "In remembrance of Me"

Said twice — once over the bread, once over the cup. Repetition in Paul is never
accidental.

The Greek **anamnēsis** is stronger than a fond recollection; the Septuagint uses
it for the memorial portion of an offering, the part that brought a thing to
mind before God.

## "The new covenant in My blood"

Not a renewal of the old. A different covenant, and Jeremiah 31 had promised it:
*I will forgive their iniquity and remember their sin no more.*

The old covenant's terms were *if you obey*. The new one's terms are the blood it
is named after.

## "You proclaim the Lord's death until He comes"

*For as often as you eat this bread and drink this cup, you **proclaim** the
Lord's death.*

The Greek is **katangellō** — to announce publicly, the word used for preaching.
Eating is a form of announcement.

And the announcement is aimed at more than the people in the room. Something is
declared every time the bread is taken.

*Until He comes.* The instruction has an end date, which is also the only date in
it that has not yet arrived.

> **What the passage is answering.** Paul does not recite the institution as a
> liturgy lesson. He recites it because the Corinthians were shaming poor people
> at that table. The most quoted communion passage in Scripture was written as a
> correction. See the notes on 1 Corinthians 11:20–22 and 11:27–30.
