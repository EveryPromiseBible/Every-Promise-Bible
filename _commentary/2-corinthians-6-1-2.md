Paul makes one appeal, and it is not about behaviour.

## "Working alongside Him"

The Greek is **sunergountes** — working together with. Paul describes his own
ministry as a partnership.

The verse before ends chapter 5: *we are ambassadors for Christ, as though God were
pleading through us: **be reconciled to God**.*

## The appeal

*We ask you as well **not to take the grace of God and let it come to nothing in
you**.*

The Greek is *not to receive the grace of God in vain* — **kenos**, empty, hollow, to
no purpose.

It is possible to receive something and get nothing out of it. That is the danger
named, and it is the only thing Paul asks in this sentence.

Not *do not lose it*. Do not let it be empty.

## The quotation

*"**At the time of My favour I listened to you, and on the day of rescue I came to
your help.**"*

Isaiah 49, from a passage about the Servant — spoken to Him, about a moment when a
request would be heard.

## Paul's comment on it

*Look — **right now is the time of favour. Right now is the day of rescue.**"*

He takes a prophecy about a future acceptable time and says it has arrived.

The Greek word he uses is **kairos** — not clock time but the right moment, the
season when a thing is possible.

## The repetition

*Right now… right now.* Paul says it twice in one breath.

It is the same urgency Isaiah has at the end of the free invitation: *seek the LORD
**while He may be found**, call upon Him **while He is near**.* See the note on
Isaiah 55:1–3.

Both passages hold two things at once — the offer costs nothing, and the moment is
now.

## What follows

Paul then gives one of the great lists in his letters, of what the ministry cost:

*In much patience, in tribulations, in needs, in distresses, in stripes, in
imprisonments, in tumults, in labours, in sleeplessness, in fastings…*

And then the paradoxes: *as unknown, and yet well known; as dying, and behold we
live; as sorrowful, yet always rejoicing; **as poor, yet making many rich**; as having
nothing, and yet possessing all things.*

## What he asks at the end of it

*O Corinthians! **We have spoken openly to you, our heart is wide open**… Now in
return for the same — I speak as to children — **you also be open**.*

The whole passage is a man asking a difficult church to stop being closed toward
him.

> **What "in vain" would look like.** Not losing the grace. Standing in it and
> getting nothing from it — which is why the chapter's ask is about an open heart
> rather than better conduct.
