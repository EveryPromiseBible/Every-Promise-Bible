He describes what one look did, and the verb is violent.

## The line

*You have taken my heart, my sister, my bride, **you have taken my heart**.*

The Hebrew is **libbavtini** — a single word, a denominative verb from **lev**, heart.

Its sense is disputed: *you have hearted me*, meaning either *you have taken my heart* or *you
have given me heart*.

It occurs nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible.

## The repetition

He says it twice in one verse.

## The address

*My sister, my bride.*

The Hebrew is **achoti kallah**.

**Sister** was a term of endearment in Egyptian love poetry of the period, used between lovers.
It is not a family claim.

He uses the pair five times in this chapter and the next, and nowhere else.

## The cause

*With **one glance of your eyes**, with **one jewel of your necklace**.*

The Hebrew is **be'achat me'einayik** — with one of your eyes.

One eye. One bead of the necklace.

The smallest possible cause, stated twice, for the thing in the first line.

## The wine again

*How beautiful is your love, my sister, my bride, **how much better than wine**.*

She had said it about him in the first lines of the book: *your love is sweeter than wine*. See
the note on Song of Songs 1:2–4.

He returns her own comparison to her, in the same words.

## The perfume

*And **the scent of your perfume than any spice**.*

She had said his name was oil poured out. He says the same of hers.

The Song works by exchange. Almost every image one of them uses comes back from the other.

## The honey

*Your lips **drip honey from the comb**, my bride, **honey and milk are under your tongue**.*

The Hebrew is **nophet tittophnah** — the comb drips.

**Nophet** — the honey that drips from the comb before pressing. The same word in Psalm 19, of
the law: *sweeter than honey, than honey straight from the comb*. See the note on
Psalms 19:10–11.

## Honey and milk

**Devash vechalav** — the pair used of the land: *a land flowing with milk and honey*.

The promised land's own phrase, applied to what is under someone's tongue.

## The garments

*And the fragrance of your garments is **the fragrance of Lebanon**.*

Cedar. Lebanon's forests were the source of the timber for the temple and the palace, and the
smell was famous.

## What follows

*A garden enclosed is my sister, my bride, a spring shut up, a fountain sealed.*

And then her answer: *awake, O north wind, and come, O south! Blow upon my garden, that its
spices may flow out. **Let my beloved come to his garden and eat its pleasant fruits***.

He calls her an enclosed garden. She invites him into it.

## What his description does

Chapter 4 is one long description of her, from the eyes down, and the last line of it is *you are
all fair, my love, **and there is no spot in you***.

The Hebrew is **umum eyn bach** — and no blemish in you.

**Mum** — the word used of the sacrificial animal that must be without defect.

## Where Paul uses the same idea

*That He might present her to Himself a glorious church, **not having spot or wrinkle or any such
thing**, but that she should be holy and without blemish.* See the note on
Ephesians 5:25–27.

> **The cause.** One eye, one bead of a necklace. The smallest thing named twice, as the reason
> for a verb that occurs nowhere else in Hebrew.
