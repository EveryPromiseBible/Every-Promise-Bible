He proves he has the right, and then says he has never taken it.

## The argument so far

Who serves as a soldier at his own expense? Who plants a vineyard and does not eat its fruit? Who
tends a flock and does not drink the milk?

And the law: *do not muzzle an ox while it treads out the grain*. See the note on
1 Corinthians 9:7–10.

Three illustrations from ordinary work, and one from Deuteronomy.

## The exchange

*If we have **sown spiritual things** among you, is it too much if we **reap material things** from
you?*

The Greek is **ta pneumatika** and **ta sarkika** — the spiritual and the fleshly.

Not a criticism of the material. Paul is naming two categories and calling the trade
proportionate.

## The comparison

*If **others share this right over you**, do we not have it all the more?*

Somebody in Corinth had been supported. Paul does not name them.

He founded the church. The comparison is uncomfortable and he makes it anyway.

## The refusal

*Yet **we have not used this right**.*

The Greek is **ouk echresametha** — we did not make use of it.

Four verses establishing the entitlement, and then that.

## What he did instead

*Instead **we bear all things**.*

The Greek is **panta stegomen** — from **stego**, to cover, to hold off, to endure. The verb of a
roof keeping out rain.

Paul worked with his hands. *These hands have provided for my necessities, and for those who were
with me* — tent-making, in Corinth, in the house of Aquila and Priscilla.

## The reason

*So that **we may put no hindrance in the way of the good news of Christ**.*

The Greek is **enkopen** — a cutting into, an obstacle. The word for breaking up a road to stop
an army advancing.

Corinth was full of travelling teachers who charged. Paul refused to look like one of them.

## The temple argument

*Do you not know that **those who do the work of the temple eat from the temple**, and that **those
who serve at the altar share in what is offered at the altar**?*

The Levites had no land allotment. They lived on the tithes and the portions of the sacrifices —
the arrangement is set out in Numbers 18.

Everyone in the ancient world, Jew or Greek, understood that priests ate from the altar.

## The conclusion

*In the same way **the Lord has ordained that those who preach the good news should live from the
good news**.*

The Greek is **dietaxen** — commanded, arranged, ordered.

Paul attributes it to Jesus: *the labourer is worthy of his wages*, from the sending of the
seventy.

## The strength of the claim

He does not call it a courtesy or a kindness. He calls it an ordinance of the Lord.

And then does not take it.

## What he says next

*But I have used none of these things, nor have I written these things that it should be done so
to me. For **it would be better for me to die** than that anyone should make my boasting void.*

The Greek is **kauchema** — his ground of boasting, which is that he preaches free of charge.

## The principle underneath

*For **though I am free from all men, I have made myself a servant to all**, that I might win the
more.*

*I have become all things to all men, that I might by all means save some.* See the note on
1 Corinthians 9:19–23.

## What he accepted from elsewhere

*I robbed other churches, taking wages from them to minister to you.* And from the Philippians:
*even in Thessalonica you sent aid once and again for my necessities*.

He took support from churches he was not currently working in, and none from the one he was.

> **The shape.** Eight verses proving the right belongs to him — a soldier's pay, a farmer's
> fruit, the law of the ox, the altar, and a command of the Lord — and then: *we have not used it*.
