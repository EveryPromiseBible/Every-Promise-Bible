The first convert in Europe is a businesswoman at a prayer meeting by a river.

## Who she was

*A woman named **Lydia**, a **seller of purple cloth** from the city of Thyatira,
and she **worshipped God**.*

Purple dye was the most expensive commodity in the ancient world — extracted a drop
at a time from a shellfish, and reserved by law in some places for royalty. A
dealer in it was a wealthy woman.

She is from Thyatira in Asia Minor, and she is in Philippi, running a business
abroad.

## Why they were at a river

Philippi had no synagogue. Ten Jewish men were required to form one, and the city
evidently did not have them.

So Paul goes down to the riverside on the Sabbath *where prayer was customarily
made*, and finds a group of women.

The gospel enters Europe at an informal women's prayer meeting outside the city
gate.

## The sentence

*She was listening, and **the Lord opened her heart** to pay attention to what Paul
was saying.*

Two things at once, and Luke puts them side by side.

She was listening — that is her. The Lord opened her heart — that is Him.

The Greek for *opened* is the word used of opening a door, and elsewhere in Luke of
opening the Scriptures on the Emmaus road and of the disciples' eyes being opened.

Paul preached. Somebody else did the opening.

## Her household

*When **she and her household** had been baptised.*

Not just her. The Greek is her house — family, servants, dependents. The whole
establishment.

The same phrase turns up at the end of the same chapter, over the jailer: *believe
on the Lord Jesus Christ, and you will be saved — you and your household.* See the
note on Acts 16:29–31.

Two households in one chapter, at opposite ends of Philippi's social order: a
purple merchant and a prison officer.

## What she did with her house

*"**If you have judged me faithful** to the Lord, come into my house and stay." And
**she prevailed on us**.*

She makes it a question of whether they think she is genuine, which is not a
request anyone can easily refuse.

The Greek for *prevailed* is to compel, to force — the same verb the Emmaus two used
on Him.

## What her house became

The church at Philippi. Paul's warmest letter is addressed to it, and the last
lines of this chapter have him and Silas going back to *the house of Lydia* after
the earthquake, to encourage the brethren, before leaving the city.

> **Where the money went.** Philippi is the church that sent Paul support again and
> again — *even in Thessalonica you sent aid once and again for my necessities* —
> and the one he thanks in Philippians for a gift carried by Epaphroditus. The
> church that funded him started in a wealthy woman's front room.
