The proverb everyone knows from this passage is about the company you keep, and
the Hebrew behind it is more physical than the English.

## The verb is "walk"

Not *admire*, not *listen to*. **Halak** — to walk, to go along with, to travel
in someone's company. It is the ordinary word for how you spend your days.

The picture is not of picking mentors. It is of who is beside you when nothing
special is happening, and the proverb claims that wisdom transfers by proximity
rather than by instruction.

The second half is deliberately blunt: *keep fools for friends and come to harm*.
Same mechanism, opposite direction. Whatever you walk with, you become.

## The line before it

*A longing fulfilled is sweet to the soul. Fools would rather die than leave
their evil.*

Odd pairing, until you see it. The verse before speaks of a desire finally
satisfied; the verse after describes people who will not let go of what is
destroying them. Then: walk with the wise. The proverb about company is set
between something worth wanting and an example of wanting the wrong thing badly
enough to die of it.

> **On reading a proverb.** These are general truths, sharply put — not
> guarantees. The same chapter says the fields of the poor could yield plenty but
> injustice sweeps the harvest away, which is the book itself acknowledging that
> the pattern does not always hold. A proverb tells you which way to lean, not
> what is certain to happen.
