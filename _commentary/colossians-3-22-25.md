Paul is writing to household slaves, which is worth saying plainly rather than
softening into "servants." These were people with no legal standing and no
prospect of wages at all — and the sentence he lands on would have been startling
in that room.

## The phrase that does the work

*Knowing that the Lord Himself will hand you the inheritance as your wages.*

Two words are being deliberately jammed together. An **inheritance** is what a
son receives because of who he is. **Wages** are what a worker earns. A slave
could expect neither.

Paul says they will be handed the first *as* the second. The thing they could
never earn is given to them in the form of the thing they never got.

## "Work at it from the soul"

The Greek is literally *out of the soul*, and it is set against work that only
happens while someone is watching — the phrase behind it means eye-service, work
done for the eye.

The instruction is not to work harder. It is to change who the work is aimed at:
*as for the Lord and not for people*. That transfer is what makes a hidden task
and a visible one worth the same.

> **The line at the end.** *There is no playing of favorites* — and the very next
> verse turns to the masters and tells them they have a Master in heaven. In a
> world that ran on rank, Paul puts both parties under the same eye and tells the
> ones on the bottom that their unwitnessed work is seen.
