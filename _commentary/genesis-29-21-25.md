Four words in this passage do more work than the rest of the chapter, and in
Hebrew they land as bluntly as they do here: *when morning came — there was
Leah.*

## What Jacob had already done

To feel this you have to remember the last time a father, a firstborn and a
disguise appeared together in this family. Jacob had gone in to his blind father
wearing his brother's clothes with goat's hair on his hands, and when Isaac asked
who he was, he said: *I am Esau your firstborn.*

Now Jacob is the one in the dark, and it is a firstborn substituted for a
younger sibling that undoes him.

Laban even says it out loud a few verses later — *it is not done here to give the
younger before the firstborn*. It is the exact thing Jacob did, spoken back to
him as local custom.

## The question he asks

*What have you done to me? Didn't I serve you for Rachel? Why have you deceived
me?*

The Hebrew word for *deceived* is the one used of Jacob in the earlier story.
He is asking Laban the question his brother had every right to ask him. The
narrator does not comment. He does not need to.

---

## What the story is not saying

It is not a lesson that God pays people back — the chapter never says so, and
what follows is not punishment but a long, tangled family in which God keeps
working anyway. Leah, married by trickery and unloved, becomes the mother of
Judah, and the line runs through her to David and to Christ. Not through Rachel,
the one Jacob chose.

## What it is for

Some things are only visible from the other side of them. Jacob had a name for
what his brother did to him and no name for what he had done himself, until he
woke up on the receiving end of it.

That is the use of this passage: it puts a man in the position he once put
someone else in, and it lets him hear his own words come back in another man's
mouth. Nothing is explained to him. He simply sees it.

> **On Leah.** She is the person this is being done *to*, and the story rarely
> gets told from where she is standing. Handed over, unwanted the next morning,
> and told for years that she was not the one. The chapter that follows names
> her sons and you can hear it in every name. And it is her son Judah, not
> Rachel's, whose line ends up carrying the promise. The Bible keeps doing this,
> and it is worth noticing every time.
