After two chapters about the man of lawlessness, Paul turns and thanks God for the
people reading it.

## The turn

*But **we owe God thanks for you always**.*

The Greek is *we are bound to give thanks*. He uses the same construction at the start of
the letter — an obligation rather than an inclination.

## What comes just before

The most alarming passage in either Thessalonian letter: the falling away, the man of
sin sitting in the temple of God, the lawless one whom the Lord will consume with the
breath of His mouth, and *strong delusion* sent on those who did not receive the love of
the truth.

Then, without transition: **but we owe God thanks for you**.

## What they are called

*Brothers and sisters **loved by the Lord**.*

The Greek is a perfect passive participle — *having been loved*, and standing loved.

## The choosing

*Because **God chose you from the beginning to be saved**.*

The Greek is **heileto** — took for Himself, selected.

**From the beginning** — some manuscripts read *as firstfruits*; either way the point is
that the choosing predates them.

## The two halves

*The **Spirit setting you apart** for Himself, and **you believing the truth**.*

Sanctification by the Spirit, and belief of the truth. Paul puts God's action and the
person's response in one clause without ranking them.

## The calling

*That is what **He called you to**, and **He called you through the good news we brought**.*

The eternal choice and the specific sermon are the same event described twice. Paul does
not treat the preaching as incidental to the choosing — it is the means.

The Greek for *good news* is **euangelion**, and *we brought* makes it concrete: Paul,
Silas and Timothy, in a synagogue in Thessalonica, for three sabbaths, before a mob drove
them out of town.

## What it is for

*So that you would come to **possess the glory of our Lord Jesus Christ**.*

The Greek is *for the obtaining of the glory*.

Not to see it. To obtain it. Paul writes the same thing to Rome: *whom He justified,
these He also glorified* — in the past tense.

## What the Thessalonians were like

Paul had been with them a matter of weeks. The church was young, poor, under pressure
from its own countrymen, and confused enough about the return of Christ that both letters
have to address it.

He tells them they were chosen from the beginning.

## The instruction that follows

*Therefore, brethren, **stand fast and hold the traditions which you were taught**,
whether by word or our epistle.*

And then a prayer: *now may our Lord Jesus Christ Himself, and our God and Father, who
has loved us and given us everlasting consolation and good hope by grace, **comfort your
hearts and establish you in every good word and work**.*

> **Where Paul puts the paragraph.** Directly after the darkest passage he ever wrote
> about the future. The answer he gives to it is not information — it is a list of what
> is already true of the people who were frightened.
