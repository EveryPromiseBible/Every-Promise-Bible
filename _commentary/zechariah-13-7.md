One verse, and Jesus quotes it about Himself on the last night.

## Who the sword is called against

*Awake, sword, against My shepherd, against **the man who stands close to Me**.*

The Hebrew for the second phrase is unusual — it means an associate, a
companion, someone of the same kind. It is used in Leviticus of a man's
neighbour, his fellow.

God calls the shepherd His own, and He calls him a companion, and He tells the
sword to wake up against him.

## Who wields it

*Declares the LORD Almighty.* The command is God's.

That is the difficult centre of the verse, and the New Testament does not soften
it — Isaiah 53 says the same: *it pleased the LORD to crush him.* The striking is
not something that happened to God's shepherd against His will.

## Where Jesus uses it

On the way to Gethsemane: *every one of you is going to fall away tonight. It
stands written: I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep will scatter.*

He quotes it to explain what His disciples are about to do — and then, in the same
breath, tells them where to meet Him afterwards. See the note on Mark 14:27–31.

## What follows in Zechariah

*I will turn My hand against the little ones.* The Hebrew phrase can carry the
sense of turning a hand **to** someone — and the same chapter goes on to describe
a remnant refined and brought through fire, who call on God's name and are
answered: *they are My people, and they will say, the LORD is my God.*

The scattering is not the end of the sentence.

> **Where the shepherd language goes.** Zechariah's struck shepherd stands behind
> John 10, where Jesus calls Himself the good shepherd who lays down His life for
> the sheep — *no one takes it from Me.* Both are true at once: the sword is
> commanded, and the shepherd lays it down.
