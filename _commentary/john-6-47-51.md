Jesus compares Himself to manna and then says the comparison does not go far
enough.

## The manna argument

*Your ancestors ate manna in the wilderness, and **they died**.*

The crowd had raised the manna themselves, as a credential — Moses gave our
fathers bread from heaven. Jesus accepts the fact and points at the graves.

The wilderness generation ate the miracle bread every morning for forty years and
is buried in the desert. Whatever the manna did, it did not deal with death.

## The claim

*This is the bread that comes down from heaven, so that a person may eat it and
not die. I am the living bread.*

The manna came down and was gathered. This bread is described as **living** —
alive in itself, not merely supplied.

And the tense at the end is future: *the bread I **will** give is My flesh, and I
will give it for the life of the world.* He is pointing forward to the cross.

## Why the crowd could not take it

The next verses have them arguing among themselves — *how can this man give us
his flesh to eat?* — and many disciples turn back before the chapter ends.

Jesus does not soften it or explain it away. He repeats it, harder.

## Eating

The verb He uses in the following verses is a blunt one — the ordinary word for
chewing. Whatever else the language does, it insists on something taken in
personally rather than admired from outside.

Manna had to be gathered by each household, every morning, and eaten. Nobody
could gather it for you.

> **Where the chapter ends.** *The words I have spoken to you are spirit and they
> are life.* Jesus Himself supplies the interpretive key after the hard sayings —
> and then asks the twelve whether they want to leave too. Peter's answer is
> about having nowhere else to go.
