The smallest possible flock, and the largest possible gift, in one sentence.

## The address

*"**Do not be afraid, little flock**."*

The Greek is **mikron poimnion** — a double diminutive. Both words are small.

*Poimnion* is already the diminutive of *poimne*, flock. Then *mikron* is added to it.

He is speaking to a group He has just told not to worry about food or clothing, and He names
them as small before He says anything else.

## What He does with the fear

*Do not be afraid* — **me phobou**, present imperative. Stop being afraid, or do not go on being
afraid.

The fear is assumed to be there already.

## The reason

*"It is **your Father's delight to give you the kingdom**."*

The Greek is **eudokesen** — He was well pleased, it seemed good to Him.

Same verb as the voice at the Jordan: *this is My beloved Son, **in whom I am well pleased***.
See the note on Matthew 3:16–17.

## What is being said about the Father

Not that He will give it if asked hard enough, or that He is willing to be persuaded.

That giving it pleases Him.

The verb is aorist — a settled decision already made, not a disposition He might arrive at.

## The size mismatch

A little flock, and the kingdom.

Jesus puts them next to each other on purpose. The size of the recipient is not what determines
the size of the gift.

## What comes immediately before

*"Consider the ravens, for they neither sow nor reap… and God feeds them. **Of how much more
value are you** than the birds?"*

*"Seek the kingdom of God, and all these things shall be added to you."* See the note on
Luke 12:22–31.

The ravens are fed and the lilies are clothed, and then the flock is given the kingdom.

## The instruction that follows

*"**Sell what you have and give it away to the poor**."*

Which only reads as reasonable after the previous verse.

He does not ask them to sell in order to receive. The kingdom has already been given in the
sentence before.

## The purse

*"**Make yourselves purses that never wear out** — a treasure in the heavens that never runs
down, **where no thief comes near it and no moth eats it away**."*

Two threats to earthly wealth: theft from outside and decay from inside. Both are ruled out.

The parallel in Matthew is in the Sermon on the Mount. See the note on
Matthew 6:19–21.

## The principle

*"Because **wherever your treasure is, that is where your heart will be too**."*

The order is the point. Treasure first, heart follows.

Not *put your heart in the right place and your money will follow*. The other way round.

## What he had just told a man

The chapter opens with someone shouting *teacher, tell my brother to divide the inheritance with
me*, and the parable of the man with the full barns who says *soul, you have many goods laid up
for many years*.

*So is he who lays up treasure for himself, and is not rich toward God.* See the note on
Luke 12:13–21.

The barn-builder had no Father in his monologue — it is all *I* and *my* and *soul*.

> **The verb.** **Eudokesen** — it pleased Him. The same word the Father speaks over the Son at
> the Jordan, used here of handing the kingdom to a flock described as small twice over.
