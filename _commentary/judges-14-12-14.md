A riddle at a wedding, and only one man in the room could possibly solve it.

## What had happened

On the way down to Timnah, *a young lion came roaring against him. And the Spirit of the LORD
came mightily upon him, and he tore it apart as one would have torn apart a young goat*. See
the note on Judges 14:5–7.

He told nobody. Not his father, not his mother.

Coming back later for the wedding, he turned aside to look at the carcass, and *behold, a swarm
of bees and honey were in the lion's carcass*.

He scooped it out and ate it as he walked, and gave some to his parents — *but he did not tell
them that he had taken the honey out of the carcass of the lion*.

A Nazirite was forbidden to touch a dead body. He had eaten out of one.

## The wager

*"Let me put a riddle to you. If you can solve it within the seven days of the feast, I'll give
you **thirty linen garments and thirty sets of clothes**. But if you can't, **then you give them
to me**."*

Thirty of each, both ways. A substantial stake — the thirty companions had been assigned to
him as groomsmen, and the Hebrew suggests they were there to watch him.

## Their answer

*"**Ask your riddle. Let's hear it**."*

## The riddle

*"Out of the eater came something to eat; out of the strong came something sweet."*

The Hebrew is compact and rhythmic: **me-ha'okhel yatsa ma'akhal, ume'az yatsa matoq**.

## Why it is unfair

It is not a riddle. It is a private incident dressed as one.

There is no chain of reasoning that gets from the words to a lion carcass full of bees. Only
somebody who had been there could answer it, and nobody had.

## The stall

*Three days passed, and **they could not solve it**.*

## What happens next

On the fourth day they go to his wife: *entice your husband, that he may explain the riddle to
us, or else we will burn you and your father's house with fire*.

She weeps on him for the rest of the week — *you only hate me, you do not love me* — and on the
seventh day he tells her, and she tells them.

## Their answer

*"**What is sweeter than honey? And what is stronger than a lion?**"*

Delivered before sunset on the last day.

## His reply

*"**If you had not plowed with my heifer, you would not have solved my riddle**."*

## What he does about the debt

Goes down to Ashkelon, kills thirty men, takes their clothes, and pays the wager.

*And his anger was aroused, and he went back up to his father's house.*

The account records the whole thing without comment. Judges is like that.

## Where the phrase goes

The riddle itself outlives the story. *Out of the strong came something sweet* is quoted for
centuries after by people who have never read the chapter.

It was on tins of Lyle's Golden Syrup — a lion carcass and a swarm of bees, with the words
underneath.

## What the chapter is doing

Samson is a Nazirite by an angel's announcement before his birth, and the vow is broken piece
by piece: the carcass here, the wine implied by the feast, and the hair at the end. See the
note on Judges 16:21–22.

And in the middle of it: *but his father and mother did not know that it was of the LORD — that
He was seeking an occasion to move against the Philistines*.

Judges says that about the marriage his parents objected to.

## The shape of what he saw

Life out of a death. Sweetness out of the thing that had come to kill him.

*"O Death, where is your sting? O Hades, where is your victory?"… But thanks be to God, who
gives us **the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ**.*

*Unless a grain of wheat falls into the ground and dies, it remains alone; **but if it dies, it
produces much grain**.* See the note on John 12:20–23.

## Where the honey was

Inside the thing he had already beaten.

A lion torn apart by the Spirit of the LORD, left at the side of the road — and on the way back
there is food in it. Judges records it without comment, and the man who found it made a party
game out of it.

> **Why it could not be solved.** It was not a riddle. It was a description of something that
> had happened to one man on a road, with nobody watching.
