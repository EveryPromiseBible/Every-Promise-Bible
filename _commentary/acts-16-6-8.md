Three sentences, and almost nothing happens in them. Paul tries to go one way
and cannot. He tries another and cannot. He ends up somewhere he was not aiming
for. It is one of the most useful passages in the New Testament about being led,
precisely because so little of it looks like leading.

## What the Spirit actually does here

Read the verbs:

- **kept** from speaking the word in Asia
- **would not allow** them into Bithynia
- so they **passed by** Mysia and came down to Troas

Not a word of positive instruction. No voice, no vision, no verse. The Spirit's
whole contribution in these three sentences is to stop them, twice, and the
narrative does not say how — Luke, who elsewhere reports angels and trances and
voices in detail, does not tell us.

## What they were being stopped from

This is the part worth sitting with. They were not being stopped from sin. They
were being stopped from **preaching the gospel in Asia**.

There was nothing wrong with the plan. It was a good plan, made by good men, for
the best possible reason. It was simply not the one.

That should adjust how a closed door gets read. A blocked way is not proof that
the intention was bad, and the passage never suggests Paul had made a mistake in
wanting to go.

---

## Where it lands

Troas is not a destination in this story; it is where you end up when you have
run out of directions. And it is there — after two refusals and a journey that
looks like wandering — that the vision of the man from Macedonia comes.

The order matters. The clear guidance arrives *last*, and it arrives to men who
had kept moving while they only had the unclear kind. If they had stopped at the
first refusal and waited for something positive, they would not have been at
Troas to receive it.

## What it does not license

It would be easy to turn this into a rule: every obstacle is God redirecting you.
Acts will not support that. In the same book Paul is stoned, jailed, shipwrecked
and opposed at length, and none of those are read as the Spirit's redirection —
he goes back to several of the cities he was driven out of.

The difference is not in the size of the obstacle. Luke simply tells us, in these
two cases, whose hand it was. Most of the time nobody gets told, which is why the
next verse matters as much as these: they were still walking when the answer came.

> **On the word "kept."** *Koluo* is an ordinary word for hindering or preventing
> — used of stopping someone crossing a street or forbidding an action. Luke does
> not dress it up. Whatever the experience was, he reports it as plainly as a
> road being shut.
