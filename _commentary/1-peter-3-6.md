Peter reaches back to Genesis for an example, and the sentence he ends on is about fear.

## The example

*Sarah did it — she **did what Abraham said and called him her master**.*

The Greek is **kurion** — lord, master.

Peter is quoting one word from one verse. Genesis 18: Sarah laughs behind the tent door
and says to herself *after I have grown old, shall I have pleasure, **my lord** being old
also?*

It is an aside, in her own head, and Peter builds on it.

## What she was doing when she said it

Laughing at the promise. And then denying she had laughed, and being told *no, but you did
laugh*.

Peter picks the sentence out of that scene.

## Who her daughters are

*You are **her daughters when you do what is right** and **will not let any scare rattle
you**.*

The Greek is *doing good and not being afraid with any terror*.

Two conditions, and the second is the one with the weight.

## The fear clause

*Will not let **any scare rattle you**.*

The Greek is **me phoboumenai medemian ptoesin** — not fearing any intimidation.

**Ptoesis** occurs once in the New Testament. It means a fluttering, a startling — the
motion of a bird put up out of cover.

Peter is not writing about courage in the abstract. He is writing to women in the Roman
world, some of them married to unbelieving husbands, in an era when a wife's religion was
supposed to be her husband's.

The verses before this address exactly that: *wives, likewise, be submissive to your own
husbands, that **even if some do not obey the word, they, without a word, may be won by the
conduct of their wives**.*

## What is not said

Nothing about the husbands being right. The paragraph assumes some of them are not
obeying the word.

## The adornment

*Do not let your adornment be merely outward — arranging the hair, wearing gold, or putting
on fine apparel — rather **let it be the hidden person of the heart**, with the incorruptible
beauty of **a gentle and quiet spirit**, which is **very precious in the sight of God**.*

The Greek for *very precious* is **poluteles** — of great cost. It is the word used of the
alabaster flask of ointment.

## What comes next

*Husbands, likewise, dwell with them **with understanding**, giving honour to the wife…
**that your prayers may not be hindered**.*

The Greek is *according to knowledge*. And the sanction attached is about prayer.

## What Peter says to everybody a few verses on

*And **do not be afraid of their threats, nor be troubled**. But sanctify the Lord God in
your hearts.*

He quotes Isaiah 8 there, and the same fear-word runs through the letter.

## The word for fear in this letter

Peter uses it both ways. *Do not be afraid* of threats — and *conduct yourselves throughout
the time of your sojourning here **in fear***, meaning reverence.

He does not confuse them.

## Where the whole passage sits

Between instructions to servants under harsh masters and the passage about suffering for
doing good, with Christ's example in the middle: *who, when He was reviled, did not revile
in return.*

> **What makes somebody Sarah's daughter, in Peter's account.** Doing right, and not
> letting a fright put you up out of cover — and he takes the example from a woman caught
> laughing at the promise.
