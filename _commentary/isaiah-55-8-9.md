The verse gets quoted at funerals and after disappointments, as a way of saying
God's reasons are beyond us. Read in its own paragraph it is doing something
else.

## What comes immediately before

*Let the wicked forsake his way, and the unrighteous man his thoughts; let him
return to the LORD, and He will have mercy on him, and to our God, for **He will
abundantly pardon**.*

Then: *for My thoughts are not your thoughts, and your ways are not My ways.*

The *for* connects them. The subject of the comparison is the pardoning.

## What is being said

Human beings forgive on a scale — a bit, grudgingly, up to a point, with
conditions. God's forgiving is described as operating at a different altitude
altogether.

The Hebrew behind *abundantly pardon* is emphatic: He will multiply to pardon.

So the sentence is not primarily about God's mysterious purposes in suffering. It
is about how far past our instincts His mercy runs.

## The measure

*As high as the heavens are above the earth.*

The largest distance available to an ancient writer, and Isaiah uses it twice
elsewhere for God's steadfast love and for the removal of sins — *as far as the
east is from the west.*

## Why this reading matters pastorally

Quoted as a general statement about inscrutability, the verse can be used to shut
down a grieving person's questions.

Read as it stands, it says something warmer: whatever you would do with someone
who had wronged you, His response is higher than that — and the gap is the size
of the sky.
