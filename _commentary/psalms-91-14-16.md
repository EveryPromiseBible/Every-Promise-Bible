The psalm changes speaker at the end, and most readings walk straight past it.

## Who is talking

Verses 1 to 13 are *about* God — He will cover you, you will not fear. Someone is
describing Him.

From verse 14 the voice switches to the first person and stays there. Seven
promises in three verses, every one of them God's own speech:

- **I** will set you free
- **I** will lift you high
- **I** will answer
- **I** will be with you in trouble
- **I** will rescue you
- **I** will honour you
- **I** will fill your hands with long life

The psalm does not end with a description of God. It ends with God taking over
the sentence.

## The reason He gives

*Because you have held onto Me in love.*

The Hebrew verb means to cling, to be joined to — the word used of a man joined
to his wife, and of Israel told to hold fast to the LORD. It is not achievement
and it is not blamelessness. It is attachment.

That is what the seven promises are hung on. Someone who has taken hold and will
not let go.

## "With long life I will fill your hands"

The Hebrew is *satisfy* — filled up, having enough. Not merely a long span of
years but a fullness of them, and then the last line: *I will let you see how
deep My rescue runs.*

The psalm ends on sight. Not only rescued, but shown the rescue — allowed to see
how far down it goes.

## Who is speaking

God, in the first person, for the last three verses of the psalm. Everything before this is
about Him; here He answers.

*"**Because he has set his love upon Me, therefore I will deliver him**; I will set him on
high, because he has known My name."*

## The seven things He undertakes

Deliver, set on high, answer, be with him in trouble, honour him, satisfy him with long life,
and show him salvation. Seven first-person promises in three verses, and every verb is His.

## The last word of the psalm

*"**And show him My salvation.**"*

The Hebrew is **yeshuati** — My salvation. The noun behind the name.

*"Lord, now You are letting Your servant depart in peace, according to Your word; for **my eyes
have seen Your salvation**."*

**Simeon** says it in the temple holding a six-week-old baby, and Luke records the word he
uses. The last line of Psalm 91 is a promise to be shown something, and an old man says he has
seen it.

## What the deliverance is attached to

*"Because he has **known My name**."*

Not because he has kept a standard. The psalm's own stated ground is knowing who He is.

> **The trouble is named, not denied.** *I will be with you in trouble* is one of
> the seven, and it is worth holding on to. This is not a psalm promising a life
> nothing ever happens in. It promises a Presence in the middle of what happens,
> and a rescue out the other side, spoken by God about Himself.
