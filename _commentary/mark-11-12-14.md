The only destructive miracle in the Gospels, and Mark puts it either side of the temple.

## When

*The next day, as they were leaving Bethany.*

The day after the entry into Jerusalem — the tenth of Nisan, with the cloaks on the road. See
the note on Luke 19:36–40.

He had gone into the temple, *looked round at all things*, and left because it was late.

## The hunger

*Jesus was hungry.*

Mark says it plainly, and it is one of the details he keeps that the others drop.

## The tree

*He noticed a fig tree **in the distance, full of leaves**.*

A fig tree in leaf is making a claim. On that species the small early fruit — the **paggim**
— appears before or with the leaves.

A tree covered in foliage in early spring is advertising fruit.

## The inspection

*And He went over **to see whether there was any fruit on it**.*

## What He found

*When He reached it He found **nothing but leaves**.*

## The clause

*For **it was not the season for figs**.*

Mark writes it and leaves it standing, which is why the incident is so often called unfair.

It is the key to the story. The tree was not expected to have a ripe crop. It was in full
leaf out of season, which meant it should have had the early fruit — and it had neither.

The display was the offence.

## The sentence

*And He said to the tree, "**May no one ever eat fruit from you again**."*

Spoken to a tree, in the hearing of the disciples. Mark notes that: *and His disciples heard
Him say it*.

## What He does next

Walks into Jerusalem and clears the temple.

*"Is it not written, '**My house shall be called a house of prayer for all nations**'? But you
have made it a 'den of thieves.'"*

The building was in full leaf. Courts, priests, sacrifices, money changing, and no fruit.

## The sandwich

Mark does this repeatedly — starts a story, interrupts it, and finishes it. Jairus and the
woman with the haemorrhage is the same shape.

Here: the tree, then the temple, then the tree again. Each explains the other.

## The morning after

*Now in the morning, as they passed by, they saw **the fig tree dried up from the roots**.*

And Peter: *"Rabbi, look! The fig tree which You cursed has withered away."*

The answer is about mountains and prayer and forgiving. See the note on Mark 11:20–26.

## What the fig tree stood for

Hosea: *I found Israel like grapes in the wilderness; I saw your fathers as the firstfruits
on the fig tree in its first season*.

Jeremiah: *there are no grapes on the vine, nor figs on the fig tree, and the leaf shall
fade*.

Micah: *there is no cluster to eat of the early-ripe fruit which my soul desires*.

## The parable He had already told

*"A certain man had a fig tree planted in his vineyard, and he came seeking fruit on it and
found none… **cut it down; why does it use up the ground?**"*

And the vinedresser: *let it alone this year also, until I dig around it and fertilise it.
And if it bears fruit, well. But if not, after that you can cut it down.*

Three years of looking, and then one more.

## What the fruitless thing had

Everything except fruit. Mark's account is precise about it: leaves visible from a distance,
and nothing underneath.

> **The clause everyone stumbles on.** *It was not the season for figs.* Which is the point —
> a tree in full leaf out of season was advertising a crop it did not have.
