He has just been counted righteous for believing, and the next thing out of his mouth is a
question.

## The self-introduction

*"**I am the LORD who brought you out of Ur of the Chaldeans** to give you this land as your own."*

The Hebrew is **ani YHWH asher hotzetika me'Ur Kasdim**.

## The form of it

The same shape as the opening of the Ten Commandments: *I am the LORD your God, who brought you out
of the land of Egypt*.

God identifies Himself by something He has already done, and then states what it was for.

## The tense

*To give you this land.*

He is standing in it, and it is not his. He will die owning one field with a cave in it, bought at
full price for four hundred shekels. See the note on Genesis 23:17–20.

## The question

*"**LORD God, how can I know for certain that I will possess it**?"*

The Hebrew is **bammah eda** — by what shall I know?

## What sits two verses earlier

*And he believed in the LORD, and He accounted it to him for righteousness.* See the note on
Genesis 15:6.

## The difficulty in that

The verse Paul builds justification by faith on is followed, almost immediately, by the man asking
for proof.

## What it is not

Not the unbelief Zacharias is struck dumb for. His question was *how shall I know this? For I am an
old man* — an objection dressed as a question. See the note on Luke 1:18–20.

**Bammah eda** asks by what means, not whether.

## How it is answered

Not with a sign in the sky, and not with a rebuke.

*"Bring Me a heifer three years old, a goat three years old, a ram three years old, along with a
turtledove and a young pigeon."* See the note on Genesis 15:9–11.

He is told to fetch five animals.

## What that answer is

A covenant.

The Hebrew idiom is **karat berit** — to *cut* a covenant. The halves are laid opposite each other
and the parties walk the aisle between them, saying by the act: let this be done to me if I break
it.

## Who walks it

*A smoking firepot and a blazing torch appeared and passed between the pieces.* See the note on
Genesis 15:17–21.

Abram is asleep on the ground. Only one party goes down the aisle.

## Why the question gets that answer

He asked how he could know. He is given the strongest assurance the ancient world had — and then
excused from swearing to it.

The certainty he asked for does not rest on him, which is the point of putting him to sleep first.

## What is promised in the same breath

*"To your offspring I give this land, from the river of Egypt to the great river, the Euphrates."*

The Hebrew is **natatti** — I have given. Perfect tense, for land not entered for four hundred more
years.

## The other man who asked

*"How shall I know this?"* asked in the temple by a priest, and answered with nine months of
silence.

*"How can this be?"* asked in Nazareth by a girl, and answered with an explanation. See the note on
Luke 1:34–38.

The same interrogative, three times in Scripture, and the answers are not alike.

## Where the covenant goes

*The law, which was four hundred and thirty years later, cannot annul the covenant that was
confirmed before by God in Christ.* See the note on Galatians 3:15–18.

A later agreement does not cancel an earlier grant.

## What a covenant can be replaced by

Only a better one.

*But now He has obtained a more excellent ministry, inasmuch as **He is also Mediator of a better
covenant**, which was established on better promises.* See the note on Hebrews 8:6–7.

## Who walks the aisle now

*This cup is **the new covenant in My blood**, which is shed for you.* See the note on
Luke 22:19–20.

The same arrangement, and the same asymmetry. One party swears and one party is asleep on the
ground — and at Calvary the party who swore is the one the penalty falls on.

## What that settles

*How can I know* is still answered the same way. A cross and a cup rather than a heifer and a
torch, and still nothing asked of the man but that he be there for it.

> **The answer he gets.** He asks how he can know, and is handed a knife and a list of animals —
> then sleeps through the part that secures it.
