A story told to a man looking for a boundary, and it ends by moving the question.

## The question that prompted it

*"**And who is my neighbour?**"*

**Luke** gives the motive: *he, wanting to justify himself, said to Jesus.*

**Thelon dikaiosai heauton** — wishing to justify himself. The parable is an answer to a man trying
to establish that he has already done enough.

*But to him who does not work but believes on Him **who justifies the ungodly**, his faith is
accounted for righteousness.*

The one thing nobody in **Luke** manages is self-justification. Two chapters later a tax collector
asks for the other kind and goes home with it. See the note on Luke 18:13–14.

## The road

Jerusalem to Jericho: seventeen miles, and it drops three thousand feet through country with enough
caves to make the ambush unremarkable.

## The two who pass

A priest and a Levite. Both would have been made ceremonially unclean by a corpse, and a man *half
dead* at that distance is indistinguishable from one.

The parable does not say they were wicked. It says they had a reason, and that the reason came from
the same law the lawyer was quoting.

## The one who stops

*"**But a Samaritan.**"*

The lawyer cannot say the word at the end. Asked which of the three was a neighbour, he answers *the
one who showed mercy on him.*

## What the Samaritan does

Seven actions: he saw, he had compassion, he went to him, he bandaged, he poured oil and wine, he
set him on his own animal, he brought him to an inn. Then two more: he paid, and he promised to come
back.

*But when He saw the multitudes, **He was moved with compassion** for them.*

**Esplanchnisthe** — the same verb **Luke** uses of the Samaritan, and of the father who runs.

## What He does with the lawyer's question

The lawyer asks who qualifies as a neighbour — a question about the boundary of his obligation.
**Jesus** ends by asking which man *was* a neighbour, which is a question about what he is, not who
they are.

*"**Go and do likewise.**"*

## Who else came where we were

*But God demonstrates His own love toward us, in that while we were still sinners, **Christ died for
us**.*

*Who, being in the form of God… **made Himself of no reputation**… and being found in appearance as a
man, He humbled Himself.*

The oldest Christian reading of this parable has the Samaritan as **Christ** — the outsider who comes
to where the man is lying, pays what he cannot pay, and promises to return. **Luke** does not say so,
and the seven verbs describe somebody doing exactly that.

> **What the parable does to the question.** He is asked where the obligation stops and answers with
> a man who did not ask. And Luke has already told us why the question was put: *wanting to justify
> himself*.
