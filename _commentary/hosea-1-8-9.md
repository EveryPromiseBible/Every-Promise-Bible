A prophet is told to name his son a sentence, and the child carries it around for
the rest of his life.

## The three children

Hosea's wife bears three, and each name is a message:

- **Jezreel** — God scatters
- **Lo-Ruhamah** — Not-Pitied, not shown mercy
- **Lo-Ammi** — Not-My-People

The prophet's household is the sermon. Every time he calls his son in for dinner
he says it again.

## The sentence

*"For you are **not My people**, and **I am not your God**."*

The Hebrew of the second clause is closer to *I am not I-AM to you* — the name
from the burning bush, withdrawn.

## What it describes

This is the season of the exile and everything after it. No temple that mattered,
no king on David's throne, no prophet after Malachi, no ark, and the visible
dealings of God with that nation suspended.

The book of Esther sits inside this. Ten chapters, a Persian empire, a death
decree, and the name of God never once written down. That is what *Lo-Ammi* looks
like from inside it.

## What it does not mean

He is not gone. Esther is the proof of it — a sleepless king, an old record read
at the right hour, a gallows turned round, and a nation alive at the end of it.

Hidden face, and not one hand withdrawn.

## Where the same chapter is already going

Read what stands immediately before verse 9 and immediately after it. Hosea 1:10:

*Yet the number of the children of Israel shall be as the sand of the sea, which
cannot be measured or numbered; and in the place where it was said to them, "You
are not My people," there it shall be said to them, "You are the sons of the
living God."*

The reversal is in the same chapter as the sentence. It is not a later
correction; the promise is written on the other side of the same page.

## "In the very place"

Not somewhere else, and not to a different people. In the place where the words
*not My people* were spoken.

Paul quotes exactly that line in Romans 9 and applies it — and he applies it to
Gentiles being brought in. See the note on Romans 9:25–26.

> **What Hosea is told to do in chapter 3.** Go and love her again. He buys his own
> wife back for fifteen shekels and a homer and a half of barley — the price of a
> slave. The names of the children are what the book says; the purchase is what the
> book is about.
