David's argument for being allowed to fight Goliath is not about courage. It is a
work history.

## What he offers as his credentials

*When a lion or a bear came and carried off a sheep from the flock, I went after
it, struck it down, and rescued the sheep from its mouth.*

Nobody saw this. There was no army watching, no king, no reward. A boy alone with
sheep, going after a predator to recover one animal that belonged to his father.

That is the entire résumé, and it is enough.

## Why he went after them

Not to prove anything. Because the sheep was his father's, and a shepherd who
lets a lion keep it is not a shepherd.

The detail is precise — *rescued the sheep from its mouth*, and when the animal
turned on him, he took it by the fur and killed it. Whoever preserved this
account preserved the specifics.

## The conclusion he draws

*The LORD who rescued me from the paw of the lion and the paw of the bear will
rescue me from the hand of this Philistine.*

The reasoning runs backwards from what has already happened. He is not claiming
to be strong; he is naming who did the rescuing last time and expecting the same
person to do it again.

Note the change of word: paw, paw, and then **hand**. Goliath is put in the same
category as the animals.

## What Saul says

*"Go, and the LORD be with you."*

A king with no better plan sends a shepherd. And the thing that persuaded him was
not a display of strength — it was an account of what God had already done where
nobody was looking.

> **Where the hidden work goes.** Everything David uses in the valley he learned
> on the hills: the sling he had used on predators, the refusal of armour he had
> never worn, and the conviction that the LORD rescues. The lion and the bear are
> not preparation for the giant in some general sense. They are the reason he
> knows what he knows.
