Peter quotes Isaiah and then attaches the quotation to the sermon they had heard.

## Where it comes from

Isaiah 40 — the chapter that opens *comfort, comfort My people*, written to exiles.

The voice in the wilderness, the valleys raised, the mountains made low, and then this:
*all flesh is grass, and all its loveliness is like the flower of the field.*

## The comparison

*Because **people are grass**. Everything splendid about us is **the little flower on the
grass**.*

Not the tree, not the crop. Grass, and the flower on it.

## What happens to it

*The grass **dries out**. The flower **drops off**.*

Two verbs, and both are ordinary. Isaiah's setting explains the speed: *the grass
withers, the flower fades, **because the breath of the LORD blows upon it**.*

In that climate the hot east wind off the desert could finish a field of wildflowers in
a day.

## What is being compared to grass

*Everything splendid about us.* The Greek is **doxa** — glory.

Not human weakness. Human glory. The best of it is the part that falls off first.

## The contrast

*But **what the Lord says stands, and keeps standing**.*

The Greek is **menei eis ton aiona** — remains into the age.

## The turn

*And that is **the message that was announced to you as good news**.*

Peter closes the loop. The eternal word Isaiah was talking about is the one his readers
heard preached — the Greek is **euangelisthen**, evangelised to you.

He does not leave it as a general statement about Scripture's durability. He points at
the specific message they received.

## Why he is saying it

The verse before: *having been **born again**, not of corruptible seed but **incorruptible,
through the word of God** which lives and abides forever.*

The argument runs on the seed. What a thing is born from determines how long it lasts,
and Peter says theirs was imperishable.

## Who he is writing to

*Pilgrims of the Dispersion* — scattered believers in five Roman provinces, under
pressure, and the letter keeps returning to *the fiery trial* and *suffering
wrongfully*.

The grass image lands differently for people whose situation is precarious.

## The other side of the same chapter

*Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who according to His abundant
mercy has begotten us again to **a living hope**… to **an inheritance incorruptible and
undefiled and that does not fade away**, reserved in heaven for you.*

**Does not fade away** — the Greek is **amaranton**, unfading. It is a flower word,
chosen deliberately.

## Where Amos fits

A famine of hearing the words of the LORD. See the note on Amos 8:11–14.

Peter says the word stands. Amos describes what it is like when people cannot find it.

> **What Peter does with an old quotation.** He cites a poem about human transience and
> then says the permanent thing in it is what they were told when somebody preached to
> them.
