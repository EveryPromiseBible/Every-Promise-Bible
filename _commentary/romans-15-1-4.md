Paul has spent a chapter on Christians who disagree about food and holy days —
the strong who can eat anything, the weak who cannot. This is his conclusion,
and it is addressed to the winners of the argument.

## The instruction

*Those of us who are strong have something to do with that strength, and it is
not to please ourselves. It is to carry the weaknesses of those who are not
strong.*

Paul counts himself among the strong. He agrees with them on the substance — he
has already said no food is unclean in itself. And then he tells them that being
right is not the end of the matter.

The verb is to *bear* — to pick up and carry. Not tolerate. Carry.

## The example

*Christ Himself never lived to please Himself.*

And the proof he offers is a quotation from Psalm 69 — *the insults of those who
insulted You fell on Me*. It is a strange verse to choose until you see the
logic: the one with every right to be pleased took what belonged to others
instead.

## The line about scripture

*Everything that was written in earlier days was written to teach us, so that
through the patience and the comfort the Scriptures give, we might go on holding
to hope.*

Paul has just quoted the Old Testament to settle a first-century church dispute
about dinner, and then says why he can: the old writings were for our
instruction. Note what he says they produce — patience, comfort, and hope. Not
primarily information.

> **Why it sits here.** This is Paul's last word on a quarrel about secondary
> things, and his solution is not a ruling. He tells the people who are right to
> use their freedom on behalf of the people who are not, and points at Christ for
> the pattern. It is the most practical thing in the chapter.
