John defines love by pointing at an event rather than a feeling.

## The claim

*Here is **what love is**.*

The Greek is **en touto estin he agape** — in this is love.

A definition, and it is about to be given as a piece of history.

## The direction

*Love **did not start with us loving God**; it started with **God loving us**.*

The Greek is *not that we loved God, but that He loved us*.

John removes one option before he states the other. The sentence is built to prevent a
misreading.

## What He did about it

*And **sending His Son** to be **the sacrifice that answered for our sins**.*

The Greek is **hilasmon** — the word for the thing that turns away wrath, or covers.

It is the word the Greek Old Testament uses for the mercy seat — the lid on the ark that the
blood was sprinkled on, over the tablets of the law.

John uses it twice, here and in chapter 2: *He Himself is the propitiation for our sins, and
not for ours only but also for the whole world*.

## The love is not the feeling

Nothing in the verse describes an emotion. The definition given is a sending and a
sacrifice.

Paul does the same: *God **demonstrates** His own love toward us, in that while we were
still sinners, Christ died for us*. See the note on Romans 5:6–8.

Both apostles define the love by an action taken at a specific time.

## The verse before it

*In this the love of God was manifested toward us, that **God has sent His only begotten Son
into the world, that we might live through Him***.

Said twice in two verses, and the second is the sharper: not only sent, but sent *as* the
propitiation.

## The conclusion John draws

*Beloved, if God so loved us, **we also ought to love one another**.*

The Greek is **opheilomen** — we are under obligation, we owe it.

The debt is stated only after the direction has been established.

## The clause that follows

*No one has seen God at any time. If we love one another, **God abides in us, and His love
has been perfected in us**.*

The Greek is **teteleiotai** — has been brought to its goal.

Nobody sees God, and the visible form the love takes is people loving each other.

## What the chapter says a few lines later

*There is no fear in love; but **perfect love casts out fear**, because fear involves
torment. But he who fears has not been made perfect in love.*

The Greek for *torment* is **kolasin** — punishment.

Fear is treated as a symptom, and the cure named is not effort but the same love the chapter
opened by defining.

## Where it ends

*We love Him because **He first loved us**.*

The Greek is **protos** — first, in order.

The whole chapter has one argument, and it is about sequence.

## Who is writing it

The man who identifies himself in his own Gospel as *the disciple whom Jesus loved*, and
does so four or five times. See the note on John 13:21–26.

He never once calls himself the one who loved Jesus.

> **What is being defined.** Not a feeling and not a duty. *Not that we loved God, but that
> He loved us* — and the evidence given is a sending.
