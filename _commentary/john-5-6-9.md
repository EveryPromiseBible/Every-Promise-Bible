He picks one man out of a colonnade full of them and asks a question nobody would need to
ask.

## What He already knew

*Jesus saw him lying there, and **He knew that he had already been in that condition a long
time**.*

John has just told us how long: thirty-eight years. See the note on John 5:1–5.

The knowing is stated before the question is asked.

## The question

*"**Do you want to be well?**"*

The Greek is **theleis hugies genesthai** — do you will to become whole.

Asked of a man who has been lying by a pool for decades. It is either the cruellest question
in the Gospel or the only one that matters, and the answer he gives is neither yes nor no.

## The answer

*"**Sir, I have no one to put me into the pool** when the water is stirred. **While I am on
my way down, someone else always steps in ahead of me**."*

Two clauses, and both are about other people. No helper, and a competitor who is always
faster.

He does not answer the question. He explains the system he has been losing at.

## What the system was

Whoever got in first. See the note on John 5:1–5.

The one requirement was the ability to move, which is the one thing everybody lying in
those porches did not have.

## The command

*"**Get up. Pick up your mat and walk**."*

Three imperatives, and no reference to the pool.

The Greek is **egeire, aron, peripatei** — rise, take up, walk about.

No water is used. Nobody carries him anywhere. The man is not touched.

## The order of events

*And **at once the man was well**. He picked up his mat, and he walked.*

The Greek is **eutheos** — immediately.

Whole first, then the mat, then the walking. The instruction was given to a man who could
not obey it and was obeyed by a man who could.

## The mat

The Greek is **krabatton** — a poor man's pallet. A rough sleeping mat rather than a bed.

It is the same word Mark uses for the one lowered through the roof. See the note on
Mark 2:1–5.

He is told to carry the thing that carried him.

## The last sentence

*Now **that day was a Sabbath**.*

John drops it at the end, as a footnote, and the whole rest of the chapter comes out of it.

Every healing controversy in the Gospels turns on this: seven of Jesus' recorded healings
happen on the Sabbath, and He never once avoids the day.

The woman bent double for eighteen years is another. See the note on Luke 13:10–17.

## What happens next

The authorities stop the healed man in the street — not for walking, for carrying. See the
note on John 5:10–13.

And Jesus' answer, when they come for Him: *My Father is working right up to this moment,
and I am working too*. See the note on John 5:14–18.

## Who was not healed

*A great multitude of sick people, blind, lame, paralysed.*

One man is spoken to. John does not explain, and does not soften it.

## What He does not ask about

Not faith, not sin, not whether he had been trying. The question is *do you want to be
well*, and the man's answer is about not having anyone.

> **What was not used.** The pool. Thirty-eight years of waiting on stirred water, and the
> cure is three words spoken to a man on a mat.
