---
key: Galatians 2:11–13
title: "The chair he got up from"
updated: 2026-08-09
---
Paul tells the story to prove a point about the gospel, and what he chooses to prove it with is not
a sermon. It is a man moving to another table.

## Face to face

*I stood up to him **face to face**, because he was in the wrong.*

**Kata prosopon antesten** — I set myself against him to his face. No delegation, no letter, no
quiet word. And Paul names the man, in a document he knew would be read aloud in churches.

Notice the reason he gives: not that Peter had taught error. Peter had taught the truth — he is the
one who stood up at the council in Jerusalem and said *God made no distinction between us and them,
purifying their hearts by faith*.

## Fear, and a mask

*He began to pull back and hold himself apart, **afraid** of those of the circumcision.*

**Phoboumenos**. The failure at Antioch is not a doctrinal failure. It is a nerve failure.

*And the rest of the Jews went along with the **pretense** too.* **Hypokrisis** — the word for an
actor playing a part behind a mask. They knew what they believed; they performed something else.

*So that **even Barnabas** was swept up in it.*

That word *even* is Paul's grief showing. Barnabas is the son of encouragement, the man who stood
next to Saul in Jerusalem when the whole church was still frightened of him, the man who would
later break with Paul rather than give up on Mark. If anybody was proof against a room's pressure it
was Barnabas — and the room won.

## What a table says

*They were not **walking straight** about the truth of the gospel.*

**Orthopodousin** — going with a straight foot. It is a walking word, not a talking word, because
nothing was said. Peter simply got up and ate somewhere else.

And that is the whole point. To rise from a table of Gentile believers is to announce, without a
syllable of doctrine, that Christ's blood was not quite enough to seat them. The gospel can be
denied by a chair.

Paul's answer runs on into the greatest sentences in the letter: *knowing that a man is not
justified by the works of the law but by faith in Jesus Christ… I have been crucified with Christ.*

> **And afterwards.** Peter is not finished by this. Years later he writes his own letter and calls
> him *our beloved brother Paul*, and commends his letters — including, presumably, this one, with
> his own worst day in it. Grace is what let Paul say it, and grace is what let Peter take it.
