Three verses that get quoted for the fight and almost never for the conclusion.
Paul describes a genuine conflict, and then draws from it a conclusion nobody
expects.

## The promise comes first

*Walk by the Spirit, and you will not carry out what the flesh craves.*

The order is worth noticing. The instruction is *walk by the Spirit* — a
positive, forward thing. The not-carrying-out is presented as the **result**, not
as a second command to be worked at.

Paul does not say fight the craving and then walk. He says walk, and the other
follows. In Greek the second half is emphatic — a strong negative, closer to
*you certainly will not*.

## The fight is real

*The flesh sets its desire against the Spirit, and the Spirit against the flesh;
the two of them stand opposed to each other, so that you do not do the things you
want to do.*

No spiritual life is being sold here as effortless. Paul describes two settled
opposing pulls, and he says plainly that they get in the way of what a person
means to do. Anyone who has tried to live this knows the sentence is accurate.

But notice which two things are opposed. Not the believer against sin. The Spirit
against the flesh. The believer is the field this happens in, not one of the two
combatants — which is a large part of why the instruction is *walk*, not *win*.

---

## The conclusion he draws

Here is the sentence that gets left off:

> But if the Spirit is leading you, **you are not under the law.**

Given everything before it, you would expect the conclusion to be about victory,
or about proving something. Instead Paul uses the reality of the Spirit's leading
to settle a question about **jurisdiction**.

The whole letter is about people being pushed back under law-keeping. So Paul's
argument runs: you are being led by the Spirit, therefore that is not where you
live. He treats being led as evidence of a legal standing, not as a performance
to be assessed.

## Why that matters for guidance

People bring this verse to decisions — am I being led? Paul brings it to
identity. He does not use it to make anyone anxious about whether their leading
is good enough; he uses it to tell them what their being led already proves.

That is the same move he makes in Romans 8, where *led by the Spirit* is
immediately followed by adoption, Abba, and inheritance. Both times, leading is
raised as evidence of sonship rather than as a test of it.

> **On "the things you want to do."** The line is often read as defeat — I cannot
> do what I want. In context Paul is describing why a person cannot simply
> execute their good intentions by willpower, which is exactly the argument for
> walking by the Spirit rather than trying harder. It is a diagnosis, and the
> prescription is in the first line of the paragraph.
