The last thing Paul is recorded saying in Acts, and it is somebody else's words.

## Where he is

Rome, under house arrest, in *his own rented house*, chained to a soldier.

He had called the leaders of the Jewish community together three days after arriving. They
said they had received no letters about him and wanted to hear what he thought — *concerning
this sect, we know that it is spoken against everywhere*.

## The day

*From morning until evening he explained and solemnly testified of the kingdom of God,
persuading them concerning Jesus **from both the Law of Moses and the Prophets**.*

A full day of it. And the result: *some were persuaded by the things which were spoken, and
some disbelieved*.

## The disagreement

*They **disagreed among themselves and began to leave**.*

The Greek is **asumphonoi** — not sounding together. Where the English *symphony* comes from.

They are walking out mid-argument, and Paul speaks over the noise.

## The attribution

*"**The Holy Spirit spoke rightly through Isaiah the prophet to our fathers**."*

He names the speaker as the Spirit and the mouthpiece as Isaiah.

*To our fathers* — he is still saying *our*.

## The quotation

Isaiah 6, from the commission given to the prophet the year King Uzziah died.

*"You will **hear and hear, and never understand**; you will **look and look, and never
see**."*

## The order

*"For this people's **heart has grown dull**. They **hear with difficulty**, and **they have
closed their eyes**."*

Heart, ears, eyes. And the last is active — *they have closed*.

## What is prevented

*"Otherwise they might **see with their eyes and hear with their ears and understand with
their heart and turn, and I would heal them**."*

Five verbs in a chain, and healing at the end.

The same passage Jesus quotes in Matthew 13 to explain the parables. See the note on
Matthew 13:10–17.

## How often it is used

This passage is quoted more often in the New Testament than almost any other Old Testament
text — Matthew, Mark, Luke, John and Paul all use it.

Every one of them uses it at a moment when the message has been heard and not received.

Paul uses it last, and closes the book of Acts with it.

## What he says next

*"So let it be known to you that **this salvation of God has been sent to the Gentiles, and
they will listen**."* See the note on Acts 28:28–29.

## What he had written about it

Romans 9 to 11, three chapters, and the anguish is real: *I have great sorrow and continual
grief in my heart. For I could wish that I myself were accursed from Christ for my brethren,
my countrymen according to the flesh.*

And the conclusion: *blindness in part has happened to Israel **until the fullness of the
Gentiles has come in**. And so all Israel will be saved.*

The blindness is temporary and partial in Paul's account, and he says so in the letter he
sent to this city before he ever reached it.

## Where Acts ends

*Then Paul dwelt two whole years in his own rented house, and received all who came to him,
**preaching the kingdom of God and teaching the things which concern the Lord Jesus Christ,
with all confidence, no one forbidding him**.*

The last word in the Greek is **akolutos** — unhindered.

Luke ends a book about a man in chains with an adverb meaning *without hindrance*.

> **The passage everyone reaches for.** Isaiah 6, quoted by four Gospel writers and Paul —
> and always at the point where the message has been delivered and not taken.
