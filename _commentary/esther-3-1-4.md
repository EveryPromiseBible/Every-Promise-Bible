The villain is introduced with a family name, and the family name is the whole
argument.

## "The Agagite"

*Haman son of Hammedatha **the Agagite**.*

Agag was the king of Amalek whom Saul was ordered to destroy and chose to spare —
1 Samuel 15, the disobedience that cost Saul the kingdom.

Amalek was the nation that attacked Israel from behind in the wilderness, picking
off the tired and the stragglers, and God said He would have war with them from
generation to generation. See the note on Deuteronomy 25:17–19.

Four centuries after Saul let Agag live, Agag's descendant is second in the
Persian empire and signing a death warrant for every Jew alive.

What is not put to death does not stay small. It comes back with an office and a
signet ring.

## The promotion

*King Ahasuerus promoted Haman… raising him higher than all the other officials.*

No reason is given. Haman has done nothing in the book. Mordecai, by contrast,
has just saved the king's life in the previous four verses — and got nothing.

The writer puts those two facts side by side without comment. It looks like the
wrong man rising, and for three chapters it is.

## "Mordecai would not bow"

The whole court bows. It has been commanded. Mordecai stands.

He gives one reason, and it is the only reason in the book: *he had told them he
was a Jew.*

He does not argue, campaign, or explain further. He is asked *day after day* and
*he would not listen.*

## What the servants were doing

*So they told Haman, **to see whether Mordecai's reason would stand**.*

They report him in order to watch what happens. That is the sentence that lights
the fuse — a small piece of court malice, and an empire-wide massacre is drafted
because of it.

> **A Benjamite and an Amalekite.** Mordecai has already been introduced as *son of
> Kish, a Benjamite* — Saul's line. The old war Saul left unfinished is standing in
> a Persian gateway, and this time the Benjamite does not bow. See the note on
> Esther 2:5–7.
