The famous line is the conclusion of three chapters about meat, and knowing that
changes how it reads.

## What the argument was

Corinth's markets sold meat that had been sacrificed in pagan temples. Some
Christians ate it without a thought; others could not, and were distressed by
those who did.

Paul's answer runs long, and it never resolves into a rule about the meat. It
resolves into a question about people: what does eating this do to the person
next to me?

## The three groups

*Give no offence to Jews, or to Greeks, or to the church of God.*

He names the whole social map — the synagogue, the pagan city, and his own
congregation — and asks them to consider all three. Not a private conscience
question; a public one.

## The standard he sets himself

*Not seeking my own profit but the profit of the many, so that they may be
saved.*

The word for *profit* is a plain commercial one — advantage, benefit, what is in
it for me. Paul says he runs the calculation for other people rather than for
himself, and he says it as a description of how he already lives, not as advice.

> **On "do everything for the glory of God."** Lifted out, it becomes a general
> piety about doing your best. In place it is a tiebreaker for a specific and
> genuinely difficult question — when two Christians disagree about something
> permitted, and there is no rule to settle it, decide by what serves the other
> person. The very next line of the letter is *follow my example, as I follow
> Christ's*.
