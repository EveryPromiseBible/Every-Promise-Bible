Paul compresses the whole gospel into two sentences, and the logic runs in a
chain where each link depends on the one before it.

## The chain

- **God sent forth His Son** — born of a woman, born under the law
- **to redeem those who were under the law**
- **so that we might receive the adoption as sons**
- **and because you are sons, God has sent forth the Spirit of His Son**

Two sendings, and the second follows the first. The Spirit is not sent to make
sons; He is sent *because* they are sons. Paul is careful about the order, and
it is the opposite of how the sequence is often felt.

## "Born under the law"

Christ is described as entering the exact position the people He came for were
in. Not visiting it — born into it, under the same obligation.

That is how the redemption works in Paul's argument: someone had to be genuinely
inside the arrangement to buy anybody out of it.

## The cry

*The Spirit of His Son into your hearts, crying out, Abba, Father.*

Notice who is doing the crying here. In Romans 8 it is *we* who cry Abba; here it
is the Spirit. Paul says it both ways within two letters, which suggests he did
not think of them as different events.

The word *Abba* is Aramaic, left untranslated in a Greek letter to Galatia. See
the note on Romans 8:15–17 for what it carries.

## The last line

*You are no longer a slave but a son; and if a son, then an heir of God through
Christ.*

Slave, son, heir — three words, and the movement between them is the whole letter
in miniature. Paul is writing to people being persuaded to go back under law, and
his argument is not that it is wrong so much as that it is *backwards*: it is
walking out of the family and asking for a job.

> **"The fullness of the time."** Paul's phrase for the timing. Not that the
> world was at its best, but that a particular moment had filled up — a Roman
> peace, Greek as a common language, roads, and a scattered Jewish diaspora with
> synagogues in every port. He treats the arrangement of an era as deliberate.
