One of the most quoted proverbs about speech, and the two lines it sits between
are what keep it in proportion.

## The image

*A man eats his fill from the fruit of his mouth; the harvest of his lips is what
satisfies him. Death and life are in the power of the tongue, and those who love
it will eat its fruit.*

The picture is agricultural and it runs across both verses: words are a crop, and
the person who grew them is the one who has to eat the harvest. What you say
comes back to your own table.

The Hebrew for *power* is literally **hand** — death and life are in the hand of
the tongue. It is a small organ given a large grip.

## What the surrounding verses are about

Look at the company it keeps in this chapter:

- *the first to plead his case seems right, until another comes and questions him*
- *a brother offended is harder to win than a fortified city*
- *the poor plead for mercy, but the rich answer with harsh words*

Every one is about speech between people — testimony, a quarrel that has hardened,
an answer given roughly to someone with no power. That is the field this proverb
grows in.

> **On how it gets used.** The verse is often taken to mean that spoken words
> shape outcomes by their own force. What the proverb states is narrower and
> harder to dismiss: your words do real damage or real good to other people, and
> you will live with the result. James says the same at length — a small rudder,
> a small fire — and both writers are talking about the tongue's effect on human
> beings, not on circumstances.
