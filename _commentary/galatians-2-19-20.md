*The life I now live in the flesh, I live by the faith **of** the Son of God, who
loved me and gave Himself up for me.*

That is how the older English versions render it, and it is the more literal
reading of the Greek. It is worth knowing why, because it changes what the verse
asks of you.

## The Greek

**Pistis Christou** — *faith of Christ*. The genitive most naturally reads as
belonging to Him: His faith, His faithfulness. Rendering it *faith in the Son of
God* requires taking the genitive as an object instead, which is possible but is
the less obvious reading of the words as they stand.

## Why it fits the sentence

Look at what Paul has just said, one clause earlier:

> *It is no longer I who live — Christ lives in me.*

He has already removed himself as the subject. To then say that the life is
sustained by the strength of his own believing would put him straight back at the
centre of a sentence he has just moved himself out of.

Read as *the faith of the Son of God*, the whole thing runs one direction: I
died, He lives in me, and the faith I live by is His.

## What that does to a struggling reader

It relocates the pressure. If the Christian life runs on the quality of my
trust, then every flat morning is a threat. If it runs on His faithfulness, then
my believing is a response to something already holding, not the thing doing the
holding.

Paul's language elsewhere runs the same way — *faithful is He who calls you, who
also will do it*, and *if we are faithless, He remains faithful, for He cannot
deny Himself.*

> **The four words at the end.** *Who loved me and gave Himself up for me.* Paul
> writes it singular, which he almost never does. In the middle of the most
> combative letter he wrote, the argument narrows to one man and the love aimed at
> him — which is the same move the whole verse makes.
