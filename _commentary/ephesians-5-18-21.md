One command, and four participles hanging off it.

## The prohibition

*Do not be drunk with wine, **which carries a person off into ruin**.*

The Greek is **asotia** — dissipation, wastefulness, an unsaved condition. Literally
*unsavedness*.

The same word is used of the prodigal's living: *he wasted his possessions with prodigal
living*.

## The command

*But **be filled with the Spirit**.*

The Greek is **plerousthe** — present, passive, plural, imperative.

Four things at once, and each one matters.

**Present**: keep on being filled, continuously. Not one event.

**Passive**: it is done to you.

**Plural**: addressed to a congregation, not an individual.

**Imperative**: it is a command, which means it is not optional and not automatic.

## Why the comparison

Wine and the Spirit are set against each other because both change how a person behaves.

The parallel is not accidental. It is the accusation at Pentecost: *these men are full of new
wine*. See the note on Acts 2:1–4.

Paul's point is about which influence is in control, and the difference in outcome — one wastes
a life, the other fills it.

## The first participle

*Speaking to one another in **psalms and hymns and songs of the Spirit**.*

The Greek is **lalountes heautois** — speaking to yourselves, or to each other.

Three kinds of song, and the first is the Psalter.

## The second

*Singing and **making melody in your heart** to the Lord.*

The Greek is **psallontes** — originally to pluck a stringed instrument, and by this period to
sing.

*In your heart* — the interior part is specified, alongside the audible part.

## The third

*Giving thanks **always and for all things** to God the Father in the name of our Lord Jesus
Christ.*

Two universals: always, and for everything.

## The fourth

*Submitting to one another in the fear of God.*

The Greek is **hupotassomenoi** — a military word for ranking under.

And it is the sentence that introduces the whole household section that follows: wives and
husbands, children and parents, servants and masters.

*Submitting to one another* comes first, and governs all of it.

## What the four have in common

None of them is an inward exercise.

Being filled with the Spirit produces, in Paul's account: singing to each other, singing
inwardly, thanking God, and deferring to people.

The evidence he lists is entirely audible and social.

## The parallel passage

Colossians has the same list with a different subject:

*Let **the word of Christ dwell in you richly** in all wisdom, teaching and admonishing one
another in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, singing with grace in your hearts to the
Lord. And whatever you do in word or deed, do all in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks
to God the Father through Him.*

Filled with the Spirit in Ephesians, the word dwelling richly in Colossians, and the outcome
described identically.

## What comes just before

*See then that you walk circumspectly, not as fools but as wise, **redeeming the time, because
the days are evil**. Therefore do not be unwise, but **understand what the will of the Lord
is**.*

And the will of the Lord, in the very next sentence, turns out to be this.

> **What the grammar carries.** Present, passive, plural, imperative — keep on being filled, by
> somebody else, together, and it is an order.
