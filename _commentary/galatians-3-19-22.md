Paul asks the question his own argument forces, and answers it in four parts.

## The question

*What purpose, then, does the law serve?*

He has just proved that the promise to Abraham came four hundred and thirty years before
Sinai, and that a later document cannot annul an earlier covenant.

Which leaves the obvious problem: then why was it given at all?

## The reason

*It was **added because of transgressions**.*

The Greek is **prosetethe** — added, placed alongside. Not the main thing.

**Because of transgressions** — the phrase is compressed and Paul unpacks it elsewhere:
*by the law is the knowledge of sin*, and *I would not have known sin except through the
law*. See the note on Romans 7:7–8.

## The term

*Until **the Seed should come to whom the promise had been made**.*

An expiry date, built into the answer.

Paul has already argued about that word: *He does not say, "And to seeds," as of many, but
as of one, "And to your Seed," who is Christ.*

## How it was delivered

*It was put in place **through angels, by the hand of a mediator**.*

Two intermediaries. Stephen says the same at his trial — *you who have received the law by
the direction of angels* — and Hebrews: *the word spoken through angels proved
steadfast*.

## The argument from arithmetic

*Now **a mediator is not a mediator for one party alone, but God is one**.*

One of the densest sentences Paul ever wrote, and the point is structural: a mediator
implies two sides. The law is an agreement with terms on both ends.

The promise to Abraham had one party. God swore it by Himself, and Abraham was asleep when
the covenant was cut. See the note on Genesis 22:15–18.

## The denial

*Is the law, then, **set against the promises of God? Certainly not**.*

The Greek is **me genoito** — Paul's strongest negative.

He has just spent a chapter setting them in opposition and refuses the conclusion.

## The concession he will not make

*For **if a law had been given that could give life, righteousness really would have come
by the law**.*

Conditional, contrary to fact. If such a law existed, Paul says he would say so.

The problem is not that the route is forbidden. It is that it does not go anywhere.

## What Scripture did instead

*But **the Scripture shut everything up together under sin**.*

The Greek is **sunekleisen** — locked up together, enclosed on all sides. It is used of a
net closing on fish.

Everything, together. Paul says the same in Romans: *God has committed them all to
disobedience, that He might have mercy on all.*

## The purpose

*So that **the promise, by faith in Jesus Christ, might be given to those who believe**.*

The shutting-up has a stated purpose, and the purpose is the giving.

## What comes next

*But before faith came, we were kept under guard by the law, **kept for the faith which
would afterward be revealed**.*

*Therefore the law was **our tutor to bring us to Christ**.*

The Greek is **paidagogos** — not a teacher, but the household slave who walked a boy to
school and kept him out of trouble on the way. The job ended when the boy came of age.

Paul carries the image straight into the next chapter. See the note on
Galatians 4:1–3.

> **What Paul refuses to say.** That the law is against the promises. He says it was added,
> that it had a term, that it came through two intermediaries, and that it could not give
> life — and then denies the conclusion anybody would draw.
