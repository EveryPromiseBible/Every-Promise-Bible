Written to Christians in Rome, about Rome.

## Who is being addressed

*Let **everyone** take their place under the authorities set above them.*

The Greek is **pasa psuche** — every soul. A Hebraism meaning every person, no exceptions
carved out for the religious.

## The claim

*For **there is no authority that does not come from God**, and the authorities that exist **have
been put in place by Him**.*

The Greek is **tetagmenai** — perfect passive of **tasso**, to arrange, to appoint to a station.
A military word for putting a man in his position.

## When this was written

Around AD 57, under Nero, in the early years before the persecution.

Within a decade the same emperor would be using Christians as garden lights, and Paul himself
would be executed under Roman authority.

Paul knew what Rome was. He had been beaten with rods three times and had already appealed to
Caesar.

## What is being said

That the institution of government is God's arrangement, not that any given ruler is good.

The passage says nothing about how rulers come to power and nothing about their character. It
speaks about the office.

## The consequence

*So whoever sets himself against the authority is **setting himself against what God has put in
place**.*

The Greek is **antitassomenos** — the same root as *appointed*, with *against* in front. Ranging
oneself against the arrangement.

## The general rule

*Rulers are **not a terror to good conduct, but to evil**.*

A statement of what government is for, phrased as a description.

Paul had been the beneficiary of it — Roman soldiers pulled him out of a lynching in Jerusalem,
and a Roman tribune moved him out of an ambush by night with two hundred men.

## The test

*Do you want to live without fear of the one in authority? **Then do what is good, and you will
have his commendation**.*

The ordinary case. Most people, most of the time, are not troubled by the magistrate.

## The title

*He is **God's servant for your good**.*

The Greek is **diakonos** — servant, minister. The word used of church officers.

Paul applies it to a pagan magistrate who has no idea he is holding it.

## The sword

*But if you do what is wrong, be afraid, for **he does not carry the sword for nothing**.*

The Greek is **machaira** — the short sword carried by Roman officials, the symbol of the power
to execute.

*He is God's servant, **carrying out wrath on the one who does wrong**.*

The same word **diakonos**, a second time, now attached to punishment.

## Why this is not vengeance

The paragraph immediately before: *beloved, do not avenge yourselves, but rather give place to
wrath, for it is written, "Vengeance is Mine, I will repay," says the Lord*. See the note on
Romans 12:17–21.

Christians are told not to retaliate, and then told who does hold the sword. The two paragraphs
are one argument, and the chapter break hides it.

## The two reasons

*This is why you must be subject — **not only because of wrath, but for the sake of conscience**.*

The Greek is **suneidesin** — conscience.

Fear of punishment is the lower reason and is acknowledged as real. The higher one is that a
believer understands what the arrangement is.

## The limit the rest of Scripture sets

*"**We ought to obey God rather than men**."* — Peter and the apostles, to the council that had
just ordered them to stop teaching. See the note on Acts 5:27–32.

The midwives who lied to Pharaoh, Daniel's three friends at the furnace, and Daniel at the open
window all disobeyed rulers, and Scripture commends every one of them.

Paul is stating the rule, not abolishing the cases.

## What follows

*Render therefore to all their due: taxes to whom taxes are due… **Owe no one anything except to
love one another**.* See the note on Romans 13:6–7.

The chapter about submission to Rome ends by saying the only permanent debt is love.

> **The word.** **Diakonos** — servant, minister — used twice of a pagan magistrate holding a
> sword he does not know whose it is.
