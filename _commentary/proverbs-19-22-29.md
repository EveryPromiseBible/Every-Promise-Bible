The first line of this passage names what everybody actually wants, and the word
it uses is the covenant word.

## The longing

*What a person longs for is **steady love**.*

The Hebrew is **chesed** — loyal love, covenant kindness, the word used of God's
own faithfulness some two hundred and fifty times.

The older rendering is *the desire of a man is his kindness*, and it has been read
both ways: what a man desires is kindness, and a man's desirability is his
kindness.

Either way the quality named is **chesed** — and it is not niceness. It is the
kindness of somebody keeping faith with you when they need not.

## And the second half

*And **better to be poor than a liar**.*

The pairing is the argument. Set a man's kindness against a man's means, and the
proverb says which one is worth having.

It is the same measure the whole book keeps applying: *better is a dinner of herbs
where love is, than a fattened calf with hatred.*

## The satisfied life

*The fear of the LORD leads to life, and one who has it **rests satisfied,
untouched by harm**.*

The Hebrew is *he abides satisfied* — lodges, stays the night, remains.

## The lazy man

*The lazy man **buries his hand in the dish and will not even lift it to his
mouth**.*

Proverbs is funny about laziness in a way it is about nothing else. He has got as
far as the food and cannot complete the journey.

## Correction, and who it works on

*Strike a scoffer, and **the simple learn caution**; correct a wise one, and **he
gains knowledge**.*

Three characters and three outcomes, and note who benefits from what.

Striking the scoffer does nothing for the scoffer. It teaches the onlooker.

The wise man is the only one in the verse who profits from correction directed at
him — which is Proverbs' definition of wisdom throughout: not knowing more, but
being correctable.

## The warning about drifting

*"**Stop listening to instruction, my son, and you will stray** from the words of
knowledge."*

Nothing dramatic is described. Just stopping listening, and then drift.

## The end of the passage

*Judgements are ready for the scoffers, and beatings for the backs of fools.*

The Hebrew is that they are prepared, already established. Nothing has to be
arranged; the consequence is standing by.

> **The verse in the middle of it all.** *A child who assaults his father and drives
> out his mother brings shame and disgrace.* Proverbs sits its bluntest sentence
> about family violence between a joke about a lazy man's dinner and a note on
> correction — the book keeps ordinary life and serious things in the same column.
