The chapter opens with a question about belief, not about suffering.

## Where the passage actually starts

Three verses earlier, at the end of chapter 52: *behold, My Servant shall deal prudently; He
shall be exalted and extolled and be very high*.

And then: *His visage was marred more than any man, and His form more than the sons of men*.

The chapter division falls in the middle of a poem.

## The question

*Who has believed **what we have heard**?*

The Hebrew is **shemu'atenu** — our report, our message.

Answered by John at the end of his Gospel: *but although He had done so many signs before them,
they did not believe in Him, that the word of Isaiah the prophet might be fulfilled which he
spoke: "Lord, who has believed our report?"*

And by Paul, about preaching: *so then faith comes by hearing, and hearing by the word of
God*. See the note on Romans 10:16–17.

## The second question

*And **to whom has the arm of the LORD been revealed**?*

The arm is God's own strength. Isaiah has used it: *the LORD has made bare His holy arm in the
eyes of all the nations*.

And a few chapters later, when nobody would intervene: *therefore His own arm brought
salvation for Him*. See the note on Isaiah 59:15b–17.

## The growing

*He grew up before Him **like a tender shoot**, like **a root out of dry ground**.*

The Hebrew is **yoneq** — a suckling, the small shoot that comes off a stump.

Isaiah has already used the image: *there shall come forth a Rod from the stem of Jesse*.

**Out of dry ground** — nothing about the soil accounts for it. The family line was a stump by
then; the house of David had not held a throne for centuries when this was fulfilled.

## The appearance

*He had **no beauty, no majesty to draw us to him**, nothing in his appearance that we should
desire him.*

The Hebrew is *no form nor comeliness*.

The one physical description of the Messiah in the Old Testament, and it says there was nothing
to see.

## What the bride says

*His mouth is all sweetness. **He is altogether lovely**.* See the note on
Song of Songs 5:14–16.

The Song's description runs to seven verses of gold, ivory, marble and cedar.

Isaiah says there was nothing in His appearance to desire.

Both are in the Old Testament, and the second is what people saw.

## The rejection

*He was **despised and rejected by men**.*

The Hebrew is **chadal ishim** — forsaken of men, ceasing from men.

## The two words

*A **man of sorrows**, and **well acquainted with grief**.*

The Hebrew is **ish makhovot** — a man of pains. And **yedua choli** — known of sickness.

**Makhov** is physical pain; **choli** is disease.

They are the two nouns that recur in the next verse: *surely He has borne our **griefs** and
carried our **sorrows**.* The same pair, and there the King James renders them the other way
round. See the note on Isaiah 53:4–6.

Matthew quotes that verse of a night of healings: *He Himself took our infirmities and bore our
sicknesses*. See the note on Matthew 8:14–17.

## The averted faces

*He was like **one from whom people hide their faces**.*

The Hebrew is *as one from whom men hide the face*.

The gesture people make around a leper, or a disfigurement.

## The verdict

*He was despised, and **we held him of no account**.*

The Hebrew is *we did not esteem Him* — reckoned Him as nothing.

## Who is speaking

*We.* All the way through — our report, we hid our faces, we did not esteem Him.

The passage is a confession. The people describing His rejection are the ones who did it, and
by verse 6 they are saying *all we like sheep have gone astray*.

> **The one physical description in the prophets.** No form, no comeliness, nothing to desire —
> and the Song of Songs, describing the same figure, spends seven verses on gold and cedar.
