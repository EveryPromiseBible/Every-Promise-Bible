The first recorded act of Samson's strength, and it happens where nobody can see
it.

## The anointing

*The Spirit of the LORD came powerfully upon him, and he tore the lion apart with
his bare hands.*

The Hebrew verb is about rushing or advancing on someone — it is used of an army
charging. It comes on Samson four times in his story, and every time something
physical follows immediately.

Note what it is not: not a mood, not an inward sense. Judges reports it as a
force arriving and a thing being done.

## "As easily as one might tear a young goat"

The comparison is deliberately domestic. Tearing a kid was something a farmhand
did without thinking about it.

Whatever the strength was, the narrator describes it as effortless rather than
strained.

## The silence

*And he told no one, not even his parents, what he had done.*

That is the sentence worth stopping on. Samson's whole story is otherwise a
catalogue of showing off — riddles, boasts, a secret eventually talked out of
him by a woman.

Here, at the start, he says nothing. His parents were walking a little way ahead
and never knew.

## What he was doing there

*He went on and spoke with the woman, and she was right in his eyes.*

The chapter is not presenting Samson as a model. He is on his way to a marriage
his parents objected to, in Philistine territory, and the phrase *right in his
eyes* is the book's own shorthand for what was wrong with that whole era —
*everyone did what was right in his own eyes.*

> **What the lion becomes.** He goes back that way later, finds honey in the
> carcass, and builds a riddle out of it that starts the feud with the
> Philistines. The private victory turns into the public trouble. Judges keeps
> reporting both without comment.
