Five proverbs, and the last one is the famous one.

## The king

*A king who **judges the poor with truth** will have **his throne made sure forever**.*

The Hebrew is *judges the poor in truth*.

The stability of a throne is attached to how it treats the people with no leverage.

## The rod

*The rod and reproof give wisdom, but **a child left to himself shames his mother**.*

The Hebrew is *a child sent away* — left to his own devices.

The mother is named rather than the father, which Proverbs does more than once.

## The wicked

*When the wicked increase, **so does wrongdoing**, but the righteous will watch their
downfall.*

## The rest

*Discipline your child, and **he will give you rest**; he will **bring delight to your
soul**.*

The Hebrew for *delight* is **maadanim** — delicacies, dainties.

## The famous line

*Where there is **no vision**, **the people cast off restraint**.*

## The two halves

The Hebrew for *vision* is **chazon** — a prophetic vision, the disclosed word. It is the
word used of Isaiah's *vision*, and of Nathan and Samuel receiving one.

Not imagination or planning. Revelation.

And *cast off restraint* is **para** — to let loose, to run wild, to go unbridled.

It is the word used of Israel at the golden calf: *Moses saw that the people were
unrestrained*.

The older rendering — *the people perish* — comes from an older reading of the verb. The
Hebrew is about a people breaking ranks, not about death.

## What the second half says

*But **happy is the one who keeps the law**.*

The Hebrew is **ashrei** — blessed, happy. The word that opens Psalm 1.

The verse is a pair: no revealed word, and a people running loose; the law kept, and a
person content.

Both halves are about what a word does to conduct.

## Where the word appears elsewhere

*Then the LORD answered me and said: "**Write the vision** and make it plain on tablets,
that he may run who reads it. For **the vision is yet for an appointed time**… though it
tarries, wait for it; because **it will surely come**."*

Habakkuk, and the same noun.

And 1 Samuel 3, of the period before Samuel: *the word of the LORD was rare in those days;
**there was no widespread revelation**.* See the note on 1 Samuel 3:1–3.

That is the condition the proverb describes, stated as history.

## What happened in that period

Judges — a book whose refrain is *in those days there was no king in Israel; **everyone did
what was right in his own eyes***.

Which is the proverb's first half, described from the inside.

## What the chapter ends on

*An unjust man is an abomination to the righteous, and he who is upright in the way is an
abomination to the wicked.*

The last verse of Proverbs 29, and it works both ways.

> **What the verse is about.** Not ambition or planning. **Chazon** is a word received —
> and the proverb's claim is that when it is absent a people does not die, it comes
> unbridled.
