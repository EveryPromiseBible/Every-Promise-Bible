Three verses after the hardest warning in the letter, the writer says he does not think
it applies to them.

## The address

*But **dear friends**.*

The Greek is **agapetoi** — beloved. It is the only time in the whole letter the writer
uses it.

He saves it for the sentence directly after the warning.

## The concession

*Even when **we talk like this**, we are **convinced of better things where you are
concerned**.*

The Greek is **pepeismetha** — perfect passive, the same word Paul uses in Romans 8: *I
am fully persuaded*. See the note on Romans 8:38–39.

A settled conviction, not a hope.

## What the better things are

*The things that **come with being saved**.*

The Greek is *things that accompany salvation*.

He names the category rather than listing it, and then gives his evidence.

## The evidence

*God is **not unjust**. He will **not forget the work you did and the love you put into
it** for His name.*

The argument is about God's character, not their performance.

**Not unjust** — the writer puts it negatively. It would be an injustice for God to
overlook it, so He will not.

## What they had done

*Looking after His people, **as you are still doing**.*

The Greek is *having ministered to the saints and still ministering*.

Two tenses. There is a record, and it is ongoing.

Chapter 10 fills it in: *you had compassion on me in my chains, and **joyfully accepted
the plundering of your goods**, knowing that you have a better and enduring possession
for yourselves.*

They had visited prisoners and lost their property for it.

## Why the warning was given at all

Not because they had defected, but because the letter has just described them as
sluggish: *you have become dull of hearing… you need someone to teach you again the
first principles… you have come to need milk and not solid food.*

The warning is aimed at where drifting ends up, and the writer says plainly he does not
think they are there.

## What he asks for next

*And we desire that **each one of you show the same diligence** to the full assurance of
hope until the end, that you do not become sluggish, but **imitate those who through
faith and patience inherit the promises**.*

**Full assurance** — the Greek is **plerophoria**, full carrying, complete certainty.

And then he names one of the imitators: Abraham. See the note on Hebrews 6:13–14.

## The shape of the chapter

Warning, then *but beloved*, then Abraham's oath, then two unchangeable things, then an
anchor behind the veil.

The chapter with the sharpest edge in it ends with the strongest guarantee in the letter.

## The anchor at the end of the argument

Follow the paragraph a few verses further and you find what the writer's confidence is finally
tied to. He speaks of *strong consolation* for people who *have fled for refuge*, and then:

*This hope we have as **an anchor of the soul, both sure and steadfast**, and which enters the
Presence behind the veil, where **the forerunner has entered for us, even Jesus**.*

An anchor normally goes down into a seabed you cannot see. This one goes up, through the veil, into
the holiest place — and it is fastened to a Person who is already standing there on our behalf.

The chapter that contains the sternest warning in the letter ends by naming what holds, and it is
not the readers.

> **What the writer bases his confidence on.** Not their record — he has just called
> them dull of hearing. God's justice, and the fact that there is something already done
> that it would be unjust to forget.
