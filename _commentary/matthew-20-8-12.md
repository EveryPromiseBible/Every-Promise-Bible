A landowner hires men all day at different hours and pays every one of them the
same. The detail that makes the parable work is the order of the queue.

## "Beginning with the last ones hired"

He could have paid the first group first, sent them home content, and settled with
the rest quietly. Nobody would have known.

He deliberately arranges it so the men who worked twelve hours watch the men who
worked one receive a full day's wage.

That is not carelessness. He wants them to see it.

## What the first group then expects

*When the ones hired first came up, they assumed they would get more.*

Nothing had been said to suggest it. They had agreed a denarius at dawn — the
standard day's wage, a fair rate, exactly what they asked for.

The expectation is generated entirely by watching somebody else be treated
generously.

## The complaint

*You have made them equal to us, who carried the weight of the whole day and the
burning heat.*

The grievance is not that they were underpaid. It is the word **equal**. They
object to the last men being brought level with them.

That is the sharp end of the parable: generosity to somebody else felt like an
injury, in men who had received precisely what they were promised.

> **The landowner's answer.** *Am I not allowed to do what I want with what is
> mine? Or is your eye evil because I am good?* The Greek idiom is about a
> grudging, envious eye. And the parable ends where the chapter before it ended —
> the last will be first, and the first last. Jesus tells it to disciples who had
> just asked what they would get for having left everything.
