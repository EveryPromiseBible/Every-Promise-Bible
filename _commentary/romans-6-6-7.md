Paul says it is something they already know.

## The opening

*We **know this**.*

The Greek is **ginoskontes** — knowing. He is not introducing information; he is reminding
them of the ground of an argument.

Romans 6 is full of it: *do you not know*, *knowing this*, *we know*. Five times in the
chapter.

## The event

*The **person we used to be was crucified with Him**.*

The Greek is **sunestaurothe** — co-crucified. One word, aorist passive.

Aorist: a point in the past. Passive: done to him.

Paul uses the same compound of himself in Galatians: *I have been crucified with Christ; it
is no longer I who live*. See the note on Galatians 2:19–20.

## What the old self is

*The person we used to be* — the Greek is **ho palaios hemon anthropos**, our old man.

Not the sinful nature as a component. The whole former person, the one who was in Adam.

Paul's other uses run the same way: *put off the old man with his deeds*, and *put off,
concerning your former conduct, the old man*. See the note on Ephesians 4:20–24.

## The purpose

*So that **the body sin had ruled might be left with no power at all**.*

The Greek is **katargethe** — rendered inoperative, put out of employment. The same verb
Paul uses of the law and of death itself.

Not destroyed. Unemployed.

The body is still there. What is gone is its position as the place sin gives orders from.

## The result

*And we might **no longer be slaves to sin**.*

The Greek is **douleuein** — to serve as a slave.

Slavery is the metaphor for the whole chapter, and Paul apologises for it in verse 19: *I
speak in human terms because of the weakness of your flesh*.

## The legal principle

*For **anyone who has died has been set free from sin**.*

The Greek is **dedikaiotai** — has been justified. The word Paul has been using for five
chapters about righteousness, used here of a corpse.

A dead man has no further liability. The claims against him do not follow him.

## What the chapter is answering

*Shall we continue in sin that grace may abound?* See the note on Romans 6:1–2.

The answer is not a threat. It is a description: *how shall we who died to sin live any
longer in it?*

Paul answers a question about behaviour with a statement about a death that has already
happened.

## The two orders

*Knowing that Christ, having been raised from the dead, **dies no more. Death no longer has
dominion over Him**… likewise you also, **reckon yourselves to be dead indeed to sin, but
alive to God**.* See the note on Romans 6:8–11.

The Greek for *reckon* is **logizesthe** — an accounting word. Count it, enter it in the
book as true.

It is an imperative, and it is the first imperative in the chapter. Five verses of what
happened, and then the instruction is to treat it as a fact.

## Where the instructions start

*Therefore do not let sin reign in your mortal body… **do not present your members as
instruments of unrighteousness**.*

Verse 12. And the reason given, in verse 14: *for **sin shall not have dominion over you**,
for you are not under law but under grace.*

The command in verse 12 rests on the promise in verse 14, and not the other way round.

## What was buried

*Therefore we were buried with Him through baptism into death, that just as Christ was
raised from the dead by the glory of the Father, even so we also should **walk in newness of
life**.*

Died, buried, raised — Paul runs the whole sequence through the reader.

> **What happened to the body.** Not destroyed. The Greek says put out of work — sin's
> employer status revoked, and the man himself listed among the dead, which is why the
> claims stop.
