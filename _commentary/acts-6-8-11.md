They lose the argument, so they change the subject to blasphemy.

## Who he was

*Stephen, **full of faith and power**, did **great wonders and signs** among the people.*

Seven men had been chosen a few verses earlier to *serve tables* — to sort out a complaint
that the Greek-speaking widows were being overlooked in the daily distribution.

Stephen is first on the list, and the qualification given was *full of the Holy Spirit and
wisdom*.

He was appointed to administer a food programme and Luke records him doing signs.

## The opposition

*But **some men stood up and argued with him**.*

The Greek is **sunzetountes** — disputing, debating.

## Who they were

*Men from the synagogue called **the Synagogue of the Freedmen**, along with **Cyrenians and
Alexandrians** and others from **Cilicia and Asia**.*

**Freedmen** — the Greek is **Libertinon**, from the Latin. Descendants of Jews taken to
Rome as slaves and later freed, who had come back.

A Greek-speaking, diaspora synagogue. These are Stephen's own people, arguing with him in
their own language.

**Cilicia** is Tarsus, and Saul of Tarsus is standing at the end of this chapter holding the
coats.

## What happened

*They **could not hold their own against the wisdom and the Spirit by which he spoke**.*

The Greek is **ouk ischuon antistenai** — they were not strong enough to stand against.

It is Luke's fulfilment of a promise: *I will give you a mouth and wisdom which **all your
adversaries will not be able to contradict or resist***. See the note on Luke 21:10–19.

Same verb, same author, deliberate.

## The response

*So they **quietly arranged for men to say**.*

The Greek is **hupebalon** — suborned, put men up to it. It is the word for smuggling
something in underneath.

They lost in public and organised a lie in private.

## The charge

*"We have heard him speak **blasphemous words against Moses and against God**."*

Moses first.

The next two verses add the rest: *this man does not cease to speak blasphemous words
against this holy place and the law*.

The same two charges brought against Jesus — the temple and the law — and both times the
witnesses are described as arranged.

## What his face looked like

*And all who sat in the council, looking steadfastly at him, **saw his face as the face of
an angel**.*

The last line of the chapter, and the high priest's question comes in the next verse: *are
these things so?*

## What he does with the question

He answers it with the longest speech in Acts — fifty-three verses of Israel's history,
from Abraham in Mesopotamia to Solomon's temple.

And the argument runs: God met Abraham in Mesopotamia, Joseph in Egypt, Moses in Midian and
at a bush in the desert. *The Most High does not dwell in temples made with hands.*

The charge was that he spoke against the holy place. He answers by showing that God kept
turning up outside it.

## The end of it

*"You **stiff-necked and uncircumcised in heart and ears!** You always resist the Holy
Spirit."*

And then: *I see the heavens opened and the Son of Man **standing** at the right hand of
God.*

Everywhere else in the New Testament He is seated. Stephen sees Him on His feet.

## What the stoning produced

*And Saul was consenting to his death.*

And: *at that time a great persecution arose against the church which was at Jerusalem; and
they were all scattered… **therefore those who were scattered went everywhere preaching the
word**.*

The first killing of a Christian is the thing that gets the message out of the city.

> **Why the charge was invented.** Luke says it plainly: they could not hold their own in
> the argument. The blasphemy accusation is what happened after the debate was lost.
