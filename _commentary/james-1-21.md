---
key: James 1:21
title: What "saved" actually covers
---

Our text says the message has power to save your souls. Both of those words — saved and souls — are wider in Greek than they have become in English.

## The same word, in this same letter, means healed

The Greek verb is sozo. James uses it again at the end of his letter: the prayer offered in faith will sozo the sick man. Same verb. There it plainly means made well — a man getting up off his bed.

So inside one short letter James uses this one word for the rescue of a person's whole life, and for a sick man being restored. That is not a stretch or a clever reading. It is simply how he uses it.

## It is a rescue word, not a religious word

Sozo means to bring someone safely through. It is used of a drowning man being pulled out, of a ship that survives a storm, of someone brought out of danger in one piece. Whatever the threat is, sozo is coming through it intact.

English saved has narrowed to mean one thing: not going to hell. The Greek word never narrowed like that.

## And "souls" is wider too

The word is psyche. In English, soul makes us think only of the invisible part that outlives the body. In Greek it means the life, the self, the whole person — you.

When Jesus asked what it profits a man to gain the whole world and lose his psyche, He was not talking about one compartment of a person.

So save your souls is not keep the spiritual part of you out of hell. It is nearer to: rescue the whole of you, and bring you through in one piece.

> Do not overreach with this. Sozo genuinely does include eternal salvation — that is not being denied here, and this is not a case of everyone having had the word wrong. The claim that holds is that saved is WIDER than we usually hear it, not that it means something other than we thought. And the width of a word does not by itself promise a particular outcome in a particular case. What it tells you is the size of what God is dealing with: not a compartment of you, but you.
