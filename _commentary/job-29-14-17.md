Job's longest speech, remembering what he used to be, and it is worth counting the
pronouns.

## The garment

*"**I wrapped myself** in righteousness, and it clothed me; justice was my robe
and my crown."*

He put it on himself. That is what the verb says, and the whole passage is built
the same way.

Isaiah describes a different arrangement: *He has clothed me with the garments of
salvation, He has covered me with the robe of righteousness.* Somebody else does
the dressing.

Job's robe is one he wove and wrapped around himself, and every word of it is
true — he really was that man. Chapter 1 said so and God said so.

## What he had done

*"I was **eyes to the blind** and **feet to the lame**. I was **a father to the
needy**, and when a stranger came with a case I did not know, **I searched it out
for him**."*

The record is extraordinary. He was not boasting about property or influence — he
is listing the blind, the lame, the poor and the stranger.

*"I broke the jaws of the wicked and pulled the prey from between his teeth."*

## What has happened to him

Read the chapter as a whole and count. In chapter 29, Job refers to himself well
over forty times and to God about five.

That is not how he began. Chapter 1: *the LORD gave and the LORD has taken away;
blessed be the name of the LORD.* Chapter 2: *shall we accept good from God and
not trouble?*

Between there and here, three men have spent twenty-five chapters telling him his
suffering is his own fault. He has had to defend himself for so long that he has
become his own subject.

That is what preaching from experience, tradition and a formula does to the man
underneath it. It does not produce repentance. It produces a case for the defence.

## And what it turns into

By the next chapter he is looking down on men whose fathers he *would not have
trusted even with my sheepdogs.* See the note on Job 30:1–4.

The bitterness and the pride arrive together.

## Where it ends

He is still in it at the end of chapter 31: *let the Almighty answer me.*

And then the Almighty does, out of a whirlwind, and Job's answer is *I have heard
of You by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees You.* The robe he wrapped
himself in comes off the moment he actually sees Him. See the note on
Job 42:1–3.

## The garment he describes

*"**I put on righteousness, and it clothed me**; my justice was like a robe and a turban."*

The Hebrew has him doing the dressing. He put it on, and everything he lists after it is
true — he was eyes to the blind, feet to the lame, a father to the poor, and he broke the fangs
of the wicked.

None of it is boasting that can be disproved. It is an accurate account of a good life.

## What happens to that garment

By chapter 30 it is gone, and by chapter 42 he is saying *I abhor myself, and repent in dust
and ashes*.

Isaiah says what happens to that garment: *we are all like an unclean thing, and **all our
righteousnesses are like filthy rags**.*

**Paul** had the same wardrobe — *concerning the righteousness which is in the law,
blameless* — and called it loss.

## The other robe

*"**He has clothed me with the garments of salvation, He has covered me with the robe of
righteousness**, as a bridegroom decks himself with ornaments."*

Isaiah's verbs are all His. Nobody puts that one on themselves.

*For **He made Him who knew no sin to be sin for us**, that we might become the righteousness
of God in Him.*

*Bring out the best robe and put it on him* — said by a father over a son who had come home
with a speech about being unworthy, and never got to finish it.

> **Why the chapter is here.** It is not a villain's speech. Every claim in it is
> true, and God will publicly call him *My servant Job* four times over in chapter
> 42. It is what a genuinely good man sounds like after twenty-five chapters of
> being told to justify himself.
