Paul tells a young minister what to feed on and what to train for.

## The instruction

*Keep **laying all of this before the brothers**.*

The Greek is **hupotithemenos** — to set before, to suggest. It is a gentle word; not
commanding, but putting something in front of people.

## The diet

*A man **fed and grown strong on the words of the faith** and on **the good teaching you have
followed closely all along**.*

The Greek is **entrephomenos** — nourished on, brought up on. A word about long feeding
rather than a meal.

**Followed closely** — **parekolouthekas**, to follow alongside, to trace out.

## What to leave

*Turn away from **godless tales and the old fireside stories people love to repeat**.*

The Greek is **bebelous kai graodeis muthous** — profane and old-womanish myths.

The letter has already named the problem: *fables and endless genealogies, which cause
disputes rather than godly edification*.

## The verb

*Train yourself **the way an athlete trains**.*

The Greek is **gumnaze** — the root of *gymnasium*. It meant training naked, as Greek
athletes did.

A word from the gymnasium, used to a man in Ephesus where the games were part of civic
life.

## What to train for

*And train for **a life turned toward God**.*

The Greek is **eusebeia** — godliness, reverence. It is the theme word of both letters to
Timothy; Paul uses it ten times.

## The comparison

*Working the body **has its uses, but they are small ones**.*

The Greek is *bodily exercise profits for a little*.

Paul does not dismiss it. He grants the benefit and measures it.

## The other side

*A life turned toward God is **useful in every direction**.*

The Greek is *profitable for all things*.

## The reason

*For it carries **a promise both for the life we are living now and for the life that is
coming**.*

Two lives named, and the promise attaches to both.

The letter says the same about wealth two chapters later: *God gives us richly all things to
enjoy*. See the note on 1 Timothy 6:17–19.

## What godliness is, in this letter

Not behaviour. Paul defines it in chapter 3, and it is a person:

*And beyond all argument, **the secret at the heart of a godly life is a great one: God was
shown to us in a human body**.* See the note on 1 Timothy 3:16.

The training is toward that.

## What the false version looks like

Two chapters on: *people who imagine that **godliness is a way of getting on in the
world**.* See the note on 1 Timothy 6:3–5.

Same word, put to a different use, and Paul says to keep away from them.

## What comes next

*Do not let anyone dismiss you because you are young.* And: *give your attention to the
reading of the Scriptures aloud, to calling people up to what is right, to teaching.* See
the note on 1 Timothy 4:11–13.

And then: *meditate on these things; give yourself entirely to them, so that **your progress
may be evident to all**.*

## The saying

*This is a faithful saying and worthy of all acceptance.*

Paul uses that formula five times across the pastoral letters, and it appears to mark
something the churches already said.

## Who does the training

Paul says *train yourself*, and it would be easy to hear that as the one place in his letters where
the work comes back to us. It is not, and he names the trainer elsewhere:

*For **the grace of God that brings salvation has appeared** to all men, **teaching us** that,
denying ungodliness and worldly lusts, we should live soberly, righteously, and godly in the present
age.*

The verb there is **paideuousa** — the word for the training of a child, the same family as the
gymnasium. **Grace is the instructor.** Not the syllabus, not the reward at the end — the one
standing in the gymnasium doing the coaching.

Which is why the godliness Paul is aiming at is not a set of behaviours at all but a Person shown in
a human body. You train toward Christ, under the tuition of grace, and the strength for it is His:
*it is God who works in you both to will and to do for His good pleasure.*

> **What Paul grants and what he measures.** Bodily training is useful — for a little. The
> comparison is not against exercise; it is a proportion, with a promise attached to one
> side covering two lives.
