The largest crowd He ever had walks away, and He asks the twelve whether they want to as well.

## What has happened

Five thousand fed, and the crowd coming back the next day wanting more: *you seek Me… because
you ate of the loaves and were filled*.

Then the bread discourse, which gets progressively harder — *I am the bread of life*, then *the
bread that I shall give is My flesh*, then *unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink
His blood, you have no life in you*.

And the reaction: *this is a hard saying; who can understand it?* See the note on
John 6:60–65.

## The departure

*From that time on, **many of His disciples turned back and no longer walked with Him**.*

The Greek is **apelthon eis ta opiso** — went away to the things behind.

**Disciples**, not the crowd. Learners who had been following.

This is the largest defection recorded in the Gospels, and it comes at the peak of His
popularity — the day before, they had wanted to make Him king by force.

## What He does about it

Nothing.

He does not soften the saying, run after them, or explain it more accessibly.

## The question

*So Jesus said to the Twelve, "**Do you want to leave too?**"*

The Greek expects the answer no — **me kai humeis thelete hupagein** — but it is a real
question, and He asks it out loud with the room emptying.

## Peter's answer

*"**Lord, to whom would we go? You have the words of eternal life**."*

He does not claim to have understood the discourse.

His answer is comparative: there is nowhere else. The alternatives have been considered and
found empty.

## The confession

*"And **we have come to believe, and we know**, that You are the Messiah, the Son of the living
God."*

The Greek is **pepisteukamen kai egnokamen** — we have believed and have come to know.

Both perfect tenses. Believing first, and then knowing.

The order is the interesting part, and it is the reverse of the one usually assumed.

## The reply

*"**Did I not choose you, the Twelve? And one of you is a devil**."*

The Greek is **diabolos** — slanderer, accuser.

Peter has just made the best confession in the chapter, and the answer names a traitor in the
room.

## The identification

*He was speaking of **Judas Iscariot**, the son of Simon — for he was the one who would betray
Him, and **he was one of the Twelve**.*

John adds the last clause. Not an outsider. One of the twelve.

## What John does with this

It is the same construction he uses at the table: *one of you is going to hand Me over*, and the
disciples looking at one another with no idea. See the note on
John 13:21–26.

Nobody spots him. Not here, and not two years later.

## The word for turning back

The same verb John uses when the soldiers in the garden *drew back and fell to the ground*.

## What was lost

A crowd that had wanted to crown Him, reduced to twelve, one of whom is named as the problem.

The chapter opens with five thousand men and ends with a question addressed to eleven honest
men and one who is not.

> **What Peter does not claim.** To have understood it. His answer is that the alternatives have
> been checked — *to whom would we go?* — and the believing comes before the knowing in his own
> sentence.
