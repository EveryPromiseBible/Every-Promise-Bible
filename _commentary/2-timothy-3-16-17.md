Paul's clearest statement about Scripture, written from a cell, in his last
letter.

## The word

*Every Scripture is **breathed out by God**.*

The Greek is **theopneustos** — from *theos* (God) and *pneo* (to breathe). It
appears once in the New Testament, here, and Paul may have coined it.

Note the direction. Not that God breathed *into* the writings, but that they were
breathed *out* — the Scripture is the exhalation.

Genesis 2 has God breathing into a man's nostrils and the man becoming a living
soul. The same picture, applied to a text.

## "Every"

The Greek can be read *all Scripture* or *every Scripture*. Either way there is no
subset — no clause exempting the genealogies, the census lists, the building
specifications, or the parts nobody preaches from.

## What it is useful for

Four things, in sequence:

- **teaching** — showing what is true
- **showing us where we have gone wrong** — the Greek is conviction, exposure
- **setting us straight again** — restoration, putting back upright
- **training us in the life that is right** — the word is *paideia*, the whole
  business of raising a child

The middle two are worth the order they are in. The exposure comes before the
correction, and the correction is described as being set back on your feet rather
than knocked off them.

## What it produces

*So that the one who belongs to God may be **complete, fully equipped** for every good
work.*

The Greek for *complete* is **artios** — fitted, in working order. And *fully
equipped* is the same root intensified: outfitted, supplied.

It is the vocabulary of a ship rigged for a voyage or a workman with his tools.

## Which Scripture Paul meant

Two verses earlier: *from childhood you have known **the Holy Scriptures**, which are
able to make you wise for salvation.*

Timothy's childhood Bible was the Old Testament. Paul says those writings can make a
man wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus.

## How much weight a single word can carry

Paul does it himself in Galatians 3: *it does not say, "and to seeds," as though to
many, but as of one — **and to your Seed**, who is Christ.* An argument on a singular
noun. See the note on Galatians 3:15–18.

Jesus did the same to the Sadducees, on a present-tense verb: *I **am** the God of
Abraham* — therefore *He is not the God of the dead, but of the living.* See the note
on Matthew 22:23–33.

Both of them read Scripture as though the individual words were load-bearing, and
both won the argument that way.

## Where Paul was when he wrote it

*Bring the cloak that I left with Carpus at Troas — and the books, especially the
parchments.*

Same letter, two chapters later. A man in a Roman cell asking for his coat and his
scrolls.

> **The verse that follows the famous one about the last days.** Paul lists the
> difficult times ahead — *lovers of themselves, lovers of money, boasters* — and his
> instruction to Timothy about it is *but you must continue in the things you have
> learned.* The remedy offered for a bad age is a book.
