The two debts in the story are not comparable, and the whole parable turns on the gap.

## What prompted it

*"Lord, how often shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? **Up to seven times**?"*

*"I do not say to you, up to seven times, but **up to seventy times seven**."* See the note on
Matthew 18:21–22.

Peter offers double the rabbinic three, plus one. The answer is a number nobody counts to.

## The first debt

*"A man was brought to him who owed him **ten thousand bags of gold**."*

The Greek is **muriōn talantōn** — ten thousand talents.

**Murias** is the largest number in Greek arithmetic. **Talanton** is the largest currency unit.

The biggest number attached to the biggest denomination. The phrase is not a sum; it is the end of
the number system.

## The scale

A talent was roughly twenty years' wages for a labourer.

The annual tax revenue of Galilee and Perea together ran to a couple of hundred talents.

The man owes more than the province.

## The sentence

*"His master ordered that **he be sold, along with his wife and his children and everything he
owned**."*

Standard practice, and it would not have made a dent in the figure.

## What he asks for

*"**Be patient with me**, and **I will pay you back everything**."*

The Greek is **makrothumeson ep' emoi** — be long-tempered toward me.

He asks for time. He does not ask for cancellation.

## What he gets

*"And the master's heart went out to him. He let the man go and **canceled the whole debt**."*

The Greek is **splanchnistheis** — moved in the bowels, the gut-level verb Matthew uses of Jesus
looking at crowds.

And **to daneion apheken** — he forgave the loan.

He asked for an extension and was given a cancellation.

## The second debt

*"He found one of his fellow servants who owed him **a hundred silver coins**."*

The Greek is **hekaton dēnaria** — a hundred denarii. About a hundred days' wages.

Real money. A labourer's third of a year.

## The ratio

Ten thousand talents is six hundred thousand times a hundred denarii.

The parable does not say the second debt was nothing. It says it was real, and small enough to be
paid.

## The identical words

*"**Be patient with me, and I will pay you back**."*

The same sentence the first servant used, minus one word.

He hears his own prayer said back to him and does not recognise it.

## What he does

*"He **seized him and took him by the throat**."*

The Greek is **epnigen** — he was choking him. The same root as the thorns choking the seed.

## The witnesses

*"When the other servants saw what had happened, **they were deeply grieved**."*

The Greek is **elupēthēsan sphodra** — grieved exceedingly.

## The charge

*"**You wicked servant**… I canceled all that debt of yours **because you begged me to**. Should you
not have had mercy on your fellow servant, **just as I had mercy on you**?"*

The Greek is **hōs kagō se ēleēsa** — as I also had mercy on you.

The standard is not fairness. It is the treatment he had already received.

## The ending

*"And in his anger his master handed him over to **the jailers until he should pay back everything he
owed**."*

Which, at ten thousand talents, is a sentence with no end date.

## The last line

*"That is how My heavenly Father will deal with each of you, **unless you forgive your brother from
your heart**."*

The Greek is **apo tōn kardiōn humōn**.

## What the man never did

Nothing in the story says he disbelieved the cancellation.

He walked out of the room debt-free and went looking for a hundred denarii. The parable's account
of him is that he had it and did not have it in him.

## Where Paul puts the same logic

*Be kind to one another, tenderhearted, **forgiving one another, even as God in Christ forgave
you**.* See the note on Ephesians 4:30–32.

The order in Paul is the same as the master's question: as, therefore.

> **The arithmetic.** Ten thousand talents against a hundred denarii — the largest number in Greek
> against a third of a year's wages. The parable makes the second debt real, and still that small.
