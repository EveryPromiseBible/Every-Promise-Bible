The last letter Paul wrote opens with a promise of life, from a man about to lose his.

## Where he is

In Rome, in a second imprisonment, chained — *for which I suffer trouble as an evildoer, even to
the point of chains*.

Four chapters later: *the time of my departure is at hand*. See the note on
2 Timothy 4:6–8.

## The commission

*From Paul, an apostle of Jesus Christ **because God willed it so**.*

The Greek is **dia thelematos Theou** — through the will of God.

The first letter said *under orders*. This one says *because God willed it*, which is the same
claim made more quietly.

## The clause nobody expects here

*And **everything about it lines up with the promise of life that is ours in Christ Jesus**.*

The Greek is **kat' epangelian zoes** — according to the promise of life.

Paul is writing under a death sentence, and defines his apostleship by a promise of life.

The greeting itself is the argument.

## The address

*To Timothy, **a son I love dearly**.*

The Greek is **agapeto tekno** — beloved child.

The first letter said **gnesio tekno**, legitimate child — a legal word about standing. See the
note on 1 Timothy 1:1–2.

By the last letter the legal word has become an affectionate one.

## The greeting

*Grace and mercy and peace be yours from God the Father and from Christ Jesus our Lord.*

Three words again, with **mercy** in the middle — the same insertion he makes for Timothy and
for nobody else.

## What follows immediately

*I thank God… as **without ceasing I remember you in my prayers night and day**, greatly desiring
to see you, **being mindful of your tears**.* See the note on 2 Timothy 1:3–5.

He remembers the young man crying, probably at their last parting.

And then the grandmother and the mother, named.

## What he asks for next

*Stir up the gift of God which is in you through the laying on of my hands.*

The Greek is **anazopurein** — to fan into flame again.

*For God has not given us a spirit of fear, but of power and of love and of a sound mind.* See
the note on 2 Timothy 1:6–7.

## What he asks for at the end

*Bring the cloak that I left with Carpus at Troas — and **the books, especially the parchments**.*

*Do your utmost to come before winter.*

The letter that opens with the promise of life closes with a request for a coat and a set of
notebooks, and a man hoping his son gets there before the shipping stops.

## What the whole letter is doing

*Be strong in the grace that is in Christ Jesus. And the things that you have heard from me…
commit these to faithful men.* See the note on 2 Timothy 2:1–2.

Handing it on, from a cell, to a son.

> **The clause in the greeting.** *According to the promise of life* — written by a man who knows
> the sentence is coming and opens his last letter with life anyway.
