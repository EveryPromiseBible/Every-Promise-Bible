Paul in front of the council, and the exchange goes badly within two sentences.

## The opening line

*Brothers, I have lived my life before God with a completely clear conscience up
to this day.*

He addresses the Sanhedrin as equals — *brothers*, not *rulers* — and claims a
clear conscience, which is not a modest thing to say to a court.

He is struck on the mouth for it.

## The reply

*God is going to strike you, you whitewashed wall. You sit there to judge me
according to the law, and you break the law by ordering me to be struck?*

The legal point is exact. Striking an unconvicted defendant was illegal, and Paul
names the contradiction precisely.

The insult is also exact. **Whitewashed wall** — clean on the outside, structurally
unsound; the same image Jesus used of whitewashed tombs.

## What happens next

Bystanders tell him he is reviling God's high priest, and Paul answers: *I did
not know, brothers, that he was the high priest* — and then quotes Exodus about
not speaking evil of a ruler of your people.

He does not defend the outburst. He retracts it on the spot, on the basis of a
text.

## Why Luke includes it

He did not have to. This is Paul at his least impressive — losing his temper, and
then discovering he had insulted the wrong man.

Luke reports his hero's failures the way the Gospels report Peter's.

> **Whether he really did not know.** Readers have wondered for centuries: poor
> eyesight, a long absence from Jerusalem, or an irregular seating arrangement.
> The text does not say. What it does record is a man who spoke first, was
> corrected, and immediately took it back — which is the more useful half of the
> story.
