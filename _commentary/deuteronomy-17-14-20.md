Legislation for a monarchy four hundred years before there is one.

## The anticipated request

*And you say, "**Let us set a king over us like all the nations around us**."*

The Hebrew is **kechol-haggoyim asher sevivotai**.

Samuel hears exactly that sentence when it comes: *now make us a king to judge us **like all the
nations***, and *the thing displeased Samuel*.

The law records the request in advance, in the words they will use.

## What is permitted

*Then **be sure to appoint the king the LORD your God chooses**.*

The monarchy is allowed and regulated rather than forbidden. The choosing belongs to God.

*He must be **one of your own people**; do not set a foreigner over you.*

## The first prohibition

*The king must not **build up great herds of horses** for himself, or **send the people back to
Egypt to get more horses**.*

Horses meant chariots, and chariots meant a standing army. Egypt was the horse market of the
region.

*For the LORD has told you, "**You are never to go back that way again**."*

The trade would run people the other direction — out of the land they were brought into.

## The second

*He must not **take many wives**, or **his heart will be led astray**.*

Royal marriages were treaties. Every foreign wife came with her gods and an alliance.

The law states the reason.

## The third

*And he must not **pile up silver and gold** for himself.*

Three prohibitions: military power, foreign alliances, accumulated wealth.

They are the three things every ancient king wanted, and the three things Solomon had in
quantities the record dwells on.

## What Solomon did

Forty thousand stalls of horses, imported from Egypt. Seven hundred wives and three hundred
concubines. Silver *as common in Jerusalem as stones*.

*But King Solomon loved many foreign women… **and his wives turned away his heart**.*

Three laws, three violations, in the order the law lists them. See the note on
1 Kings 10:23–25.

## The positive command

*And when he takes the throne, he is to **write out for himself, on a scroll, a copy of this
law**.*

The Hebrew is **mishneh hattorah hazzot** — a copy, a duplicate of this law.

The Septuagint rendered that phrase **deuteronomion** — second law — and the book got its English
name from a translator's reading of this verse.

## By hand

*For himself.*

Not commissioned from a scribe. The king writes it out.

## From the right copy

*From the one **the Levitical priests keep**.*

Checked against the authorised text. He cannot work from memory or from a version he likes
better.

## What he does with it

*Let it **stay beside him**, and let him **read it all the days of his life**.*

The Hebrew is **vehayetah immo** — and it shall be with him.

Not consulted at coronations. Kept next to him, and read for life.

## The two purposes

*So that he **learns to hold the LORD his God in awe** and to **follow carefully every word**.*

And then the one that names the danger:

*So that **his heart is not lifted above his brothers**.*

The Hebrew is **levilti rum-levavo me'echav** — that his heart not be lifted above his brothers.

## What the daily reading is for

To stop a king forgetting that the men in front of him are his brothers.

The law's own stated reason for a monarch reading Scripture every day is not wisdom or piety. It
is to prevent the elevation that the job produces automatically.

## The promise

*Then **he and his descendants will reign long** over his kingdom in Israel.*

## Who did it

*Then he brought out the king's son, put the crown on him, and **gave him the Testimony**.* — at
Joash's coronation.

And Josiah, when the book was found in the temple: *he tore his clothes*, and then read it aloud
to everyone.

> **The stated reason.** Not wisdom, and not piety. *So that his heart is not lifted above his
> brothers* — a daily reading prescribed against the specific hazard of the office.
