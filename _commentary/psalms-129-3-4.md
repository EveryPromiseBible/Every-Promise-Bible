One of the shorter Songs of Ascents, and one of its images is unusually brutal.

## The picture

*The plowmen plowed across my back and cut their long furrows there.*

A plough opening ground, used of a human back. The Hebrew for *furrows* is the
agricultural term — the parallel cuts a blade leaves in a field.

Israel is speaking as one person here, describing centuries of oppression from
Egypt onward: *many a time they have afflicted me from my youth, yet they have
not prevailed against me.*

## Why it is a harvest image

The psalm is one of the pilgrim songs, sung by farmers walking up to Jerusalem
for the festivals. A ploughed field was not a picture of ruin to them — it was
what you did before sowing.

That tension is left standing. A back opened like a field, in a psalm sung on the
way to a harvest feast.

## The turn

*But the LORD is just. He has cut me free from the ropes of the wicked.*

Two verbs in tension: they cut the back, He cuts the ropes. The same action word
answering itself.

*The LORD is just* is stated as the reason. Not that Israel endured well — that
God is righteous, and therefore the cords did not hold.

## Where readers have taken it

The scourging of Jesus is described in the Gospels in a single word and no
detail. Christian readers have long heard this verse behind it, and it sits
naturally beside Isaiah's servant whose back was given to those who struck him.

The psalm does not say that. It is Israel's own account of survival — and it is
worth noticing that the language it reaches for is the language of a body taking
what a field takes.

> **How the psalm ends.** With the wicked as grass on a roof — sprouting fast in
> shallow soil, withered before anyone can gather it. Set against a ploughed
> field, the contrast is between ground that gets worked and ground nothing grows
> in.
