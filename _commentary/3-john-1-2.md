A single verse from a letter of fourteen, written by an old man to a friend named
Gaius.

## What he prays

*More than anything I hope things go well for you and that your health holds up,
the way your soul is already doing well.*

Three things in one sentence: that it goes well with him generally, that his body
holds up, and that both would keep pace with a soul that is already thriving.

The Greek verb behind *go well* is **euodoo** — literally to have a good road, a
prosperous journey. It is used of travelling without obstruction.

## The measure John uses

*The way your soul is already doing well.* The soul is the benchmark, and it is
in good order — that much John states as fact rather than wish.

So the prayer is not for prosperity in the abstract. It is that the outward life
would come up level with the inward one, which John already knows is flourishing.

## Why he was confident about the soul

The next verse says why: travellers had come and reported that Gaius was walking
in the truth, and John says nothing gives him greater joy. Then the letter
commends him for how he treated strangers and missionaries passing through — at
his own cost.

Gaius was a generous man, and John prays for his health and his road in the same
breath as praising it.

> **What kind of sentence it is.** It sits where a first-century letter always put
> a wish for the reader's health — the standard opening courtesy of the era. John
> takes that ordinary form and fills it with something else: a prayer that the
> body and the circumstances would match the state of the soul. He evidently
> thought the three belonged together.
