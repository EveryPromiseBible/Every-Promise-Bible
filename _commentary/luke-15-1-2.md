Two verses of setup, and the whole of Luke 15 is the answer to them.

## Who came

*All the **tax collectors** and all the people with **ruined reputations** kept **crowding in
close** to Jesus **to hear Him**.*

The Greek is **telonai** — men who bought the right to collect Rome's taxes and kept the
surplus. Collaborators, and extortioners by design.

And **hamartoloi** — sinners, which in the mouths of the Pharisees meant people who did not
keep the purity laws: the trades that made ritual observance impossible, and anybody with a
public failure attached to their name.

## The verb

*Kept **crowding in close**.*

The Greek is **engizontes** — drawing near, and it is an imperfect. They kept doing it.

The word is the same root as the one for approach in the Levitical offering. See the note on
Leviticus 1:1–2.

## Why they came

*To hear Him.*

Luke says it plainly. Not for miracles.

## The reaction

*And the Pharisees and the teachers of the law **muttered about it among themselves**.*

The Greek is **diegonguzon** — grumbled through, murmured.

It is the verb the Greek Old Testament uses of Israel in the wilderness, complaining about
the manna.

## The charge

*"Look at Him. **This man welcomes sinners. He sits down and eats with them**."*

Two clauses, and the second is the offensive one.

Eating with somebody in that culture is not a neutral act. A shared table is a declaration of
acceptance — which is why the peace offering was a meal. See the note on
Leviticus 3:1–5.

## What the charge gets right

Everything. It is an accurate description and He never denies it.

He had already answered a version of it: *those who are well have no need of a physician, but
those who are sick. I have not come to call the righteous, but sinners, to repentance.*

## What follows

Three parables, told back to back, and all three are about something lost:

A shepherd with a hundred sheep who leaves ninety-nine. See the note on Luke 15:3–7.

A woman with ten coins who lights a lamp and sweeps the house.

And a man with two sons.

## The pattern in the first two

Something is lost, somebody searches, it is found, and the finder calls the neighbours
together: *rejoice with me*.

And each closes with the same line about heaven: *there will be more joy in heaven over one
sinner who repents than over ninety-nine just persons who need no repentance*.

## What the third one does differently

Nobody goes looking. The younger son comes to himself and starts walking, and the father runs.
See the note on Luke 15:17–20.

And it does not end with a party. It ends outside the house, with the older brother refusing
to come in and the father pleading. See the note on Luke 15:25–28.

Luke leaves the ending open.

## Who the third parable is aimed at

The men in verse 2.

The older brother has done everything right, has never left, and is standing outside a
celebration objecting that his father is welcoming somebody he should not.

That is the complaint in verse 2, put into a story and handed back.

## The father's last words

*"Son, **you are always with me, and all that I have is yours**. It was right that we should
make merry and be glad."*

Addressed, in the parable, to the men who were grumbling.

> **What produced the chapter.** A complaint that was factually correct. He welcomes sinners
> and eats with them — and the answer runs to thirty-two verses and three stories.
