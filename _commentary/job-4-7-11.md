The first friend to speak, and the ground he stands on is his own observation.

## Where his authority comes from

*"**What I have seen** is this: those who plough trouble and plant sorrow harvest
exactly that."*

That is his source. Not a word from God, not a Scripture — what he has watched
happen.

He says the same thing again a few verses later: *a word was secretly brought to
me… a spirit passed before my face, and the hair of my flesh stood up.* A vision
in the night, a voice he could not identify.

Experience is not the mother of wisdom. The Word of God is. A man who preaches
from what he has personally seen will build a rule out of a sample, and then
apply the rule to a man he does not understand.

## The question

*"**Name one honest person who was ever destroyed for nothing.** Where were the
upright ever cut down?"*

It sounds unanswerable. It is a rhetorical question with an obvious answer, put to
a man sitting in ashes.

And the answer is sitting in front of him. Job is the one honest person destroyed
for nothing, and Eliphaz cannot see it because his rule will not let him.

## What the rule does

It converts the suffering into evidence. Once you hold *the upright are never cut
down*, then a man who is cut down cannot be upright, and every day he stays in the
ashes is another day of proof.

That is the machine Job is now inside for twenty-five chapters, and all three
friends are feeding it.

## Sowing and reaping

*"Those who plough trouble and plant sorrow harvest exactly that."*

It is a true principle. Paul states it — *whatever a man sows, that he will also
reap.*

True, and applied to the wrong man, in the wrong situation, at the worst possible
hour. The problem with Eliphaz is not that what he says is false in general. It is
that it is false about Job, and he says it anyway.

## The lions

Four lines about lions with their teeth broken and their cubs scattered.

He is describing the fate of the wicked, in poetry, to a man whose ten children
were killed a fortnight ago.

## What God says about him at the end

*"My wrath is aroused against you and your two friends, for **you have not spoken
of Me what is right**, as My servant Job has."*

Said directly to Eliphaz, by name, in chapter 42. He is the first of the three to
speak and the one God addresses.

Whatever else the three of them got wrong, God's own summary of the charge is
that they misrepresented **Him**.

> **What the friends thought they were defending.** God's justice. They spent
> twenty-five chapters protecting His reputation, and the verdict at the end is
> that they had not said one right thing about Him. See the note on Job 42:7–8.
