The bride is asked a question and answers it at length.

## The question she is answering

*What is your beloved more than another beloved, O fairest among women? What is your beloved
more than another beloved, **that you so charge us**?*

The daughters of Jerusalem ask it because she has just made them swear: *if you find my
beloved, tell him I am lovesick*.

The description that follows is her reply, and it runs from his head to his legs.

## Where she is when she says it

She has lost him. The chapter opens with him at the door — *open for me, my sister, my love* —
and by the time she gets up he has gone.

*I sought him, but I could not find him; I called him, but he gave me no answer.*

And then the watchmen: *they struck me, they wounded me; the keepers of the walls took my veil
away from me*.

The most lyrical description of him in the book is spoken by a woman who has just been beaten
looking for him.

## The materials

*Arms are **rods of gold set with jewels of Tarshish**.*

**Tarshish** — a stone from the far west, probably beryl or topaz.

*His body is **polished ivory inlaid with sapphires**.*

*His legs are **pillars of marble set on bases of pure gold**.*

Gold, ivory, sapphire, marble. The list is temple architecture — these are the materials of
Solomon's building and of the tabernacle furniture.

## The stature

*His bearing is **like Lebanon**, noble as **the cedars**.*

Lebanon meant timber. The cedars were the finest building material in the ancient Near East,
and Solomon imported them for the temple.

The comparison is to a mountain range and a forest.

## The mouth

*His mouth is **all sweetness**.*

The Hebrew is **mamtaqqim** — sweetnesses, plural.

## The summary

*He is **altogether lovely**.*

The Hebrew is **kullo machamaddim** — all of him is desirable, wholly delights.

Ten features have been listed and she ends by refusing to itemise further.

## The two titles

*This is **my beloved**. This is **my friend**.*

**Dodi** and **re'i**.

The first is the lover's word she has used throughout. The second is the ordinary word for a
companion.

She puts them side by side in the last line, and the second one is the surprising one.

## What Isaiah says about the same figure

*He had **no beauty, no majesty to draw us to him, nothing in his appearance that we should
desire him**.* See the note on Isaiah 53:1–3.

The Hebrew there is **lo nechmedehu** — we did not desire him.

Same root as **machamaddim** here, and negated.

The two descriptions sit in the same Old Testament. One is what the bride says; the other is
what the passers-by said.

## The pattern in the book

He describes her at length, twice, in almost the same terms — *behold, you are fair, my love*.

She describes him once, and only when challenged to justify herself.

## What the Song is

Eight chapters of dialogue, no narrator, no plot, and no mention of God's name.

Read at Passover, and Rabbi Akiva's line about it: all the writings are holy, and this one is
the Holy of Holies.

## Where the last word goes

*Daughters of Jerusalem.*

She is answering a group who asked what was so special about him, and the answer is a
description of somebody built like a temple.

> **When she says it.** After losing him, searching the streets at night, and being beaten by
> the watchmen. Asked what makes him different, she lists gold, ivory and cedar.
