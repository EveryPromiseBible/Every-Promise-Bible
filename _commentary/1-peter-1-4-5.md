Three Greek adjectives, all built the same way, describing what cannot happen to
what is being kept for you.

## The three

*Nothing can rot it, nothing can dirty it, nothing can drain the colour out of
it.*

Each word in Greek begins with an *a-* prefix — the negative, like our *un-*:

- **aphthartos** — not perishable. It cannot decay from inside.
- **amiantos** — not defiled. Nothing can spoil it from outside.
- **amarantos** — not fading. It will not lose its colour with time.

Peter stacks three negatives because he is ruling out every way anything has ever
been lost: rot, contamination, and the slow bleaching of age.

The third word is where we get *amaranth*, the flower the ancients said never
withered.

## Where it is kept

*It is put away in heaven, waiting for you.* The verb is perfect passive —
already reserved, and standing reserved. Someone else did the putting away, and
it is still there.

## And who is kept

*God's power stands guard over you.*

That is the second half, and it is the one people miss. The inheritance is
guarded in heaven; the heir is guarded on earth. Peter uses a military word —
garrisoned, protected as a city is protected.

Both ends of the arrangement are held by someone other than the person inheriting.

> **Who he was writing to.** Scattered believers about to face real persecution —
> the letter goes on at length about suffering. This is what he opens with:
> everything you were born into is out of reach of anything that is about to
> happen to you.
