A run of contrasts, and one of them is a picture rather than a principle.

## The hunter who does not cook

*The lazy man never roasts what he caught, but the diligent hold their gain as
precious.*

The verse is difficult in Hebrew and translators have long divided over it. The
sense most take is the one here: he went out, he caught something, and then he
never got round to doing anything with it.

The failure is not in the hunt. It is in what happened after — the thing obtained
and not used, the catch left on the ground.

It is one of the few proverbs about laziness that concerns something already
acquired rather than something never attempted.

## The line worth stopping on

*Worry weighs a heart down low, but a kind word makes it glad.*

The Hebrew verb means to bow down or make stoop. Anxiety is described physically —
it bends a person over.

And the remedy offered is not advice or resolution. It is *a good word* from
somebody else. In a chapter full of proverbs about hard work and honest speech,
this one says that a heart bent by worry is straightened by a kindness spoken to
it.

> **The last line.** *In the way of righteousness there is life, and along that
> path there is no death.* It is the strongest thing said in the chapter, and it
> reads oddly among proverbs about lying lips and lazy hands — which is how
> Proverbs works. The small practical sayings keep being punctuated by something
> much larger, without transition.
