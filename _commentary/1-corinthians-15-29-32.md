Paul argues from his own risk: if there is no resurrection, his life makes no sense.

## Where this is

The middle of the resurrection chapter.

Some in Corinth were saying *there is no resurrection of the dead*, and Paul has spent twenty-eight
verses on the consequences: if Christ is not risen, the preaching is empty, the faith is futile,
and the dead in Christ have perished.

## The obscure verse

*Otherwise, what about those who are **baptized for the dead**, if the dead do not rise at all?*

The Greek is **hoi baptizomenoi huper ton nekron**.

## What it means

Nobody knows. There have been dozens of proposals since the second century.

Paul mentions the practice in the third person — *those who*, not *we* — and does not endorse or
condemn it. He uses it as an argument: whatever they think they are doing, it assumes a
resurrection.

## The turn to himself

*And **why do we stand in danger every hour**?*

The Greek is **kinduneuomen pasan horan** — we are in peril every hour.

## The oath

*I swear it by **the pride I take in you** in Christ Jesus our Lord: **I die daily**.*

The Greek is **kath' hemeran apothnesko**.

## What that means here

Not daily self-mortification.

The context is danger. He is saying his life is at risk every day, and swearing to it.

The catalogue is in 2 Corinthians: five times thirty-nine lashes, three beatings with rods, once
stoned, three shipwrecks, a night and a day in the deep. See the note on
2 Corinthians 11:24–28.

## The beasts

*If, **to put it the way people do**, I have fought with beasts at Ephesus.*

The Greek is **kata anthropon** — humanly speaking.

He flags the phrase as figurative. A Roman citizen could not be sent to the arena, and Acts
records a riot at Ephesus rather than a beast fight.

The opposition he faced there was human.

## The question

*What did I gain by it **if the dead are not raised**?*

The Greek is **ti moi to ophelos** — what is the profit to me?

An accountant's question, applied to a life.

## The conclusion

*"**Let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die**."*

Isaiah 22, quoted. The response of Jerusalem under siege when told to mourn: *joy and gladness,
slaying oxen and killing sheep… "let us eat and drink, for tomorrow we die."*

## What Paul is granting

If there is no resurrection, that is the correct conclusion.

He does not argue that unbelievers ought to live better anyway. He says the hedonist's logic is
sound on his own premises.

## What follows

*Do not be deceived: "**Evil company corrupts good habits**." Awake to righteousness, and do not sin.*

A quotation from the Greek playwright Menander, which Paul drops in without attribution.

## Where the chapter goes

*But someone will say, "**How are the dead raised up? And with what body do they come?**"*

The seed, the different kinds of flesh, the sun and moon and stars.

*It is sown in corruption, it is raised in incorruption.* See the note on
1 Corinthians 15:42–44.

*Behold, I tell you a mystery: **we shall not all sleep, but we shall all be changed**.* See the note
on 1 Corinthians 15:50–52.

## The last verse

*Therefore, my beloved brethren, be steadfast, immovable, always abounding in the work of the
Lord, **knowing that your labour is not in vain in the Lord**.*

The Greek is **ou kenos** — not empty.

The answer to *what did I gain by it*.

> **The argument.** He does not say the hedonist is wrong on his own terms — he grants it. *If the
> dead are not raised, let us eat and drink.* The whole case rests on whether the premise holds.
