Jesus has just told the strangest parable in Luke — a dishonest manager who
cooks the books and gets commended for shrewdness — and this is what He draws
out of it.

## The scale He puts money on

Read how He describes it:

- *a very small thing*
- *something as small as money*
- *what belongs to someone else*

Three times, and each one demotes it. Money is the small thing, the borrowed
thing, the test case — not the prize.

Set against it is *what is genuinely valuable* and *anything of your own*. The
comparison is not between poverty and wealth. It is between two orders of
property, and the one everybody worries about is the lesser.

## The strange logic of the first line

*Use the money of this world to make friends for yourselves, so that when you
finally fail, you will be welcomed into a home that lasts.*

The phrase for money is literally *unrighteous mammon* — wealth belonging to a
crooked world. Jesus does not tell them to avoid it; He tells them to spend it in
a direction that outlasts it. The manager in the parable used his master's money
to secure a future. Do that, He says, with better aim.

## The line that ends it

*You cannot serve God and money both.*

The verb is to serve as a slave, and the point is not that money is evil but that
it competes. Two owners cannot both have you. Everything before this — the
smallness, the borrowed quality, the shrewd spending — is preparing for a
sentence about who owns whom.

> **What "faithful in little" means here.** Not that careful budgeting earns
> spiritual promotion. The argument is about character being one thing: how a
> person handles the small borrowed thing is what they will do with the large
> permanent one, because it is the same person doing it.
