Two verses, and one of them contains the strangest image of God's attention in the Psalter.

## Where it was written

The heading: *when the Philistines captured him in Gath*.

David, on the run from Saul, in the home town of the giant he had killed — carrying, according
to 1 Samuel, Goliath's own sword.

He got out by pretending to be insane, scrabbling at the gates and letting saliva run into his
beard, and Achish said *have I need of madmen?*

That is where this psalm comes from.

## The counting

*You have **counted my every wandering**, each **toss and turn in the dark**.*

The Hebrew is **nodi** — my wanderings, my homelessness. And the verb is **saphar**, to count,
to number, to recount.

The old versions have *You tell my wanderings* — tell in the sense of a bank teller: keeping
count.

Every displacement, tallied.

## The bottle

*Gather **my tears in Your bottle**.*

The Hebrew is **no'd** — a skin bottle, the kind used for water or milk. The same word as the
skin Abraham gave Hagar. See the note on Genesis 21:14–16.

And it sits one letter away from **nodi**, wanderings, in the line before. The Hebrew is doing
something with the sound: the wanderings counted, the tears in a skin.

## Whether the custom existed

Roman tear bottles — small glass vials found in graves — are often cited. Whether Israelites
kept them is not established.

The image works either way. A man asks for his crying to be collected in a container, which
is what you do with something of value that would otherwise be lost.

## The book

*Are they not **written in Your book**?*

The Hebrew is **sipratekha** — Your book, from the same root as the counting verb in the
previous line.

Counted, bottled, and written down. Three forms of record for the same thing.

## The turn

*On the day I call to You, **my enemies will turn and run**.*

Future tense, from a man currently in enemy territory.

## The clause

*This much I know: **God is for me**.*

The Hebrew is *this I know, for God is for me* — **ki Elohim li**.

Four words, and it is the psalm's centre.

Paul writes the same sentence in Greek: *if God is for us, who can be against us?* See the
note on Romans 8:31–34.

## The refrain

The psalm has it twice: *in God I have put my trust; **I will not fear. What can flesh do to
me?***

And *in God (I will praise His word), in the LORD (I will praise His word), in God I have put
my trust; I will not be afraid. What can man do to me?*

He says he will not be afraid, in a psalm whose heading says he was caught.

## The vow

*Vows made to You are binding upon me, O God; I will render praises to You.*

## The last verse

*For You have **delivered my soul from death**. Have You not kept my feet from falling, that
I may walk before God in the light of the living?*

Past tense. The psalm that begins *be merciful to me, O God, for man would swallow me up*
ends with the rescue reported as done.

## Where the tears go in the end

*And God will **wipe away every tear from their eyes**.*

The bottle is emptied by a hand.

> **What is asked for.** Not that the crying stop. That it be collected — counted, put in a
> skin, and written in a book, which are three ways of saying *do not let this go unrecorded*.
