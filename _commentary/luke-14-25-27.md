The crowd is at its largest, and He turns round and makes it harder.

## The setting

*Great crowds were **traveling along with Him** now, and **He turned around to face them**.*

The Greek is **suneporeuonto** — journeying together with Him.

He is on the road to Jerusalem. The numbers are up, and He stops and faces the direction they
are coming from.

## What He does with a crowd

The same thing He does in John 6, where the largest gathering He ever had thins out to twelve
after a hard saying. See the note on John 6:66–71.

He never treats size as the thing to protect.

## The first condition

*"If anyone comes to Me and **does not hold Me dearer than his own father and mother**, dearer
than his wife and his children, dearer than his brothers and sisters — **dearer even than his own
life** — he cannot be My disciple."*

The Greek verb is **misei**, literally *hate* — a Semitic idiom of comparative preference, the
same construction as *Jacob have I loved, Esau have I hated*, and the same one used of Leah
being *hated* when the text has already said Jacob loved Rachel more.

It is a ranking, not an emotion. The Illumination reads it as **dearer**, which is what the
idiom means.

## Why the list runs that way

Father and mother, wife and children, brothers and sisters — every tie a man in that culture
had, in descending order of obligation.

And then *his own life*, which is not a relationship at all. The list runs past family and ends
at the self.

## The second condition

*"Whoever will not **pick up his own cross and walk along behind Me** cannot be My disciple."*

**His own** cross. Not the one Jesus carried.

## What the crowd heard

They had seen it. Crucifixion was a Roman public work, and a man carrying a crossbeam through
the streets was a man already condemned, walking to a place he would not walk back from.

Nobody in that crowd took the image as a metaphor for inconvenience.

## What follows

Two parables, both about counting: a man building a tower who works out the cost first, and a
king with ten thousand men considering another with twenty thousand.

*Which of you, intending to build a tower, **does not sit down first and count the cost**?*

He is telling a growing crowd to do the arithmetic before joining.

## Where this sits in the chapter

Two paragraphs earlier, a dinner party — the seating, the guest list, and the great supper where
the invitations go out to the streets and lanes. See the note on Luke 14:21–24.

The invitation in that parable is unconditional and free, and the servant is sent out twice to
bring people in.

And then this, on the road, to the people who came.

## What the chapter is doing with the two

Luke sets the free invitation and the cost of the road next to each other on the same page.

The supper is entered by being brought in. The journey is walked by counting what it takes.

## What Jesus says about His own

*I lay down My life… **no one takes it from Me, but I lay it down of Myself**.* See the note on
John 10:16–18.

He carried His to a place outside the city, and the cross He asks for here is *his own*, which
is a different beam.

## The closing image

*"Salt is good; but if the salt has lost its flavour, how shall it be seasoned?"*

The chapter ends with something that has stopped being what it is.

> **The verb.** *Hate* is a Semitic idiom of ranking, the same one that makes Leah *hated* in a
> sentence where Jacob simply loved Rachel more. It is an order of preference, and the list runs
> past family to end at the self.
