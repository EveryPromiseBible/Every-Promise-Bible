Three commands, three promises, and then an argument from the worst father in the room.

## The three

*"**Ask**, and it will be given to you. **Seek**, and you will find. **Knock**, and the door
will be opened to you."*

The Greek verbs are present imperatives — continuous. Keep asking, keep seeking, keep
knocking.

Each is more active than the last: asking is words, seeking is movement, knocking is
arriving at a specific door.

## The repetition

*"**Everyone** who asks receives, and the one who seeks finds, and to the one who knocks
the door is opened."*

He says all three again, as statements of fact rather than instructions.

**Everyone** — the Greek is **pas**. No category is excluded.

## The two questions

*"Which of you, if your son asked you for **a loaf of bread**, would hand him **a stone**?
Or if he asked for **a fish**, would hand him **a snake**?"*

Both pairs are things that look alike. A flat stone from the Galilee shore looks like a
loaf; an eel-like fish and a water snake are hard to tell apart in a net.

The absurdity is not a father giving something useless. It is a father giving something
that looks right and is not.

## The premise

*"So **if you, who have plenty of wrong in you**, know how to give good gifts to your
children."*

The Greek is *if you, being evil* — **poneroi**.

He says it to the crowd on the hillside without softening it, and then uses their
fatherhood as the good half of the comparison.

## The conclusion

*"**How much more** will your Father in heaven **give good things to the ones who ask
Him**."*

The Greek is **posso mallon** — the standard rabbinic form of argument from lesser to
greater.

The gap between a bad human father and a good one is small. The gap between any human
father and this one is the size of the argument.

## What Luke has instead

*"How much more will your heavenly Father **give the Holy Spirit** to those who ask
Him!"*

Matthew says *good things*; Luke names one.

## Where it sits

Near the end of the Sermon on the Mount, after *do not judge* and immediately before the
golden rule: *therefore, whatever you want men to do to you, do also to them, for this is
the Law and the Prophets.*

The **therefore** connects them. Because your Father gives like that, treat people like
that.

## What has already been said about asking

Two chapters earlier, in the same sermon: *your Father knows the things you have need of
before you ask Him.*

And: *do not worry, saying, "What shall we eat?"… **your heavenly Father knows that you
need all these things**.* See the note on Matthew 6:25–34.

## What the passage does not say

Nothing about how much faith, how long, or how well. The conditions in the sentence are
asking, seeking and knocking.

> **The argument's weakest link is deliberate.** He builds the case on a father He has
> just called evil, and says the conclusion holds *how much more* at the other end.
