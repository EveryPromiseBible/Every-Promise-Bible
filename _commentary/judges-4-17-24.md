The commander of nine hundred iron chariots is killed with two objects that
belong to housework.

## Who Sisera was

The chapter opens with him: twenty years of harsh oppression, nine hundred
chariots of iron, an army Israel had no answer to.

He ends the day on foot, in a woman's tent, asking for a drink.

## What she used

*Jael took up a **tent peg** and a **hammer**.*

Pitching and striking the tents was women's work among the nomads. The peg and
the mallet were the tools of her trade, in her hand every time the camp moved.

She does not go looking for a weapon. She kills him with what she already had.

## The milk

*He asked for a little water, and she opened a skin of milk and gave him a
drink.*

He asked for less than she gave him. The milk is what puts him into the
*exhausted sleep* the next sentence describes.

Judges 5, Deborah's song, dwells on this: *he asked for water, she gave him milk;
she brought him curds in a lordly bowl.*

## "At the hand of a woman"

*So the great commander died at the hand of a woman.*

The narrator says it as a verdict, and it had been announced ten verses earlier.
Deborah had told Barak that because of the way he was going about it, *the LORD
will sell Sisera into the hand of a woman.*

Barak arrives after it is over. *Come, and I'll show you the man you're looking
for.*

## Why the story is told this way

Iron chariots against a hammer and a tent peg. The book of Judges keeps doing
this — an ox goad, a jawbone, three hundred men with jars and torches, a
millstone dropped from a wall.

It is the same principle Paul states outright: *God chose the weak things of the
world to shame the strong.* See the note on 1 Corinthians 1:26–31.

The small ordinary thing is not a lesser weapon. It is the one God keeps
choosing.

> **How the chapter closes.** *Israel's hand pressed harder and harder against
> Jabin, until at last they destroyed him.* One peg in one tent, and then twenty
> years of oppression unwinding.
